A Malaysian solar quote is decided before the design starts. Domestic NEM 3.0 installations are capped at 12 kWp, and non-domestic installations at 75% of Maximum Demand taken from the TNB bill. Above that, nothing is permissible regardless of roof area. The scheme is also quota-based through SEDA in periodic finite rounds, so approval is not automatic, and without it TNB will not fit the bi-directional meter. Then the whole process takes 4 to 6 months from application to first billing. A proposal that ignores any of those three facts is a proposal that will be revised. ## Quote The Cap, Not The Roof

> **What most Malaysian commercial quotes get wrong.** They present a system sized to available roof, then discover at SEDA application that 75% of Maximum Demand permits far less. The revision costs the survey, the customer's confidence, and a place in a quota round that may not reopen for months.

The sequence that works is unglamorous and reliable. Take the Maximum Demand from the TNB bill. Multiply by 0.75. That is the ceiling. Check whether roof area supports it. Only then design.

Because NEM 3.0 offsets exports against imports at the same tariff, the payback ratio does not change with system size. A 300 kWp system and a 500 kWp system on the same tariff have the same payback period. There is therefore no economic argument for quoting above the cap and no economic loss in quoting at it. The only thing the oversized design achieves is a rejected application.

That 1:1 equivalence has a second consequence worth explaining to customers who have read about solar elsewhere. In markets with low export rates, installers push batteries and load shifting hard because self-consumption is worth several times export. Under NEM 3.0 that gap does not exist, so the same recommendations carry much less weight. Being straight about this builds credibility, and it steers the conversation toward the things that actually matter here, which are the cap, the quota and the timeline.

## Timeline And Quota Belong In The Document

The 4 to 6 month window from SEDA application to first billing is the most under-communicated fact in Malaysian solar sales. Customers routinely assume that installation means generation and export. It does not. Between the two sits quota approval, TNB's bi-directional meter, and the connection process.

A proposal should state the expected timeline explicitly and identify the quota round it depends on. Where quota availability is uncertain, the honest structure is to show two cases: the export case against confirmed quota, and the self-consumption-only fallback if quota is not secured. The second case is a genuinely different investment, and presenting it as a risk rather than concealing it is what separates a professional quote from an optimistic one.

Regional variation matters here too. Sarawak operates independently under Sarawak Energy Berhad with separate portals and potentially different caps and rates. Sabah and Labuan use SESB's distinct scheme. A template built on Peninsular Malaysia and TNB will be wrong in East Malaysia, and the customer will not be the one who discovers it first.

## GITA And The Commercial Case

GITA gives a 100% investment tax allowance over three years, subject to MIDA approval. On a commercial project that is a substantial accelerator, but it is an allowance against statutory income rather than a cash grant, so its value depends on the customer's tax position and their ability to absorb it. Modelling it at a generic rate overstates the benefit for a company with limited taxable income and understates it for a profitable one.

The MIDA approval dependency also belongs in the document. An allowance contingent on an approval that has not yet been granted is not the same as one already secured, and a proposal that presents it as certain is making a promise it does not control.

GITE, the 70% income tax exemption over ten years, applies to solar service providers rather than to the end customer, so it should not appear in a customer-facing proposal at all.

## Worked Example: 300 kWp Factory, With And Without Quota

An illustrative calculation, not a specific customer project. Inputs: 400 kW Maximum Demand giving a 300 kWp ceiling, 1,450 kWh per kWp specific yield, 435,000 kWh generation, 65% consumed on site during production hours, tariff RM 0.50 per kWh, installed cost RM 660,000.

| Line | With NEM 3.0 quota | Self-consumption only |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Generation | 435,000 kWh | 435,000 kWh |
| Self-consumed | 282,750 kWh | 282,750 kWh |
| Exported | 152,250 kWh | 0, curtailed |
| Value of self-consumption at RM 0.50 | RM 141,375 | RM 141,375 |
| Value of export at RM 0.50 offset | RM 76,125 | RM 0 |
| Year 1 benefit | RM 217,500 | RM 141,375 |
| Simple payback | 3.0 years | 4.7 years |

Failing to secure quota costs RM 76,125 a year and pushes payback out by more than a year and a half. The project still works, which is the useful point: a factory with strong daytime load has a defensible case even without export. A warehouse with low daytime consumption does not, and for that customer the quota is the whole investment case. Showing both columns tells the customer which of those they are.
