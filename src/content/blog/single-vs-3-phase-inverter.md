> [!KEYTAKEAWAYS]
>
> - Phase choice follows your sanctioned connection, not your rooftop capacity.
> - A three-phase inverter cannot work on a single-phase service. There are no phases to synchronise with.
> - A single-phase inverter on a three-phase connection is possible, but phase-wise netting may cost you export credit.
> - Capacity thresholds that force three phase are set by DISCOM, vary by state, and change by order.
> - Phase count does not by itself make an inverter more efficient.
> - Settle the connection question before the equipment question.

**Short version.** Read your sanctioned connection from the bill and the main breaker, confirm your DISCOM's current capacity threshold and netting method, then match the inverter to that. Choosing phase from panel kW alone is the common and expensive mistake.

## What phase actually means here

A single-phase supply delivers power on one live conductor and a neutral. A three-phase supply delivers it on three live conductors carrying alternating voltages offset from one another, usually with a neutral.

For solar the practical consequence is synchronisation. A grid-tied inverter matches its output to the incoming supply. A three-phase inverter has three output legs and needs three incoming phases to lock onto. Present it with a single-phase service and there is simply nothing for two of its legs to connect to.

The reverse direction is physically possible. A single-phase inverter can be connected to one phase of a three-phase service. Whether that is a good idea depends on how your DISCOM settles net metering, which is the part most quotes leave out.

## How to check what you have

Do this before you discuss equipment with anyone.

<!-- steps -->
1. **Read the bill.** It normally states the sanctioned load and whether the connection is single or three phase. Sanctioned load is the number that matters for DISCOM thresholds, not your typical consumption.
2. **Look at the main incoming breaker.** One wide breaker fed by a single live conductor plus neutral indicates single phase. Three mechanically linked poles fed by three live conductors indicates three phase.
3. **Count the meter terminals** if accessible, or note the meter model. Three-phase meters are visibly larger and have more terminals.
4. **Confirm with the DISCOM or your installer.** Treat steps one to three as indicative. The sanctioned connection on record is what governs the application.

If you are planning to add significant load such as multiple air conditioners, a lift, a workshop or an EV charger, raise it now. A connection upgrade decided later can mean revisiting the inverter choice.

## The four-question phase test

Answer capsule: four questions, in order, settle the phase before you talk to an installer. Each one removes a category of inverter.

1. **What is your sanctioned connection?** Single-phase connections eliminate three-phase inverters. Three-phase connections allow either, subject to the netting rules below. If you are unsure, read the bill and the main breaker, as set out above.
2. **What do your loads need?** A household of lights, fans, a refrigerator and one or two air conditioners runs on a single-phase service. Three-phase motors, central air conditioning and lifts need three-phase supply whatever inverter you choose.
3. **How big is the system against your DISCOM's limit?** The state limits table below shows how differently thresholds are set. A system larger than the single-phase limit needs a three-phase connection.
4. **Will the load grow?** If you expect a workshop, a lift or an EV charger within a few years, decide the connection first. A later upgrade can reopen the inverter choice.

## Worked example: AC current by phase and system size

Answer capsule: the same power needs about three times the current on one phase as it does spread across three. That is one reason single-phase limits exist, and the arithmetic is simple enough to check yourself.

> [!FORMULA] Rated AC current
> Single-phase at 230 V and unity power factor: current = power ÷ 230 V.
>
> Three-phase at 415 V line-to-line: current per phase = power ÷ (1.732 × 415 V).

These are rated-power figures, before any overload margin, and an actual datasheet lists the maximum output current of the model.

Two things follow. A 10 kW single-phase inverter would export about 43.5 A on one conductor, which is above the 40 A single-phase supply limit in the MERC Supply Code and well above the current a typical home service is built for. And a 5 kW single-phase unit at about 21.7 A is a comfortable load for a 40 A service but is a larger share of a smaller sanctioned connection. This is why inverter datasheets state maximum AC output current, and why the AC cable and breaker rating must match it.

Voltage rise follows current. The more current you push into one phase of a shared low-voltage feeder, the more that phase's voltage rises at the point of connection. Many inverters trip on overvoltage when it rises too far, as the guide to [grid overvoltage tripping](/blog/solar-inverter-grid-overvoltage/) explains. Spreading export across three phases reduces the rise on each.

## Phase-wise netting, the expensive detail

This is the point that most often surprises owners of three-phase homes after commissioning.

Some DISCOMs settle net metering on the total across all three phases. Others settle it phase by phase. Where settlement is phase-wise, a single-phase inverter connected to one phase exports only on that phase, while your loads on the other two phases continue to import.

The result is that you can be billed for imports on two phases while the export credit on the third is settled separately, sometimes at a less favourable rate or capped. The meter is behaving correctly. The design simply did not match the settlement method.

> [!EXAMPLE] Phase-wise versus aggregate settlement
> A simple example shows the gap. A three-phase home has a single-phase inverter on the first phase producing 3 kW at noon. Each phase carries 1 kW of load. On an aggregate settlement the 3 kW of generation offsets the 3 kW of total load, so the net is zero. On a phase-wise settlement the first phase exports 2 kW while the other two phases import 1 kW each, so you export 2 kW and import 2 kW, and the two are credited and billed separately. Whether the export credit equals the import charge depends on your DISCOM's rules, which is why the written answer matters.

Where a DISCOM nets phase-wise, matching a three-phase inverter to a three-phase connection removes the issue because generation is distributed across all three phases.

> [!IMPORTANT] Two questions to get answered in writing
> Ask two specific questions before signing: does my DISCOM settle net metering per phase or on the aggregate, and if per phase, how will this design be credited? Get the answer in writing. The [net metering application guide](/blog/how-to-apply-net-metering-india/) covers what to confirm during the application.

## When three phase is required rather than preferred

Several DISCOMs set a rooftop capacity or sanctioned load threshold above which a three-phase connection becomes mandatory. The threshold is not uniform across India and it changes by order, so no single figure applies. The table that follows shows published examples from five states.

What is consistent is the process. The DISCOM assesses technical feasibility against your sanctioned connection, and a design that exceeds the permitted single-phase capacity will not be approved as submitted. Discovering that at the feasibility stage costs you weeks. Discovering it after equipment is delivered costs considerably more.

Beyond the regulatory threshold, three phase is usually the practical choice where the building already runs three-phase equipment, where loads would otherwise be badly unbalanced, or where future expansion is likely.

## What goes wrong when the phase is wrong

The cost of a mismatch is rarely just the inverter. It usually lands in three places.

**The application stalls.** DISCOM technical feasibility assesses the proposed design against your sanctioned connection. A design that exceeds the permitted single-phase capacity, or proposes a three-phase inverter on a single-phase service, comes back for revision. That is weeks, not days, and it happens before any equipment is useful.

**The equipment has to change.** An inverter bought for the wrong connection type cannot be reconfigured. A single-phase unit does not become three-phase through a settings change or a firmware update. Whoever bought it absorbs the loss, and who that is depends on how the contract allocated responsibility for the connection check.

**The billing disappoints quietly.** This is the one that surfaces months later. The system generates as designed, the meter records as designed, and the credit is smaller than the customer expected because settlement is phase-wise and nobody raised it. There is no fault to repair, which makes it harder to resolve than a broken component.

On a three-phase service there is a further design consideration even when the inverter is correctly matched. Household loads are rarely spread evenly across phases. Where the imbalance is significant, discuss it with the installer during design, because balancing loads across phases is cheaper at the distribution board stage than after commissioning.

## When three phase is mandatory: industrial and commercial loads

Answer capsule: the inverter does not make a premises three-phase. The loads and the sanctioned connection do. Where any of these apply, three-phase supply is a practical need regardless of the solar system.

- **Variable-frequency drives and industrial pumps.** Three-phase motors need three-phase supply, and drives add harmonic content that the supply must tolerate.
- **Central air conditioning.** Chillers and large packaged units are three-phase machines.
- **Commercial and industrial net metering.** Commercial consumers usually hold three-phase connections, and a single-phase inverter on one leg creates unbalance that DISCOMs restrict.
- **Harmonics and balance.** Large single-phase loads on one leg of a three-phase supply raise neutral current and voltage unbalance. See the [harmonics glossary entry](/glossary/harmonics/) and the [three-phase inverter guide](/blog/3-phase-solar-inverter/).

## Can you upgrade from single-phase to three-phase?

Answer capsule: usually yes, through the DISCOM, but it is an application with cost, inspection and possibly a meter change. Settle it before buying equipment, not after.

The usual steps are a load or connection-change application to the DISCOM, a feasibility and site check, wiring changes inside the premises, a new meter if required, and then a revised solar design if the system is larger than before. DISCOMs set their own charges and timelines. Where you expect heavy new equipment, upgrade the connection first, then size the inverter. The [net metering application guide](/blog/how-to-apply-net-metering-india/) covers the paperwork, and the [DISCOM approval guide](/blog/discom-approval-solar-inverter-net-metering/) explains the inspection stage.

## Power factor, load balancing and neutral current

Answer capsule: three terms appear in phase discussions. Each describes a real effect, and none needs a special inverter in an ordinary home.

**Power factor.** It is the ratio of real power to apparent power. Grid-tied inverters typically run near unity and offer an adjustable range, which is a datasheet line to read. See the [power factor glossary entry](/glossary/power-factor/).

**Load balancing.** On a three-phase service, household loads are rarely evenly spread across phases. Where the imbalance is large, ask the installer to balance loads at the distribution board, which costs less than fixing it after commissioning.

**Neutral current.** In a balanced three-phase system the neutral carries little current. Imbalance, and harmonic currents from electronic loads, raise it. Large single-phase injection on one leg changes the balance and can push voltage up on that phase.

## Common mistakes when choosing between single-phase and three-phase

1. **Choosing phase from panel kW alone.** The sanctioned connection and DISCOM rule decide.
2. **Buying a three-phase inverter for a single-phase service.** It has nothing to synchronise with.
3. **Putting a large single-phase inverter on one leg of a three-phase supply without asking about netting.** Phase-wise settlement can cost export credit.
4. **Assuming a state's limit applies elsewhere.** The state limits table shows different ways of setting thresholds.
5. **Ignoring future load.** A later connection upgrade can reopen the design.
6. **Leaving the connection check unassigned in the contract.** Decide in writing who confirms the sanctioned connection and handles the DISCOM application.
