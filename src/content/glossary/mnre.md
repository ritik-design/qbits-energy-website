## What is MNRE

The Ministry of New and Renewable Energy (MNRE) is the central government ministry responsible for renewable energy in India. Headquartered in New Delhi, MNRE designs national policy, runs subsidy and tender programmes, sets equipment specifications, funds R&D and coordinates with states, regulators and the private sector across the renewable energy value chain.

> **TL;DR**
> - MNRE (Ministry of New and Renewable Energy) is India's central renewable energy ministry, tracing back to 1982 and renamed to its current form in 2006.
> - It runs PM Surya Ghar, PM-KUSUM, the solar PLI manufacturing scheme, and controls ALMM equipment listing for subsidised projects.
> - MNRE sets policy and disburses subsidy; it does not set tariffs (CERC/SERC) or write technical grid standards (CEA).
> - SECI, IREDA and NISE are the main implementing institutions that report to MNRE.
> - India's target is 500 GW of non-fossil capacity by 2030, with roughly 280-300 GW expected to come from solar.

MNRE evolved from the Department of Non-Conventional Energy Sources (1982), which became the Ministry of Non-Conventional Energy Sources in 1992, and the Ministry of New and Renewable Energy in 2006. India's renewable capacity growth from 18 GW in 2010 to over 200 GW by 2025 ran almost entirely through MNRE schemes and policies.

For Indian solar specifically, MNRE controls [ALMM](/glossary/almm-list/) listing, [PM Surya Ghar](/glossary/pm-surya-ghar/), [PM-KUSUM](/glossary/kusum/), rooftop solar guidelines, off-grid solar applications, the solar PLI scheme and the National Green Hydrogen Mission. Anyone designing, financing or building a solar project in India interacts with MNRE rules at some point — our [MNRE Rooftop Solar Scheme guide](/blog/mnre-rooftop-solar-scheme/) walks through the residential programme in detail.

## MNRE explained simply

If you want to know who decides national solar policy, where the subsidies come from, what equipment is approved for subsidised projects, and how big India's solar build-out gets, the answer is MNRE. It is the central ministry that writes the rules, signs the cheques and runs the targets for renewables across the country.

## Why MNRE matters

National policy. MNRE drafts the renewable strategy that flows into state implementation.

Subsidy administration. Central subsidies for PM Surya Ghar, PM-KUSUM and prior schemes flow through MNRE.

Equipment standards. ALMM and MNRE technical specifications gate which products can be used in subsidised projects.

Target setting. India's 500 GW non-fossil 2030 target was articulated through MNRE at COP26.

Industry coordination. SECI, IREDA, NISE and renewable PSUs all sit under the MNRE umbrella.

International engagement. International Solar Alliance, IRENA, ISA initiatives and bilateral renewable agreements run through MNRE.

## How MNRE works

1. Cabinet approves scheme budgets and policy direction.
2. MNRE issues scheme guidelines and operational orders.
3. Implementing agencies (SECI, IREDA, state nodal agencies) run programmes on the ground.
4. DISCOMs and state agencies handle commissioning, metering and disbursement.
5. MNRE tracks progress through monthly state-wise dashboards.

## Institutions under MNRE

- Solar Energy Corporation of India (SECI): central tender and PPA operator
- Indian Renewable Energy Development Agency (IREDA): finance for renewable projects
- National Institute of Solar Energy (NISE): R&D and testing
- National Institute of Wind Energy (NIWE): wind R&D
- Sardar Swaran Singh National Institute of Bio-Energy (SSS-NIBE)

## Real-world impact

Setting ALMM compliance as the precondition for subsidy.

Coordinating PM Surya Ghar across all 28 states.

Issuing guidelines that [DISCOMs](/glossary/discom/) use for [net metering](/glossary/net-metering/) operationalisation.

Funding rural and off-grid renewable applications.

Driving the solar manufacturing PLI that catalysed gigawatt-scale Indian module factories.

## Advantages of MNRE-led approach

Central coordination across diverse states.

Steady policy direction since 2006.

Large-scale subsidy disbursement mechanism.

International alignment with global renewable targets.

Integrated approach across solar, wind, hydrogen and biomass.

## Limitations

Implementation depends on state agency capacity.

Subsidy disbursement timelines often slip.

Equipment standards lag international best practice.

Coordination with [CEA](/glossary/cea/) and [CERC](/glossary/cerc/) has historical friction.

Scheme design changes between phases can disrupt market expectations.

## Common problems

ALMM listing delays for new manufacturer models, blocking project execution.

Subsidy disbursement waiting on DISCOM commissioning data uploads.

Empanelled vendor list gaps in remote districts.

State-level interpretation differences for MNRE guidelines.

Scheme transition periods leave projects in limbo.

## Best practices for working with MNRE

Track MNRE notifications through the official website and PIB releases.

Plan project timelines with realistic buffer for ALMM updates.

Maintain documentation per MNRE scheme guidelines for each project.

Coordinate with state nodal agencies, not directly with MNRE for individual project queries.

Use the MNRE grievance portal for delays beyond stated timelines.

Validate ALMM listing on the day of equipment supply.

## Comparison: MNRE vs CEA vs CERC

| Body | Role | Reports to |
|---|---|---|
| MNRE | Renewable policy and programmes | Cabinet, MNRE Cabinet Minister |
| CEA | Technical grid standards | Ministry of Power |
| CERC | Central tariff regulation | Statutory, autonomous |
| SECI | Central tendering for renewables | MNRE |
| IREDA | Renewable project finance | MNRE |

## Indian market context

MNRE's 500 GW non-fossil capacity target by 2030 underwrites the entire current renewable build-out.

The PLI scheme for solar PV manufacturing is being implemented in phases, with Tranche I and II tendered.

The [National Green Hydrogen Mission](/glossary/green-hydrogen/) seeks to position India as a global green hydrogen exporter.

Coordination with the Ministry of Power and the Ministry of Heavy Industries on grid integration and EV alignment is increasingly important.

State-level renewable policies generally follow MNRE direction, with state-specific additions.

## Common mistakes

Engaging with MNRE directly on project-level queries. Use state nodal agencies and DISCOMs first.

Assuming national uniformity. Each state interprets MNRE guidelines with local nuance.

Skipping the ALMM check before equipment supply.

Treating MNRE schemes as static. Phase transitions change rules.

Ignoring SECI, IREDA and NISE roles. Each owns part of the operational chain.

## Key takeaways

MNRE is the central nervous system of Indian renewable energy. It writes the policy, runs the programmes, sets equipment standards and tracks the 500 GW target. Anyone touching subsidised, government-tendered or grid-connected renewable projects in India operates within the MNRE framework. Implementation runs through DISCOMs, state agencies and central PSUs.
