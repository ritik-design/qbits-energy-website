Most Scanifly reviews get the framing wrong before they reach the first feature. They line it up next to Aurora, OpenSolar, and HelioScope, score it on proposal templates and financial modelling, and conclude it is a narrow product. That comparison is unfair, because Scanifly is not trying to be a design and proposal platform. It is drone photogrammetry and site survey software, and it competes with the truck roll, the tape measure, and the change order rather than with the design tool you already own. This review assesses it on that basis: what it does, how well it does it, what it costs you operationally, and the specific conditions under which the drone workflow earns its keep.

## What Scanifly Actually Is

Scanifly is a site survey product. The workflow starts on site, not at a desk.

A pilot flies a structured capture pattern over the building using a consumer or prosumer drone, collecting dozens to hundreds of overlapping geotagged photographs. Those images upload to Scanifly, which runs photogrammetry over them. Photogrammetry is the technique of reconstructing 3D geometry from overlapping 2D images by solving for the camera positions and triangulating shared points. The output is a photorealistic, to-scale 3D model of the roof, its obstructions, the surrounding trees, and the ground around the building.

From there the model becomes a working surface. You measure planes, pitches, setbacks, and obstruction clearances against the real building rather than against an interpretation of aerial imagery. You can lay out modules on it, produce plan-set documentation, and hand engineering an artefact that reflects the site as it stands today.

The important distinction is the word "as-built". A satellite-derived model is an inference from imagery that may be a year or more old. A Scanifly model is a measurement of the building on the day someone flew it. That difference does not matter on a clean two-plane roof. It matters a great deal on a building that has been modified, on a roof with dense mechanical equipment, or on a site where the trees have grown since the last imagery refresh.

## Why It Is Not a Direct Competitor to Aurora or SurgePV

This is the single most important thing a buyer needs to understand, so it belongs early rather than buried in a comparison table.

Aurora Solar and SurgePV are design and proposal platforms. You give them an address, they construct a roof model from remote data, and they carry you through layout, shading, production simulation, financial modelling, and a customer-facing document. The site model is an input to a much longer chain. Our [Aurora Solar review](/blog/aurora-solar-review/) covers that category in detail, as does the [HelioScope review](/blog/helioscope-review/) for the engineering-first end of it.

Scanifly owns one link in that chain, and owns it differently. It replaces the site model input with a measured one. Everything downstream of the model still has to happen somewhere.

So the real question is not "Scanifly or Aurora". It is "does my site model need to come from a drone, and if so, what am I designing on top of it". Teams that get this right typically run two tools with clear roles. Teams that get it wrong buy Scanifly expecting a proposal engine, or buy a design platform expecting survey-grade as-built capture, and then blame the product for a scoping mistake they made themselves.

> **What most buyers get wrong.** They evaluate Scanifly against a design platform's feature list and conclude it is missing things. It is missing them on purpose. The correct comparison is Scanifly against your current site survey process: the truck roll, the ladder, the tape measure, and the revisions you issue when the measurements were wrong.

## What Scanifly Genuinely Does Well

These are real strengths. This is a good product inside its niche, and a review that failed to say so would be dishonest.

**As-built accuracy from real capture.** Scanifly states an accuracy of 2 to 3 inches across the X, Y, and Z planes for models built from proper drone flights, according to Scanifly's own documentation. Treat that as a vendor claim and test it, but the underlying method is sound. Photogrammetry from a disciplined flight with good overlap is a measurement, not an estimate. When the design has to fit hardware into a tight setback, the difference between measured and estimated geometry is the difference between an install and a revision.

**Complex and heavily obstructed roofs.** This is where the product earns its reputation. Multi-plane roofs, dormers, stepped commercial rooftops, parapets, dense HVAC equipment, skylights, vents, and mature tree canopies are exactly the conditions under which remote imagery degrades. A drone flown around and over the structure sees the geometry from angles a satellite never gets. If your portfolio is full of these buildings, the accuracy gain is not marginal.

**Fewer truck rolls and fewer change orders.** The economic argument is straightforward. One structured drone flight can replace a measurement visit, a second visit when the first measurements were incomplete, and the redesign cycle that follows a bad number. Every solar business that has been through it knows what a change order costs in schedule, crew time, and customer confidence. Scanifly attacks that cost directly.

**Commercial site survey.** Large rooftops are where the case is strongest. A 500 kW commercial roof has more equipment, more penetration constraints, more structural questions, and far more cost riding on getting the layout right the first time. It is also the type of site where nobody wants to walk the whole roof with a tape measure. Drone capture scales better with roof area than manual survey does, which inverts the usual assumption that drones are a residential gadget. If commercial is your core, read the [commercial solar design software guide](/blog/commercial-solar-design-software/) alongside this review.

**A shared visual record.** An underrated benefit. The 3D model becomes a common reference for sales, design, engineering, and the install crew. Arguments about what is on the roof stop being arguments, because everyone is looking at the same photographic evidence. That has a soft value that never appears in a feature comparison.

## The Real Limitations

**Drone capture is an operational commitment, not a feature.** This is the honest core of the review. Buying Scanifly means buying a workflow. You need drone hardware and spares. In the United States you need an FAA Part 107 certified remote pilot, and most other markets impose an equivalent licensing regime. You need insurance, airspace awareness near controlled zones, and a weather window. You need a person whose schedule can absorb travel to site before design work can start. None of that is a criticism of the software. It is simply the cost of the method, and it is the part buyers consistently underestimate when they evaluate the licence in isolation.

**It inserts a site visit into a process many teams are trying to remove one from.** The last decade of solar software has been about designing without going to site. Scanifly runs against that current on purpose, and for the right roofs that is correct. But it does mean the time from lead to first design gets longer, because you cannot produce the model until somebody has flown the building. For a sales motion that depends on quoting fast, that delay has a real conversion cost you should measure rather than assume away.

**Overkill on simple residential roofs.** A 6 kW system on a clean south-facing gable with no shading does not need survey-grade photogrammetry. In our own testing, satellite-derived 3D modelling gets close enough on those roofs that the layout, the string design, and the production estimate all land in the same place. Paying for a flight on that job buys precision you will never use. The exception, and it is a genuine one, is when local imagery is old or low resolution, which is common in parts of India and other fast-growing markets.

**Not positioned as a proposal platform.** Scanifly has added proposal capability, but the product's depth sits in survey, and in our view buying it to solve a proposal problem is a mistake. Production simulation depth, financial modelling across tariff and subsidy structures, and branded customer documents are the domain of design platforms. Expect to run Scanifly alongside one, and budget for both. Confirm the current scope with the vendor.

**Cost is reported to scale with sites rather than seats.** Scanifly does not publish list prices, so pricing is quote-based and reported structures vary. Buyers commonly describe volume-linked arrangements. Volume-linked pricing is not inherently bad, and for a low-volume commercial engineering shop it is arguably fairer than per-seat billing. But if your quote works that way, cost tracks project count, so a growth year raises the bill in a way a flat seat licence does not. Model your own quote against projected volume, not last year's.

## Who Should Buy Scanifly

Buy it if several of these describe your business.

- Commercial and industrial rooftops are a meaningful share of your pipeline.
- Your buildings are complex: multi-plane, heavily obstructed, or surrounded by mature trees.
- You are already absorbing change orders or redesigns caused by bad site measurements.
- You have, or can realistically hire, a certified drone pilot who is not your best designer.
- Your customers or engineering partners expect documented as-built evidence of the site.
- Satellite imagery for your service area is stale, low resolution, or frequently obstructed.

At that profile, the drone overhead is not overhead. It is the cheapest way to buy certainty about a building, and the avoided rework pays for the programme.

## Who Should Not Buy Scanifly

- **Small residential teams with simple roofs.** The precision gain is real but too small to justify the operational load.
- **Anyone who cannot staff a pilot.** Software you cannot fly is shelfware. Do not buy the licence hoping the workflow will organise itself later.
- **Teams whose bottleneck is proposal turnaround.** If deals are lost to slow quoting, adding a site visit makes the problem worse, not better.
- **Anyone expecting it to replace a design platform.** That is not what it is positioned to do, so scope it as a survey capability alongside one.
- **High-volume residential operations quoting remotely.** The model conflicts with the sales motion.

## Verdict

Scanifly is a good product that solves a real problem properly. The photogrammetry works, the as-built models are trustworthy in a way inferred models are not, and on complex or commercial roofs it removes a class of expensive surprises that no amount of remote imagery will catch. Teams doing difficult buildings should take it seriously.

The honest verdict is not about software quality. It is about a workflow decision that sits underneath the purchase: is drone capture worth the overhead on your projects. That overhead is hardware, certification, insurance, weather, and a site visit ahead of design. On a heavily obstructed commercial roof, the answer is clearly yes. On a clean residential gable where satellite-derived modelling lands within a few percent, the answer is just as clearly no, and paying the overhead anyway is precision you cannot spend.

The mistake to avoid is buying Scanifly as a design platform substitute. It is not positioned as one, it does not claim to be one, and the comparison tables that score it that way are measuring the wrong thing. Scope it as a survey capability, run it alongside a design and proposal platform, and it will do exactly the job it was built for.

Three things to do next:

- Audit your last 20 projects and count how many actually had a measurement problem. That number decides whether you need drone survey at all.
- Price the full drone programme, including pilot time, certification, hardware, and the schedule delay, not just the software.
- Model your simplest and your most complex roof in a satellite-based platform and compare both against reality before committing either way.
