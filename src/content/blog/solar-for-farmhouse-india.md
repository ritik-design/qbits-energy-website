## Why Farmhouse Grids Fail: Voltage Swings and Long Outages

Farmhouse feeders fail in two ways at once. The power goes off for hours, and when it is on, the voltage often swings too low or too high for standard equipment.

The root cause is physical. A farmhouse typically sits at the end of an 11 kV rural feeder that may run 15 to 30 km from the substation. Every kilometre adds resistance. When farmers switch on irrigation pumps at the same time, voltage at the far end can sag below 180 V. At night, when the load drops, it can float above 250 V. The Central Electricity Authority prescribes a declared voltage band for low-tension supply. Rural feeders routinely drift outside it during peak agricultural seasons, per CEA supply standards (2024).

Outages compound the problem. The same Prayas Energy Group monitoring that recorded roughly nine hours of rural outages per day also found that cuts are erratic, not scheduled. For a farmhouse owner, this means the AC trips during a weekend party and the bore well pump refuses to start on low voltage. The fridge full of food spoils during the week while nobody is there.

Standard on-grid inverters make things worse, not better. Their anti-islanding protection disconnects them the instant the grid fails, and most of them refuse to reconnect until voltage sits stably inside a narrow band. An inverter that is not tuned for Indian grid conditions can spend half the day offline even when power is technically available. This is exactly why we built the Qbits range with a wide 180 to 270 V operating window. The details are in [why India needs grid-tuned inverters](/blog/india-grid-tuned-inverters/) and the engineering behind [tuning inverters for the Indian grid](/blog/tuning-inverters-indian-grid/).

> **What most farmhouse owners get wrong.** They measure grid quality by whether the bulb glows. A bulb glows at 150 V. An AC compressor, an inverter, and a pump starter do not. Measure voltage at your farmhouse with a simple plug-in meter for one week before you design the system.

## Why Hybrid Plus Battery Is the Default Answer for Farmhouses

For a farmhouse on a weak rural grid, a hybrid inverter with battery storage is the correct default, and on-grid or pure off-grid are the exceptions.

The logic is simple. On-grid fails because it produces nothing during outages, which at a farmhouse is most of the time you are actually there in summer. Pure off-grid fails because it needs a battery bank sized for your entire consumption. It also wastes surplus solar once batteries are full, since it cannot export to the grid. Hybrid sits between the two. It exports surplus to the grid when the grid is healthy, and islands the house onto solar plus battery the moment the grid drops.

Three farmhouse realities make the battery non-negotiable:

- **Weekday absence.** The house sits empty five days a week. Solar charges the battery all week, and the stored energy covers the weekend load without depending on the grid at all.
- **Night loads.** ACs, lights, and the fridge run hardest after sunset. Without a battery, evening comfort still depends on the rural feeder.
- **Pump starts.** A battery-backed hybrid inverter delivers the starting surge for pumps cleanly, even at 6 in the morning when the grid is at its weakest.

The battery also replaces the diesel generator in practice. A 10 kWh lithium iron phosphate bank can run a farmhouse's overnight lights, fans, fridge, and one inverter AC for a full night. Diesel costs ₹18 to ₹24 per unit generated, counting fuel and maintenance. That makes every stored solar unit worth far more than the ₹7 to ₹9 grid tariff city homeowners calculate against.

## Typical Farmhouse Loads and How to Size the System

Size a farmhouse system from the load list, not from the city-home rule of thumb, because pumps and multiple ACs change the answer completely.

The table below shows a realistic load profile for a mid-sized Indian farmhouse, typical of the outskirts of Ahmedabad, Pune, and the Delhi NCR farmhouse belt.

**Table 1: Typical farmhouse load profile (mid-sized, 3 to 4 bedrooms)**

| Load | Running Power | Surge / Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1.5-tonne inverter AC (x2) | 1.2 to 1.5 kW each | Up to 4.5 kW surge each at start |
| Bore well pump, 1.5 HP | 1.1 kW | 3 to 3.5 kW starting surge |
| Ceiling fans (x6) | 0.45 kW | Runs evenings and nights |
| Refrigerator | 0.2 kW | Runs 24x7, even when empty |
| LED lighting, full property | 0.3 kW | Includes outdoor and gate lights |
| Staff quarter loads | 0.4 kW | Fan, lights, small TV |
| CCTV and router | 0.1 kW | Must stay on backup always |
| Miscellaneous | 0.2 kW | Chargers, kitchen appliances |

Simultaneous running load lands around 4 to 5 kW with both ACs on. Surge can touch 9 to 10 kW if an AC and the pump start together. A 5 kW hybrid inverter with a healthy overload rating handles this if the pump is scheduled. A 10 kW unit is the comfortable answer for farmhouses with three or more ACs or a pool pump.

Panel capacity should exceed inverter capacity by 20 to 40 percent, so 6 to 7 kWp of panels behind a 5 kW inverter. That oversizing matters at farmhouses because generation must both run daytime loads and fully charge the battery before evening. ## Rooftop vs Ground Mounting on Farmhouse Land

Farmhouses are one of the few residential cases in India where ground mounting is a genuine option, and often the better one.

**Table 2: Rooftop vs ground mounting for farmhouse solar**

| Factor | Rooftop | Ground Mount |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cost per kW | Lower (base case) | 10 to 20 percent higher (structure, civil, trenching) |
| PM Surya Ghar subsidy | Eligible (residential connection) | Not eligible |
| Orientation | Fixed by roof pitch and direction | Perfect south-facing tilt, any angle |
| Capacity limit | Roof area, often 40 to 80 sq m usable | Effectively unlimited on one acre plus |
| Shading | Trees and parapets often shade farmhouse roofs | Site the array in a clear patch |
| Cleaning and service | Roof access needed | Ground-level, easy for staff |
| Security | Out of reach | Needs fencing or boundary placement |
| Land use | None | 60 to 100 sq m per 5 kWp |

The subsidy point decides many projects. PM Surya Ghar Muft Bijli Yojana covers residential rooftop systems only, according to MNRE (2026). The central subsidy is ₹30,000 per kW for the first 2 kW and ₹18,000 for the third kW, a maximum of ₹78,000. Nothing more is paid for capacity above 3 kW. If your farmhouse connection is a domestic category connection and the roof is usable, rooftop mounting plus subsidy is hard to beat.

The exception is common, though. Many farmhouse roofs are sloped tile or sheet roofs with poor orientation, heavy tree shade, or structural doubts. In that case, choose a ground-mounted array along the boundary wall. Oriented true south at a 20 to 25 degree tilt, it generates 8 to 12 percent more than a badly oriented roof array. It can also scale to any size. Spend the extra 10 to 20 percent on structure and civil work, and treat the lost subsidy as the price of a better plant.

A hybrid approach also works well. Use a rooftop array for the subsidy-eligible portion and a ground array for the balance, both feeding the same hybrid inverter on separate MPPT inputs.

## Solar for Water Pumps, Staff Quarters, and Remote Security

Pumps are the load that separates farmhouse solar design from city solar design, and they deserve their own plan.

A bore well or surface pump of 1 to 3 HP is the single hardest load in the house. Its starting surge and low tolerance for voltage dips are the reason. The clean solution is to schedule pumping for 10 am to 3 pm, when solar generation is at its peak and the battery is already charging. A hybrid inverter handles the surge from the battery and solar together, so the pump starts cleanly even on a weak-grid morning. The dedicated engineering is covered in our [solar inverter for water pump](/blog/solar-inverter-for-water-pump/) guide.

Staff quarters change the load math in one specific way: they consume power when you are not there. A caretaker's fan, lights, and TV add 2 to 3 kWh of nightly consumption, seven days a week. This is actually good for system economics, because it means the battery cycles daily and the solar generation is never wasted. Count these loads explicitly in the sizing table rather than discovering them after commissioning.

Security loads, CCTV, gate lights, and the router, are small but critical. Put them on the backup circuit without exception. A farmhouse that loses its cameras during a weeknight outage loses the main reason the cameras exist. The draw is only 100 to 150 W, so a 10 kWh battery carries security loads for days even with zero solar input.

## Monitoring and Security From the City

A farmhouse solar system must be observable from 100 km away, or small faults will silently eat your generation for months.

The failure pattern we see repeatedly is this. An owner installs a system, visits monthly, and discovers in month four that a string fuse blew in week two. Half the array has been idle for months. At a farmhouse, nobody checks the inverter display. Remote monitoring is not a luxury feature here; it is the difference between a working plant and a decoration.

The practical requirements:

- **Works on 4G, not broadband.** Most farmhouses have no wired internet. The inverter must push data over a basic mobile connection.
- **Pushes alerts to you.** Daily generation summary, battery state, and fault alerts should arrive on a channel you actually read. Qbits inverters send all of this over WhatsApp, so a caretaker or owner sees a fault the same day. - **Tracks battery health.** In a weak-grid area the battery cycles hard. Watch the depth of discharge and charge patterns monthly.

For physical security, mount the inverter inside a locked room and keep the battery bank indoors. Put one 4G camera watching the array if it is ground-mounted. Panel theft from isolated properties is rare but real in some districts, and a ₹5,000 camera on the backup circuit is cheap insurance.

## The Tradeoffs Nobody Mentions

Honest setup advice requires naming the downsides, because farmhouse solar has real ones.

- **Battery is half the budget and the shortest-lived component.** LFP banks last 10 to 15 years, but lead-acid alternatives die in 4 to 6 years in hot rural conditions. Do not let an installer quietly substitute lead-acid to win the quote.
- **Hybrid costs more up front and pays back slower than city on-grid.** You are buying reliability and diesel displacement, not the fastest possible ROI. If your farmhouse somehow has a stable grid, on-grid is the financially better answer. That exception is rare, but it exists.
- **Ground mounting invites vegetation and security headaches.** Uncut grass shades the bottom row of panels by monsoon end, and an unfenced array is a theft and cattle-damage risk. Budget for upkeep or a fence.
- **Oversized systems on weak grids trip on export.** Some rural feeders cannot absorb export at noon, and the inverter derates or trips. A hybrid with zero-export control solves this, but confirm the feature is enabled at commissioning.
- **Weekday-empty houses waste rooftop-only systems.** Without a battery, most generation happens while nobody is home and exports at low feed-in rates. This is the quiet reason so many farmhouse on-grid systems disappoint their owners.

Our opinionated take, after watching dozens of these projects: the battery is not an accessory at a farmhouse. It is the point of the system. Everything else, panels, inverter, mounting, exists to keep that battery charged.
