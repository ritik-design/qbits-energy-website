## Why Schools Are the Ideal Solar Candidate in Tamil Nadu

The phrase "solar makes sense" is used so broadly in India that it has lost most of its analytical precision. For a factory running night shifts, the self-consumption rate after 6 PM drops sharply, and export earnings depend on DISCOM grid health. For a residential complex, subsidy calculations and sanctioned-load limits dominate the decision. Schools are different (structurally different) for three reasons.

**First**, the consumption window is almost identical to the generation window. A CBSE school operates from roughly 8 AM to 4 PM, Monday through Saturday. Solar generation in Tamil Nadu builds from 8 AM, peaks between 11 AM and 1 PM, and tapers after 3:30 PM. The two curves overlay with a correlation that no other commercial building type can match. The result is a [self-consumption](/glossary/cuf/) rate (the fraction of generated solar power consumed on-site rather than exported) that regularly reaches 85–92% for a well-sized school system. Compare that with a retail mall (55–65%), a factory with a B-shift (40–50%), or a hospital running 24 hours (65–75%).

**Second**, the load is predictable and measurable from DISCOM billing history. Unlike a factory where production volumes shift monthly, a school's electricity consumption in June 2025 is a reliable predictor of June 2026 (within 5–10%) because the occupancy pattern, AC setpoints, and computer lab schedules barely change year to year. That predictability makes solar sizing unusually accurate.

**Third**, Tamil Nadu's solar resource is among the strongest in India. Coimbatore receives 5.2–5.5 peak sun hours per day averaged across the year, with minimal haze and relatively low humidity compared to coastal Tamil Nadu. The [Central Electricity Authority](https://cea.nic.in/) radiation data confirms that rooftop systems in this region yield 1,450–1,550 kWh per kWp per year under optimal conditions.

> **5.3 peak sun hours per day.** Coimbatore's annual average solar irradiation, among the top 10 sites for rooftop solar in India. A 25 kWp system yields approximately 37,500 kWh per year at a performance ratio of 0.78. *Source - [MNRE Solar Resource Assessment, National Institute of Wind Energy data](https://mnre.gov.in/), 2025.*

The combination of these three factors (timing alignment, load predictability, and strong irradiation) is why schools in Tamil Nadu consistently deliver the fastest payback periods and the highest self-consumption rates in the C&I solar segment.

## The School's Load Profile and Why 25 kW Was the Right Size

Before arriving at 25 kW, the EPC team conducted a detailed load audit of this particular school. The methodology matters because over-sizing a system for a school is a common and expensive mistake, a 40 kW system on a campus that peaks at 20 kW during assembly hours will export 30–40% of its generation at the TNEB feed-in tariff of ₹2–3 per unit, sharply extending the payback period.

The school's sanctioned load with TANGEDCO was 50 kVA. The actual measured peak demand (logged from the energy meter during the three-week audit in May 2025) reached 21 kW on a hot afternoon with 22 out of 24 air conditioners running simultaneously. The breakdown was as follows:

| Load Category | Number of Units | Connected Load (kW) | Typical Load Factor |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Classroom ACs (1.5 ton split) | 22 units | 16.5 kW | 0.80 (not all at full blast simultaneously) |
| Computer lab PCs + server | 40 PCs + 1 server | 2.5 kW | 0.90 |
| Cafeteria (microwave, refrigerators, fans) | Various | 1.8 kW | 0.60 |
| Lighting (LED, corridors, classrooms) | 200+ fixtures | 2.0 kW | 0.85 |
| Admin block (PCs, printer, PA system) | Various | 1.2 kW | 0.70 |
| Miscellaneous (water coolers, pumps) | Various | 0.8 kW | 0.50 |
| **Total peak demand** | | **~20 kW** | |

A system sized at 20 kW would match peak demand but leave no headroom for future load additions, the management was planning two additional smart classrooms with interactive boards. A 25 kW system was chosen to cover the expanded load while keeping the DC oversizing ratio at a sensible 1.26× relative to peak AC demand.

The 25 kW system uses a single Qbits TLD-25K three-phase on-grid inverter, handling the school's three-phase TANGEDCO supply without the phase-imbalance issues that arise when multiple single-phase inverters are installed on different phases independently. Three-phase architecture is important for schools with centralised AC systems, since split-phase loading causes neutral current issues in LT distribution panels.

## The School Solar ROI Framework

Every school management committee reviewing a solar proposal faces the same four questions in the same sequence. The School Solar ROI Framework structures the decision-making process so that no assumption is left unstated and the payback calculation reflects actual site conditions.

### Step 1: Measure the Consumption Window

Pull 12 months of DISCOM billing data and identify what fraction of consumption falls between 8 AM and 4 PM. For a school, this fraction is typically 80–90%. The closer it is to 90%, the higher the self-consumption rate will be, and the more aggressive the savings projection. If the school has a hostel or residential quarters, night-time consumption may reduce the fraction to 65–70%, and the system size calculation changes accordingly.

### Step 2: Match System Capacity to the 8 AM–4 PM Load

Size the solar system to cover 95–105% of the daytime peak load, not the total sanctioned load. The sanctioned load for a school is often set conservatively high for future expansion; installing solar to match it produces an oversized system with chronic export at low tariff. The school in this case study has a 50 kVA sanctioned load but a 20 kW measured daytime peak, the 25 kW solar system covers the peak with a 25% buffer, not a 25 kW system chasing a 50 kVA ceiling.

### Step 3: Apply TNEB Net Metering Correctly

Under [TNERC net metering regulations](https://www.tneb.in/), commercial consumers can net exported units against imported units at the prevailing tariff, with annual settlement of accumulated credits. The critical insight is that during vacation periods (May–June and December–January) the school generates solar power but consumes almost nothing. Those export units accumulate as credits. During peak academic months (July–November), the school draws on those credits to offset high-consumption months. The net metering mechanism effectively lets the school use the grid as a zero-cost battery across the year.

### Step 4: Layer Accelerated Depreciation

Section 32 of the Income Tax Act allows [accelerated depreciation](/glossary/accelerated-depreciation/) at 40% on solar energy equipment in the year of commissioning. For a school trust registered as a company paying 25–30% corporate tax, a ₹14 Lakh installation yields a depreciation benefit of ₹5.6 Lakh in year one, producing a tax saving of ₹1.4–1.7 Lakh. The effective net cost of the system falls to ₹12.3–12.6 Lakh, shortening the payback by six to eight months. Trusts with charitable registration should verify applicability with their chartered accountant before factoring this in.

## System Configuration: 63 Panels, One Inverter, One Rooftop

The physical configuration of the Coimbatore school's 25 kW system was driven by three constraints: roof area available on the main building terrace, the three-phase connection requirement, and the need to minimise shading from the overhead water tank and two staircase head-rooms.

The [site survey](/glossary/site-survey/) identified 160 square metres of unshaded south-facing roof at a natural slope of 8° on a reinforced concrete flat terrace. The final layout uses 63 panels of 400 Wp each (total installed capacity: 25.2 kWp) arranged in two strings per MPPT channel on the Qbits TLD-25K inverter:

- **String configuration**: 21 panels per string, 3 strings, Voc at standard test conditions 43.2 V × 21 = 907 V (within the inverter's 1,000 V maximum DC input)
- **MPPT channels**: Two, allowing the installer to split the array across two roof sections with slightly different shading profiles
- **Panel type**: 400 Wp monocrystalline [PERC](/glossary/perc-cell/) half-cut modules, ALMM-listed
- **Mounting structure**: Hot-dip galvanised MS railing at 10° tilt, anchored without penetrating the waterproofing membrane using ballast-weighted brackets

The inverter was installed in the electrical room adjacent to the main distribution board, an air-conditioned space that ensures the inverter operates below 35 °C ambient, avoiding the thermal derating that affects rooftop-mounted inverters in Tamil Nadu's 40–45 °C summer afternoons. For a 25 kW unit drawing ₹14 Lakh in capital expenditure, protecting the inverter from thermal stress adds years to its operational life.

The Qbits TLD-25K's IP66 rating provides weather protection against the heavy south-west monsoon rains that reach Coimbatore in June–August. The school originally requested a wall-mounted outdoor location near the panels to reduce DC cable length, but the EPC team recommended the indoor electrical room for both temperature and security reasons, an outdoor inverter in a school compound is accessible to students and vulnerable to accidental contact.

## TNEB Net Metering Process: What the School Actually Did

The net metering application process with TANGEDCO was the most time-consuming element of the project, though not unusually so for Tamil Nadu. The complete sequence took 74 days from initial application to bidirectional meter installation.

### Documentation Submitted

The school gathered the following documents for the TANGEDCO net metering application:

- Latest three months of electricity bills (for sanctioned load verification)
- School's property tax receipt and ownership document
- Society/trust registration certificate
- Single-line diagram of the proposed solar installation, signed by a licensed electrical contractor
- Technical specification of the inverter and panels, including ALMM certification numbers
- No-objection certificate from the school's electrical contractor

### TANGEDCO Inspection and Approval

TANGEDCO's distribution team conducted a site inspection within 18 days of application. The inspector verified that the proposed system capacity (25 kWp) did not exceed the school's sanctioned load (50 kVA = approximately 40 kW usable), and that the inverter model was on the [MNRE ALMM list](https://mnre.gov.in/almm-list/), the Approved Models and Manufacturers list that is a mandatory compliance requirement for all grid-connected solar installations in India. The approval-in-principle was issued on day 26.

### Bidirectional Meter Installation

TANGEDCO replaced the existing single-direction energy meter with a [bidirectional meter](/glossary/bidirectional-meter/) on day 74. From that date, every unit exported to the grid during vacation periods earns a credit at ₹3.00 per unit, netted against the school's monthly import bill. The school now receives a single monthly bill showing net units (imports minus exports) at the applicable tariff slab.

The process description here is representative of the TANGEDCO net metering flow as of June 2026. Individual TANGEDCO divisions (Coimbatore, North, South, Rural) may have slightly different processing timelines and documentation requirements. The school's EPC partner managed the application and follow-up on the school's behalf, reducing the management committee's time investment to document signing and a single site-visit day.

The pure on-grid configuration is optimal when the grid is reliable during school hours and the school does not have a critical-continuity requirement. A hybrid system makes sense for schools in Tier-2 towns with 2–3 hour midday power cuts that affect exams or computer lab sessions. ## Common Sizing and Design Mistakes to Avoid

Schools that have gone through a poorly designed solar installation tend to make one of five predictable mistakes. School administrators reviewing a solar proposal should check each of these before signing an EPC contract.

- **Oversizing to match sanctioned load**: A 50 kVA sanctioned load does not mean a 50 kW solar system is the right choice. Always size to measured daytime peak demand, not to the fuse rating. Oversizing generates chronic export at below-tariff prices and extends the payback by 2–3 years.
- **Ignoring three-phase imbalance**: Schools with three-phase supply that install multiple single-phase inverters on separate phases risk neutral current issues and potential damage to sensitive lab equipment. A single three-phase inverter or a properly balanced multi-inverter configuration avoids this problem.
- **Skipping the shading analysis**: Water tanks, staircase rooms, and antenna masts cast shadows that reduce annual yield by 5–15% if not accounted for during layout planning. A proper [shading analysis](/glossary/shading-analysis/) using the PVSyst simulation tool or equivalent should be a mandatory deliverable in the EPC proposal.
- **Accepting a warranty shorter than 12 years**: An inverter is the only active electronic component in a solar system and the most likely to fail before the panels degrade. A 5-year warranty on a 25-year asset shifts the replacement risk entirely to the school. The [solar inverter warranty](/blog/solar-inverter-warranty/) on any system for a school should be 12 years minimum, full replacement.
- **No fire or surge protection**: School electrical rooms sometimes lack proper DC-side surge protection devices (SPDs). A lightning strike or grid surge event without DC SPDs can destroy the inverter and cause a DC arc fault. Confirm that the system includes DC SPD, AC SPD, and a DC isolator rated for the string configuration.

The [solar inverter payback period](/blog/solar-inverter-payback-period-in-india/) analysis for any school should account for these risks as adjustment factors. An undersized design or an inverter failure in year six without warranty coverage can erase several years of projected savings in one event.

## The Decision Checklist for School Management Committees

Before a school management committee authorises a solar installation, every item on this list should have a documented answer:

**Financial verification**
- ✓ 12-month TANGEDCO billing history pulled and peak demand measured
- ✓ System size confirmed at 95–105% of measured daytime peak, not sanctioned load
- ✓ Payback calculated using self-consumed units at commercial tariff, not at export tariff
- ✓ Accelerated depreciation eligibility confirmed with chartered accountant

**Technical verification**
- ✓ Three-phase vs single-phase inverter decision made based on supply configuration
- ✓ Shading analysis completed using PVSyst or equivalent simulation
- ✓ DC and AC SPD specified in the bill of materials
- ✓ ALMM-listed panels and BIS-certified inverter confirmed
- ✓ Net metering pre-approval feasibility checked with local TANGEDCO division

**Contract verification**
- ✓ Inverter warranty: 12 years full replacement
- ✓ Panel warranty: 25-year linear performance
- ✓ EPC workmanship warranty: minimum 5 years
- ✓ Service SLA: maximum 72-hour fault response
- ✓ Monitoring: daily generation reports, WhatsApp alerts for faults

**Educational and accreditation**
- ✓ Lobby monitoring display included in scope (optional but high visibility)
- ✓ Generation data format suitable for CBSE Green School Award documentation
- ✓ Annual CO2 report to be provided by EPC or inverter monitoring system

A school that works through this checklist before signing an EPC contract will not experience the most common post-installation regrets: discovering an undersized system two years later, finding that the warranty is prorated and the inverter replacement costs ₹2 Lakh out of pocket, or realising that the net metering application was never submitted.
