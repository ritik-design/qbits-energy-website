The hardest number in a Polish solar proposal is the one nobody can pin down. RCEm, the market reference rate that settles prosumer exports under net billing, was 132.92 PLN per MWh in April 2026, the lowest since the mechanism was introduced, and 191.37 in May. That is a swing of more than 40 percent in the value of exported energy inside a single month. A proposal that quotes one export rate as fact is quoting a number that may be wrong before the customer signs. The way out is not a better forecast, it is a document built around the part of the value that does not move. This guide compares the platforms Polish installers should shortlist.

## Build The Document On What Does Not Move

> **What most Polish quotes get wrong.** They lead with annual generation and attach an export revenue figure calculated at whatever RCEm happened to be that month. Both numbers look precise and one of them is close to meaningless.

The structural fix is to reorganise the proposal around the stable half of the equation.

| Component | Stability | Where it belongs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Avoided import at retail | Moves slowly and predictably | The centre of the document |
| Self-consumed volume | Determined by load profile and storage | The number to optimise and highlight |
| Exported volume | Determined by design | Shown, but as the residual |
| RCEm export rate | Moved 40 percent in one month | A tested range, never a point estimate |

A proposal organised this way says: here is how much energy this household will use directly, here is what that saves at today's retail prices, and here is a smaller additional amount from exports which will vary. That is a claim you can stand behind in twelve months.

The alternative, leading with a single payback figure derived from one month's RCEm, is a claim with a short shelf life. It also invites a competitor quoting during a high-RCEm month to show a better number for an identical system.

## Show Payback As A Range, With The Reason

Precise payback figures are a habit worth breaking in Poland specifically.

Honest ranges look roughly like this: photovoltaics without storage in the region of nine to thirteen years, with storage roughly seven to ten, and with subsidy applied closer to five to six. Those bands move with electricity prices and with RCEm.

Quoting "8.4 years" implies a precision the mechanism does not support, and it is trivially falsifiable later. Quoting "roughly seven to ten years, and here is what moves it" is both accurate and more persuasive to a customer who has done any reading, because it signals that you understand the variable rather than pretending it away.

There is a competitive benefit too. When you show the range and explain that the low end assumes strong RCEm and high electricity prices, a rival's confident single figure becomes something the customer can interrogate.

## Mój Prąd Is Configuration-Dependent, So Show Configurations

Support is not a flat discount to subtract at the end. Photovoltaics alone attracts around 7,000 PLN, while an integrated system including storage, a heat pump and energy management can reach substantially higher, with storage support of up to around 16,000 PLN within that.

That means the subsidy is an output of the design conversation, not an input to the price. The proposal structure that follows naturally is a comparison of two or three configurations, each showing its own support level, its own self-consumption share and its own payback range.

This is a better sales document than a single-option quote for a reason that has nothing to do with subsidies. It moves the conversation from "is this worth doing?" to "which of these is right for us?", and it lets the customer arrive at the storage option themselves rather than being sold it.

## What Else Belongs In The Document

**The 8 percent VAT rate, as a visible line.** Residential installations attract the reduced rate, customers often do not know this, and it improves the comparison against other home improvements. Confirm applicability rather than assuming it, since commercial work may not qualify.

**A stated self-consumption assumption.** This is now the most load-bearing number in the proposal. Say where it came from and show what happens if it lands ten points lower.

**Winter expectations.** Polish winter generation is a small fraction of summer output. A customer who has seen the monthly profile before signing does not phone in December.

**Shading priced by hour, not percentage.** Under net billing an annual shading percentage does not convert into money. Our [solar shading analysis software Poland](/blog/solar-shading-analysis-software-poland/) guide covers why.

## Worked Example: The Same System Quoted In Two Months

An illustrative calculation showing why a point estimate fails, not a specific customer project. Inputs: 8 kWp array, 8,000 kWh generation, 5,000 kWh consumption, 35% self-consumption, 5,200 kWh exported.

| | Quoted in April at 0.133 PLN | Quoted in May at 0.191 PLN | Quoted as a range |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Export revenue shown | about 690 PLN | about 990 PLN | 690 to 990 PLN |
| Avoided import | Unchanged | Unchanged | Unchanged |
| Payback headline | Longer | Shorter | A band, with drivers named |
| Accurate in the other month? | No | No | Yes |
| Survives customer scrutiny? | No | No | Yes |

Two installers quoting the identical system five weeks apart produce materially different documents, and both are defensible on the day and wrong shortly after. The third column is the only one that stays true.

Note what does not change across the columns: avoided import. That is the argument to lead with, and it is stable precisely because it depends on retail prices and the household's own consumption rather than on a wholesale reference. Every hour of generation moved from the export column into the self-consumption column moves value from the volatile side of this table to the stable side.
