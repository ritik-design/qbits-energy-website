## Understanding Inverter Financing in 2026

India's solar capacity exceeds 100 GW cumulative in 2026 with annual additions exceeding 20 GW. The market has developed specialised financing mechanisms for solar equipment procurement, addressing working capital challenges for EPCs, distributors, and end-customers.

Five forces shape the 2026 financing landscape:

- **PM Surya Ghar Muft Bijli Yojana** with up to ₹78,000 central subsidy driving residential adoption
- **IREDA expansion** of concessional renewable energy lending
- **Bank product specialisation** for solar across major public and private banks
- **NBFC growth** in solar-specific financing with faster processing
- **Corporate sustainability mandates** driving CAPEX commitments from large businesses

For any buyer profile, financing options are now more accessible than at any previous point in the Indian solar market.

## Financing Option 1: Upfront Cash Purchase

The simplest and historically most common approach for cash-rich buyers.

### Advantages

- **Eliminates interest charges** entirely
- **Simplifies accounting** with single asset capitalisation
- **Qualifies for accelerated depreciation** at 40% WDV in year one (Section 32)
- **Tax shield offsets opportunity cost** of capital deployed
- **No ongoing payment obligations** to track
- **Negotiating leverage** with EPCs for better pricing on cash deals

### Best for

- Established businesses with strong cash flow and tax liability
- Residential buyers with available capital and tax-advantaged accounts
- Distributors managing working capital cycles
- Buyers prioritising simplicity over leverage

### Considerations

For business buyers with significant tax liability, the after-tax effective cost of upfront purchase is often lower than financed alternatives once the Section 32 depreciation tax shield is applied. Run the after-tax comparison before defaulting to financing.

## Financing Option 2: EMI Schemes and Loan-Based Financing

The dominant approach for residential PM Surya Ghar buyers and growing EPCs.

### Current 2026 market rates

| Parameter | 2026 range |
| --- | --- |
| Interest rate (banks) | 10-12% annually |
| Interest rate (IREDA) | 8.5-10% annually (concessional) |
| Interest rate (NBFC) | 11-14% annually |
| Tenure | 12-60 months typically |
| Loan-to-value ratio | 70-90% depending on profile |
| Processing fee | 0.5-2% of loan amount |
| Prepayment penalty | 0-2% (varies by lender) |

### Advantages

- **Monthly payments** become predictable operational expenses
- **Working capital preservation** for growing businesses
- **Bulk financing arrangements** available for distributors and large EPCs
- **EMI offset** by monthly electricity bill savings (often cash-flow neutral)
- **Subsidy integration** - PM Surya Ghar disbursed post-installation, applied to principal

### Best for

- Growing EPCs with project pipelines requiring working capital
- Residential PM Surya Ghar eligible buyers
- Mid-size commercial buyers preferring leverage
- Distributors needing inventory financing

## Financing Option 3: CAPEX vs OPEX Models

Strategic choice for commercial and industrial buyers between owning the solar asset and consuming the electricity as a service.

| Model | Pros | Cons |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **CAPEX** (own the system) | Full ownership, asset control, depreciation benefits, lower 10-year TCO | Capital intensive upfront, project management overhead |
| **OPEX/PPA** (third-party owns) | Pay-as-you-go, off-balance-sheet, bundled maintenance, no capital required | Higher lifetime cost, no depreciation benefit, no asset ownership |

### Decision drivers

- **Tax liability**: businesses with high tax liability favour CAPEX for Section 32 depreciation
- **Capital availability**: businesses constrained on capital prefer OPEX
- **Operational ownership**: businesses wanting full control prefer CAPEX
- **Balance sheet management**: businesses preferring off-balance-sheet leverage OPEX

For businesses with tax liability above ₹50 lakh annually, CAPEX with WDV depreciation almost always wins on 10-year TCO basis. See the [solar inverter depreciation in India guide](/blog/solar-inverter-depreciation-in-india/) for detailed tax shield analysis.

## Financing Option 4: Leasing Structures

Less common but useful for specific buyer profiles.

| Lease type | Structure | End-of-term |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Operating lease** | Monthly rental payments, asset on lessor's balance sheet | Asset returned to lessor |
| **Finance lease** | Higher monthly payments, asset on lessee's balance sheet | Ownership transfers to lessee |

Typical terms:

- 5-10 year tenor
- Minimal upfront capital
- Tax treatment varies (operating lease deductible as rental; finance lease subject to depreciation rules)

### Best for

- Businesses preferring off-balance-sheet structures
- Buyers without sufficient capital for upfront purchase
- Specific accounting or tax planning requirements

## Required Documentation

Standard documentation across most loan applications:

### For business borrowers

- Business registration certificate (proprietorship, partnership, LLP, or company)
- GST returns for last 2 years
- 2-3 years of audited income tax returns
- Bank statements (6-12 months)
- Inverter quotations from approved manufacturers
- Client contracts demonstrating project pipeline (for EPCs)
- Land/premises documents
- Identity proof of directors/partners

### For residential borrowers

- Identity and address proof (Aadhaar, PAN)
- Income proof (salary slips for last 6 months, or ITR for last 2 years)
- Bank statements (6 months)
- PM Surya Ghar registration certificate (if applicable)
- Quotation from empanelled installer
- Roof ownership / society NOC for installations

### For IREDA-specific loans

- Additional technical documentation: system design, panel specifications, EPC capability statement
- Detailed project DPR for larger installations
- Performance guarantees from EPC
