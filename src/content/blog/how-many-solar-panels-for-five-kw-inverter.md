A 5 kW inverter works well with 9 to 11 panels of the sizes sold in India today. With 580 W panels that is 9 panels (5.22 kWp) or 10 panels (5.80 kWp). With 540 W panels it is 10 or 11. With 625 W panels it is 8 or 9. The exact number depends on the panel wattage, your roof, and how far above 5 kW you are allowed and willing to go.

The reason the answer is a range is simple. The "5 kW" on the inverter is its AC output. The panels are measured in DC kWp, and the two numbers do not have to match.

>
> - Panel sizes and ratings are read from the makers' own datasheets: Vikram Solar Somera (540 W), Waaree BiN-08 (580 W and 600 W) and Waaree BiN-21 (625 W). We did not check whether a given model is on the current ALMM list. Ask your installer for the ALMM status of the exact panel.

> - The single-phase limit is shown with two regulator documents, Gujarat and Odisha. Your own DISCOM may differ.
> - **Limit:** pmsuryaghar.gov.in did not open from our network on 30 October 2026, so this post states no subsidy amount. Qbits sells inverters, not panels. The panels named here are examples, not a recommendation.

## Is a 5 kW inverter the same as 5 kWp of panels?

No. The inverter rating and the panel rating are two different measurements.

- **5 kW (inverter):** the most AC power the inverter can send into your home wiring and the grid.
- **5 kWp (panels):** the total DC rating of the panels in test conditions. A panel marked 580 W makes 580 W only in lab light at 25 °C cell temperature.

On a real roof, panels get hot and dusty. They rarely give their full rating. So an array a little bigger than the inverter is normal, and it is not a mistake. The ratio of panel kWp to inverter kW is called the DC to AC ratio. [This guide explains the ratio and clipping in detail](/blog/dc-oversizing-in-solar/).

## How many panels does a 5 kW inverter need?

The table gives panel counts for four common panel sizes, from just under 5 kWp to about 6.5 kWp. The ratio is kWp divided by 5 kW.

| Panel (datasheet) | Panels | Array size | DC to AC ratio |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 540 W (Vikram Somera) | 9 | 4.86 kWp | 0.97 |
| 540 W | 10 | 5.40 kWp | 1.08 |
| 540 W | 11 | 5.94 kWp | 1.19 |
| 540 W | 12 | 6.48 kWp | 1.30 |
| 580 W (Waaree BiN-08-580) | 9 | 5.22 kWp | 1.04 |
| 580 W | 10 | 5.80 kWp | 1.16 |
| 580 W | 11 | 6.38 kWp | 1.28 |
| 600 W (Waaree BiN-08-600) | 9 | 5.40 kWp | 1.08 |
| 600 W | 10 | 6.00 kWp | 1.20 |
| 625 W (Waaree BiN-21-625) | 8 | 5.00 kWp | 1.00 |
| 625 W | 9 | 5.63 kWp | 1.13 |
| 625 W | 10 | 6.25 kWp | 1.25 |

Read the table like this:

- **To match the inverter exactly (about 5 kWp):** 9 panels of 540 W is just under, 8 panels of 625 W is exact. 9 panels of 580 W is slightly over.
- **Common choice:** 9 or 10 panels of 580 to 600 W, which gives 5.2 to 6.0 kWp.
- **Why the count changes between quotes:** lower watt panels need more pieces for the same kWp, and higher watt panels need fewer.

The table uses three datasheets we opened. Waaree lists the BiN-08 series in steps from 565 W to 600 W, and Vikram lists Somera from 525 W to 550 W. Panels sold in your city may be different. The arithmetic stays the same: panels times watts, divided by 1,000.

## What happens when the panels add up to more than 5 kW?

A 5 kW inverter never sends out more than 5 kW, so when the panels try to give more, it holds the output at its limit. This is called clipping. It is not a fault.

Here is why it matters less than people fear. The Waaree datasheets list a power loss of 0.30% for every degree the cell is hotter than 25 °C. Other makers list a slightly different figure, so treat this as a general illustration. On a hot Indian afternoon a cell can be 65 °C, which is 40 degrees hotter. That cuts about 12% (40 times 0.30%). This is an illustrative cell temperature, not a measurement of your roof.

| Array | Rated | After 12% heat loss (illustrative) | Against a 5 kW inverter |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10 x 580 W | 5.80 kWp | about 5.10 kW | clips very little, only at peak |
| 10 x 600 W | 6.00 kWp | about 5.28 kW | clips a little near noon |
| 12 x 540 W | 6.48 kWp | about 5.70 kW | clips about 0.7 kW at peak |

These numbers are before dust, wiring and inverter losses, which reduce DC power further. In cooler winter noon sun the panels run cooler and give more power, so the clipping is larger in winter than in summer.

So 5.5 to 6.0 kWp on a 5 kW inverter is a comfortable fit. At 6.5 kWp you trade a little peak energy for more output in the morning and evening. Whether that is worth it depends on your roof and your tariff. We do not quote a clipping percentage, because it depends on your site. If you want to see how a bigger inverter instead compares, read [how to choose a 5 kW solar inverter](/blog/best-5kw-solar-inverter-india-2026/).

Two limits can stop you before clipping does:

- **Maximum DC voltage.** The Qbits QB 4.2/4.6/5/5.4/6KTLS is listed with a 600 V maximum DC input. Panels give more voltage when cold. Ten 580 W panels in one string reach about 552 V on a 5 °C morning (52.5 V open-circuit each, plus 5.2% for cold). Eleven reach about 607 V, which is over the limit. The Qbits QB 4/5/6 KTLD is listed at 550 V maximum DC, so ten 580 W panels need to be split into two strings of five on its two MPPTs.
- **Maximum DC current.** Both families are listed at 20 A per input. A 580 W panel is rated 13.93 A short-circuit current, so one string fits. Two strings joined on one input do not.

Your installer must check both against the exact datasheet. The [string sizing calculator](/string-sizing-calculator/) does the same arithmetic, and the [KTLS family page](/our-products/QB-4.6-6KTLS/) lists the model limits. This post does not state a maximum DC input power for any Qbits model. Read that line on the current datasheet for your model before you go above 5 kWp.

## How much roof does a 5 kW system need?

A 5 kWp system needs about 50 square metres of shadow-free roof, and 6 kWp about 60. The MNRE FAQ says 1 kW of rooftop solar generally needs 10 square metres of shadow-free area. It adds that the real need changes with module efficiency and placement.

The panels themselves take less. From the datasheet sizes:

| Panel | Size per panel | Area of one panel | Area of 10 panels |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 540 W (Vikram Somera) | 2,274 x 1,134 mm | 2.58 sq m | 25.8 sq m |
| 580 W or 600 W (Waaree BiN-08) | 2,278 x 1,134 mm | 2.58 sq m | 25.8 sq m |
| 625 W (Waaree BiN-21) | 2,382 x 1,134 mm | 2.70 sq m | 27.0 sq m |

So ten panels cover about 26 to 27 square metres of surface. The rest of the 50 or 60 square metres is space for tilt, row gaps so one row does not shade the next, walking room, the water tank, and stair headroom. On a flat roof with tilted rows, plan for roughly double the panel area as a first estimate. That ratio is our illustration, not a rule from any scheme.

Shade matters more than total area. The MNRE FAQ says even a shadow on part of a module reduces generation a great deal. A tank or a parapet wall that shades two panels can cost more than leaving the panels out. [This post shows how much shade costs](/blog/solar-panel-shadow-effect-generation/).

## How many units does a 5 kW system give?

On a clear sunny day, each 1 kWp of panels can generate 4 to 5.5 units, according to the MNRE FAQ. That is the best-case range, not the yearly average. Cloudy days, monsoon and dust bring the average down.

Using that clear-day range:

| Array | Clear sunny day (MNRE FAQ range) |
| --- | --- |
| 5.22 kWp (9 x 580 W) | 20.9 to 28.7 units |
| 5.80 kWp (10 x 580 W) | 23.2 to 31.9 units |
| 6.25 kWp (10 x 625 W) | 25.0 to 34.4 units |

For a yearly figure we use an illustrative average of 3.5 to 4.0 units per kWp per day over the whole year. This is our assumption and not an MNRE number. On a 5.8 kWp array it gives about 20 to 23 units a day, or about 7,400 to 8,500 units a year. Your roof direction, shade, state and cleaning habit move this up or down.

Do not read this as a bill saving. How much of the output you use at home and how your DISCOM settles exports decide that, and [this guide to reading a net meter bill](/blog/read-net-meter-bill-after-going-solar/) shows where those export units appear. For what a complete 5 kW quote must contain, see the [5 kW solar system price guide](/blog/5kw-solar-system-price-india/), which also explains why capacity alone does not set a price.

## Single phase or three phase for a 5 kW inverter?

Whether your 5 kW inverter is single phase or three phase depends on your electricity connection, not on the number of panels. Your state sets the rule, and states differ. Two examples we read from regulator documents:

- **Gujarat:** an older GERC text, the net metering regulations (Regulation 8(2), notified 18 June 2016, read in consolidated copies up to January 2020), listed a connected load up to 6 kW at 240 V single phase, and above 6 kW up to 18.65 kW at 415 V three phase. GERC's Fifth Amendment of 3 November 2025 is reported to have replaced that table with a pointer to the GERC Electricity Supply Code. We could not open that amendment, so we do not state a current Gujarat number here.
- **Odisha:** the OERC Supply Code, as quoted in OERC Case No. 16/2019, sets supply for a contract demand not exceeding 5 kW or 5.55 kVA at single phase and 230 V. Above that it is two-phase or three-phase.

In Odisha, a 5 kW inverter sits at the upper edge of the single-phase limit quoted in that 2019 order, and we did not read the current Supply Code. For Gujarat, ask your DISCOM: an older GERC text set 6 kW, but it has reportedly been replaced by a pointer to the Supply Code. Either way, ask your DISCOM what applies to your connection before you buy.

Ask three things in writing:

1. Is my connection single phase or three phase, and what is the sanctioned load?
2. Does the net-metering approval count the panel kWp or the inverter kW as my system size?
3. Does the DISCOM allow a single-phase inverter on my meter, and does it settle net metering across all phases or phase by phase?

The last one matters if you have a three-phase connection. The [single-phase versus three-phase guide](/blog/single-vs-3-phase-inverter/) explains the phase-wise netting problem.

Qbits lists 5 kW class inverters in single-phase and three-phase form, both on-grid and hybrid. They are in the QB 4.2/4.6/5/5.4/6KTLS and QB 4/5/6 KTLD families (single phase on-grid), the QB 5/6/8/10/12/15/17KTLC family (three phase on-grid), and the hybrid families QBH 3KS to 6KS48P (single phase) and QBH 5/6/7/8/10/12KS48P3 (three phase). ## Do you need a 5 kW system at all?

Check your monthly units first. On the illustrative average above, 5.8 kWp gives about 600 to 700 units a month. If your use is far below that, a smaller system may be enough. [This comparison of inverter sizes](/blog/3kw-vs-5kw-vs-10kw-solar-inverters/) helps you choose before you count panels.
