Most 8–12 year old inverters fail at least three of these checks.

## Understanding Solar Inverter [Degradation](/glossary/degradation/) and Why It Accelerates After Year 7

Solar panels degrade slowly and linearly, typically 0.5–0.7% per year, meaning a 10-year-old panel still produces around 93–95% of its original rated output. Solar inverters age differently. Their degradation is non-linear and component-driven, particularly by electrolytic capacitors and insulated-gate bipolar transistors (IGBTs) that are sensitive to thermal cycling.

In India, rooftop conditions are particularly demanding. Ambient temperatures in central and western India reach 45 °C–50 °C from April through June. Inverters mounted outdoors or in poorly ventilated utility rooms see internal temperatures of 60 °C–70 °C during peak generation hours. Every 10 °C rise in operating temperature roughly halves the theoretical lifespan of electrolytic capacitors, a well-established principle in power electronics engineering, documented across [IEC 60068 environmental testing standards](https://www.iec.ch/homepage).

This thermal stress is why most Indian homeowners who installed systems in 2014–2018 are now noticing their first serious performance symptoms. The inverter that worked flawlessly for six years has reached the point where cumulative thermal cycling has measurably degraded its internal components. The question is whether that degradation is in the early or late stage.

The [solar inverter lifespan guide](/blog/solar-inverter-lifespan/) covers the full degradation curve in technical detail. For this article, the key takeaway is that year 7 is the natural inflection point where corrective action (whether repair, replacement, or upgrade) becomes worth evaluating formally.

| Age Band | Typical Condition | Recommended Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–5 years | Components within spec; minor calibration drift | Annual inspection only |
| 5–7 years | Early capacitor wear; occasional fault codes | Monitor closely; apply Health Check |
| 7–10 years | Significant capacitor and IGBT fatigue; frequent faults | Apply Health Check; replacement often warranted |
| 10–12 years | Late-stage thermal degradation; parts often discontinued | Strong replacement case; upgrade opportunity |
| 12+ years | Near end of rated life for most models | Replace; do not repair |

## The 7-Point Inverter Health Check (The Structured Framework)

### The 7-Point Inverter Health Check

This framework condenses seven independent diagnostic checks into a scored evaluation. Each check is binary: it either passes (score 0) or flags a problem (score 1). A total score of 2 or more points strongly supports replacement over repair.

1. **Generation decline percentage**: Pull 30 days of generation data from your monitoring app and compare it to the same 30-day period from installation year one (or year two, to account for initial system settling). Calculate the percentage difference, adjusting for any panels added or removed. If the decline exceeds 15% and panel soiling or shading has not changed, the inverter's conversion efficiency has degraded. Score 1 point.

2. **Fault code frequency**: Check the fault log in your monitoring system or the inverter's onboard display. If fault codes appear more than twice in any given seven-day period (regardless of whether the inverter self-recovers) the protection circuit is activating abnormally. Score 1 point.

3. **Capacitor degradation signs**: During evening hours when the inverter is in standby (not generating), place your hand 15 cm from the unit's vents. If the heat output is noticeably warmer than it was in year one, or if you can hear a higher-pitched hum or buzz compared to the original installation sound, internal capacitor equivalent series resistance (ESR) has risen. Score 1 point.

4. **Warranty status**: Is the original manufacturer's warranty still active? If the warranty has expired and the manufacturer no longer offers extended coverage for the model, you carry 100% of the financial risk on all future failures. Score 1 point.

5. **ALMM and BIS compliance of the original unit**: Check whether your inverter model appears on the current [MNRE ALMM list](https://mnre.gov.in/almm-list/). Models delisted or never listed may create complications when renewing net metering agreements or applying for PM Surya Ghar top-up subsidies. The [ALMM Phase III guide](/blog/almm-list-phase-iii-guide/) explains which certifications are mandatory. Score 1 point if the unit is not currently listed.

6. **Spare parts availability**: Contact the manufacturer's service centre and ask explicitly: "Are spare boards and IGBT modules available for my model number?" If the model has been discontinued for three or more years, spare parts availability is typically below 50% and declining. A repair today may be impossible six months from now. Score 1 point.

7. **Technology benefit gap**: Assess whether your original inverter type still matches your needs. If power cuts in your area have increased (common in tier-2 cities and rural feeders), or if you have added significant new loads since installation, the inverter technology may be mismatched to current requirements. An on-grid-only unit cannot provide backup power regardless of how well it is maintained. Score 1 point if a hybrid or higher-capacity unit would materially improve your situation.

Apply the check, tally the score, and use this decision table:

| Health Check Score | Interpretation | Recommended Path |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–1 | Inverter in reasonable health | Repair faults; schedule professional inspection |
| 2–3 | Moderate degradation; borderline case | Run repair-vs-replacement NPV (see Economics section) |
| 4–5 | Advanced degradation; replacement likely optimal | Get replacement quotes; plan within 6 months |
| 6–7 | End of useful life | Replace immediately; every month of delay costs yield |

## Check 1 in Depth: Measuring Generation Decline Against Your Baseline

The generation baseline is the most objective data point in the entire health evaluation. Most monitoring apps and DISCOM net metering portals store monthly generation records going back to installation. The calculation is straightforward.

**Step 1, Pull generation data.** From your monitoring app (Growatt ShinePhone, Sungrow iSolarCloud, Qbits WhatsApp monitoring, or a generic data logger), extract total kWh generated in the most recent full month where weather was similar to a historical reference period.

**Step 2, Normalise for irradiance.** Generation varies with weather. If possible, compare the same calendar month across years. June-to-June comparison is more reliable than June-to-December. If you have access to your local weather station data, adjust for any significant cloud-cover difference.

**Step 3, Calculate the decline percentage.** Subtract current monthly generation from baseline monthly generation, divide by baseline, and multiply by 100. A 5-year-old system showing 8% below baseline is within acceptable panel degradation range. The same system showing 18% below baseline has a problem that panels alone cannot explain.

**Step 4, Isolate the inverter.** Ask your installer to measure panel open-circuit voltage (Voc) strings individually. If panel output is within 5% of nameplate ratings but inverter AC output is proportionally lower, the efficiency loss is in the inverter's conversion stage, confirming a replacement case.

> **15–20%.** The generation decline threshold that typically indicates inverter conversion efficiency has fallen below economic justification for repair. *Source - [NREL National Solar Radiation Database technical documentation](https://nsrdb.nrel.gov/), 2025.*

Panel [degradation](/glossary/degradation/) accounts for 0.5–0.7% per year in normal conditions. A 10-year-old system should show roughly 5–7% generation decline from panel ageing alone. Any decline significantly beyond this range (especially when panels test healthy) points directly to the inverter.

## Check 2 in Depth: Fault Codes, Trips, and What They Actually Mean

Not all fault codes are equal. Some are transient (grid undervoltage during a momentary dip) and self-clear within seconds. Others are symptomatic of hardware failure. The [solar inverter troubleshooting guide](/blog/solar-inverter-troubleshooting/) maps the full fault taxonomy; for replacement decision purposes, focus on three fault families.

### Hardware Faults (Score Immediately)

IGBT overcurrent faults (commonly labelled F01, E02, or "overcurrent protection" depending on brand) indicate the switching devices are struggling to handle normal DC input levels. When these appear during normal-irradiance hours (not during the first 15 minutes after morning startup) the power stage is degrading. Three or more instances within 30 days is a replacement signal.

### Insulation Resistance Faults (Score Immediately)

Insulation resistance degradation faults (often labelled "ISO fault", "ground fault", or "insulation low") indicate moisture ingress or cable insulation failure. While the panels and DC cables should also be inspected, this fault appearing in an inverter older than 8 years often points to internal insulation breakdown in the DC bus or transformer windings. Do not repair-cycle these faults repeatedly without replacing the unit.

### Thermal Shutdown Faults (Score After Second Occurrence)

The inverter shuts down because internal temperature exceeded threshold. A single thermal shutdown during a 48 °C heatwave day may be acceptable. Thermal shutdowns occurring on days below 40 °C ambient, or recurring more than twice per month, indicate the thermal management system (fans, heat sinks, or internal thermal paste) has degraded. Fan replacement is a valid first repair; if shutdowns continue after fan service, the heat sink bonding has failed and replacement is the correct path.

The [solar inverter failure modes analysis](/blog/solar-inverter-failure/) provides a comprehensive taxonomy of failure modes and their typical repair costs, which feeds directly into the economics section below.

## Checks 3–4 in Depth: Capacitor Degradation and Warranty Status

### Why Capacitor Health Is the Hidden End-of-Life Indicator

Electrolytic capacitors are the component most sensitive to thermal cycling and age. Their job is to smooth the DC bus voltage, without them functioning correctly, the inverter produces a distorted output waveform that reduces generation efficiency and can damage sensitive grid-connected equipment.

The two field-observable signs of capacitor degradation are:

- **Increased standby heat**: A healthy capacitor has low ESR and dissipates minimal heat. As ESR rises with age, heat dissipation increases. An inverter that runs noticeably warm in zero-generation night hours is showing early capacitor ESR rise.
- **Audible hum change**: Electrolytic capacitors resonate at the AC mains frequency (50 Hz in India). As they degrade, the resonance amplitude increases, creating a louder hum. A capacitor hum that has become clearly audible from 1–2 metres away, when it was previously inaudible, is a reliable degradation indicator.

Professional capacitor replacement (replacing the main DC bus capacitor bank) costs ₹4,000–₹12,000 depending on the inverter model and capacitor specification. This repair is worth doing if the inverter is under 7 years old and all other Health Check points pass. For a unit scoring 3+ points overall, capacitor replacement is throwing good money after bad, the next component failure is typically 12–18 months away.

### Warranty Expiry: The Financial Risk Transfer Moment

When the manufacturer's warranty expires, every future failure cost transfers entirely to you. For a unit with active warranty, a fault trip is a phone call and a service ticket. For an out-of-warranty unit, the same fault is ₹8,000–₹25,000 out of pocket per incident.

The [solar inverter warranty guide](/blog/solar-inverter-warranty/) explains warranty structures in detail. The critical check for replacement decisions is whether an extended warranty is available for your model. Some manufacturers offer 2–5 year warranty extensions purchased at any point during the original warranty period; many do not offer extensions at all for discontinued models.

If the answer is "no extension available", the warranty check scores 1 point not just because you are unprotected today, but because the manufacturer's end-of-service signal is embedded in that answer. Manufacturers stop offering warranty support when the supply chain for spare parts becomes uneconomical to maintain.

## Checks 5–6 in Depth: ALMM Compliance and Spare Parts Availability

### ALMM Compliance: More Than a Paperwork Issue

The [ALMM list](/glossary/almm-list/) (Approved List of Models and Manufacturers) is maintained by MNRE and updated periodically. An inverter model can be listed, voluntarily withdrawn, or delisted for non-compliance with updated standards. For homeowners, the practical consequences of operating a non-ALMM unit are:

- **Net metering renewal risk**: When net metering agreements come up for renewal (typically every 5–10 years depending on state regulations), DISCOMs increasingly cross-check inverter compliance. A non-ALMM unit may be flagged, requiring you to replace the inverter anyway before reconnection is approved.
- **PM Surya Ghar subsidy ineligibility**: If you are considering a capacity expansion that qualifies for PM Surya Ghar subsidy, the replacement inverter must be ALMM-listed. A non-listed replacement means you forfeit ₹30,000–₹78,000 in central subsidy.
- **BIS certification gap**: Units installed before BIS IS 16221 became mandatory may not carry the current certification. While operation is not prohibited, the compliance gap affects resale value and insurance coverage for system damage.

The practical check: search for your inverter's model number on the [MNRE ALMM portal](https://mnre.gov.in/almm-list/). If it does not appear, this scores 1 point in the Health Check and becomes a strong argument for replacement at the next natural opportunity (fault occurrence, warranty expiry, or planned capacity upgrade).

### Spare Parts: The Discontinuation Signal

Contact the manufacturer's authorised service centre and request spare board availability for your specific model. Ask for: the main control board, the IGBT module or power board, and the main DC bus capacitor bank. Three responses are possible:

- **"In stock, 2–4 week lead time"**: Parts available; repair is viable if other checks pass.
- **"Available only through refurbished/third-party suppliers"**: Model is effectively discontinued; repair carries execution risk (incorrect part specification, no quality guarantee).
- **"Not available"**: Replace. No further analysis required.

Third-party spare boards for discontinued inverter models introduce a specific risk: the replacement board may not be tuned to the grid firmware of the original unit, potentially causing anti-islanding compliance gaps. This matters for net metering, an inverter failing its anti-islanding test after a board swap will be disconnected by the DISCOM until recertified.

## Check 7 in Depth: Technology Benefit Analysis and the Upgrade Decision

The seventh check asks a forward-looking question rather than a backward-looking diagnostic one: would a different inverter technology serve your needs materially better than a like-for-like replacement?

Two upgrade paths are worth evaluating when you are already replacing an old inverter.

### On-Grid Replacement (Same Technology)

A direct on-grid replacement makes sense when:
- Your grid is reliable (fewer than 1 hour of daily outages)
- Your electricity consumption profile has not changed significantly
- Panel capacity is unchanged
- Budget is the primary constraint

A like-for-like replacement delivers two immediate benefits: factory-fresh conversion efficiency (typically 97–98% peak, versus 91–94% for a degraded 10-year-old unit) and a fresh warranty period, resetting your risk exposure to zero.

### Hybrid Upgrade (Technology Advancement)

A hybrid upgrade (where the old on-grid inverter is replaced with a hybrid unit and battery storage is added, while existing panels are reused) makes sense when:
- Your area has more than 90 minutes of daily power cuts (data from your DISCOM or local RWA records)
- You have added significant new loads (EV charger, additional air conditioner, medical equipment)
- Diesel generator running costs exceed ₹25/unit in equivalent electricity
- You have roof space to expand panel capacity alongside the new inverter

The [on-grid vs hybrid decision guide](/blog/on-grid-vs-hybrid/) provides a full financial comparison. For the replacement context specifically, the incremental cost of upgrading to hybrid at replacement time (versus doing a second upgrade later) is typically ₹20,000–₹40,000, far less than the ₹55,000–₹80,000 cost of a second installation if done separately.

## The Repair-vs-Replacement Economics: Running the Real Numbers

Once the Health Check is complete, the financial comparison determines the action. The framework here is a simplified net present value (NPV) comparison over a 5-year horizon.

### Repair Scenario

- Repair cost (capacitors, control board, labour): ₹8,000–₹20,000
- Expected fault-free period post-repair: 12–30 months (based on unit age and failure pattern)
- Annual yield loss vs a new unit: 5–8% generation shortfall (degraded efficiency persists)
- Subsequent repair or replacement cost within 5 years: ₹15,000–₹55,000

Total 5-year cost of repair path: ₹35,000–₹85,000 in expenditure plus unrealised generation value.

### Replacement Scenario

- New inverter cost (3–5 kW, ALMM-listed, 12-year warranty): ₹28,000–₹55,000
- Installation cost: ₹3,000–₹8,000
- Total upfront cost: ₹31,000–₹63,000
- Recovery through restored generation (at ₹7.5/unit avoided tariff, recovering 15% of annual output): ₹6,750–₹11,250 per year
- Payback from generation recovery alone: 3–6 years
- Additional benefit: 12-year warranty with zero repair risk during that period

For a 5 kW system generating 7,000 kWh per year at ₹7.5/unit, recovering 15% of lost generation adds ₹7,875 annually. A ₹45,000 replacement investment pays back in under 6 years from generation recovery alone, before accounting for the warranty risk elimination value.

## Common Mistakes Homeowners Make When Replacing an Old Inverter

Replacing an inverter is a straightforward project when planned well and an expensive problem when it is not. These are the seven most common mistakes observed in the Indian market.

- **Buying on price alone**: Choosing the cheapest available unit without checking ALMM status or BIS certification saves ₹3,000–₹5,000 upfront but risks voiding net metering eligibility and PM Surya Ghar subsidy claims worth ₹30,000–₹78,000. Always verify the model on the [MNRE ALMM portal](https://mnre.gov.in/almm-list/) before purchasing.

- **Choosing the wrong capacity**: Replacing a 3 kW inverter with another 3 kW unit without re-evaluating current load growth locks in the same constraint for another decade. If consumption has grown 20–30%, upgrade capacity at replacement time.

- **Ignoring the [MPPT](/glossary/mppt/) count**: Older inverters often had single-MPPT inputs. If the existing panel array has mixed orientations or partial shading, a dual-MPPT replacement significantly improves generation from the same panels without any panel changes. The [MPPT guide](/blog/inverter-mppt/) explains the yield difference with real data.

- **Skipping the string compatibility check**: New inverters may have different DC input voltage ranges than the old unit. Confirming the existing string configuration is within the new inverter's MPPT window is mandatory before installation. An incompatible string that pushes voltage above the inverter's rated Voc maximum can damage the new unit within months.

- **Delaying notification to the DISCOM**: Replacing an inverter without notifying the DISCOM and updating the net metering registration creates a paperwork gap. Some DISCOMs have suspended net metering credits for homeowners caught with unregistered replacement units during routine inspections.

- **Choosing a brand without local service infrastructure**: A 12-year warranty is only valuable if the service network can actually reach you. Verify that the replacement brand has an authorised service partner within 50 km. The [inverter maintenance guide for India](/blog/inverter-maintenance-india/) covers service network evaluation criteria.

- **Missing the upgrade window**: Homeowners who replace like-for-like today and then decide to add batteries 18 months later pay a second installation cost. If hybrid is even a 30% possibility in the next 3 years, buying a battery-ready hybrid inverter now is almost always cheaper in total than two separate projects.
