## The Four-Layer Compliance Stack

South African solar sits under four separate approval layers, and a design tool that handles none of them leaves all four as manual review.

**NERSA licensing** applies at 1 MW and above. Below that, registration rather than licensing.

**NRS 097-2-1:2024** governs grid integration of embedded generation at low voltage: inverter protection settings, anti-islanding, export limits, and metering. Non-compliant inverter models are a common cause of application rejection, and incorrect protection settings are a common cause of failed inspection. SurgePV's [NRS 097-2-1 guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/south-africa/guides/nrs-097-2-1) breaks down the technical requirements.

**SANS 10142-1:2020** is the wiring code, covering DC and AC sizing, earthing, isolators, and labeling, with SANS 10142-2:2017 on earthing and bonding. A registered electrical contractor under the Department of Labour issues the Certificate of Compliance, and an unregistered contractor cannot issue a valid one. See the [Certificate of Compliance guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/south-africa/guides/certificate-of-compliance).

**Network operator approval** through SSEG registration, which must be obtained in writing before energizing. Pre-application notification is required before installation begins. Municipal procedures differ, and SurgePV maintains separate guides for [Cape Town](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/south-africa/municipalities/cape-town) and the other major metros.

The October 2025 policy update added an ECSA Professional Engineer requirement for systems above 25 kVA or on medium-voltage connections, covered in the [DoL versus ECSA sign-off guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/south-africa/guides/dol-vs-ecsa-sign-off). That threshold sits exactly where a lot of small commercial work lands, so it changes project economics for anyone quoting in the 25 to 50 kVA band.

## Why The Capacity Cap Changes The Design Question

> **What most South African quotes get wrong.** They design to the roof and the bill, then discover the 10 kVA single-phase limit at approval. The correct sequence is the reverse: establish the connection type and the network operator's limit first, then design within it.

The 10 kVA residential ceiling has a specific consequence. On a large house with high consumption, the array cannot be sized to cover the load, so the design question becomes which loads to cover and when. That is a battery and load-management question, not a panel-count question, and it needs an hourly model to answer.

It also means the export case is weak for most residential customers. With inverter capacity capped and export constrained in some zones, the return comes almost entirely from avoided import and from avoided outage. Our [solar plus storage design software](/blog/solar-plus-storage-design-software/) comparison covers the dispatch modelling, and [solar shading analysis software](/blog/solar-shading-analysis-software/) covers why module-level hourly resolution is the minimum standard for that kind of sizing.

The commercial picture is different and considerably better. Section 12B gives businesses a 125% first-year capital deduction on solar generation assets with no upper size limit. Combined with three-phase capacity headroom and daytime-aligned industrial load, C&I is where South African solar pencils most strongly. Because 12B is a deduction rather than a credit, its value depends on the buyer's tax position, so the model should show the effect at the customer's actual rate rather than a headline number.

## Worked Example: 8 kVA Residential Hybrid In Johannesburg

An illustrative calculation, not a specific customer project. Inputs: 8 kVA hybrid inverter within the 10 kVA single-phase limit, 9.6 kWp array, 1,750 kWh per kWp specific yield, 16,800 kWh generation, 14,000 kWh household consumption, import at R2.80 per kWh, 10 kWh battery.

| Line | Grid-tied only | Hybrid with 10 kWh battery |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Generation | 16,800 kWh | 16,800 kWh |
| Self-consumed | 6,720 kWh (40%) | 11,760 kWh (70%) |
| Exported or curtailed | 10,080 kWh | 5,040 kWh |
| Value of avoided import at R2.80 | R18,816 | R32,928 |
| Export value (constrained zone, nominal) | R0 | R0 |
| Year 1 energy benefit | R18,816 | R32,928 |
| Backup capability | None | Essential loads through typical outage |

In an export-constrained zone the grid-tied system throws away 60% of its generation for nothing. The battery converts half of that into avoided import and adds the backup capability that is often the actual reason the customer called. The economic case and the resilience case reinforce each other here in a way they do not in most markets, which is why hybrid dominates South African residential.
