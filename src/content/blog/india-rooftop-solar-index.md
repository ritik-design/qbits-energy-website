> **Direct answer.** India rooftop solar 2026 presents a two-speed market: six high-readiness states (Gujarat, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, Telangana, and Rajasthan) offer sub-35-day net metering, active state subsidies, dense installer networks, and 4–6 year payback at current tariffs. The remaining twelve states require 60–120+ day project cycles, thin installer ecosystems, and 7–9 year payback periods. The national picture is unambiguously positive: India added approximately 4.2 GW of rooftop solar capacity in FY 2025–26, with the residential segment accounting for 1.8 GW, driven largely by PM Surya Ghar Muft Bijli Yojana. Yet state-level variance is the dominant variable for any EPC operating across geographies. A 3 kW residential project in Ahmedabad generates a very different project economics and timeline profile than the same project in Patna. This report quantifies that variance across five dimensions.

## Why a Rooftop Solar Readiness Index Matters for Indian EPCs

The [solar EPC market in India](/blog/solar-epc-india/) has matured rapidly since 2020, but market intelligence has lagged behind on-the-ground complexity. Most published reports aggregate national figures (total MW added, total installations) which obscure the state-level friction that determines whether an EPC project cycle takes 21 days or 120 days. For an installer running 40–80 projects per month, that cycle time difference determines whether the business generates positive cash flow or requires working capital loans to survive.

The five dimensions of the Qbits Index were chosen because each one directly affects EPC project economics:

- **DISCOM net metering turnaround time**: controls when the customer's inverter switches to export mode and when the PM Surya Ghar subsidy clock starts ticking.
- **State subsidy availability**: determines whether the customer's net cost is ₹35,000 or ₹1,10,000 for a 3 kW system, which in turn drives demand conversion rates.
- **Installer density**: determines labour availability, subcontractor rates, and competitive margin levels in each geography.
- **Grid absorption capacity**: sets the ceiling on system sizes EPCs can propose without facing DISCOM rejection.
- **Tariff payback attractiveness**: the ultimate customer conversion lever; states with higher per-unit tariffs convert solar inquiries to signed contracts at higher rates.

> **4.2 GW.** Total rooftop solar capacity added in India in FY 2025–26, with 1.8 GW in the residential segment, making it the fastest residential solar growth year on record. *Source - [Mercom India Solar Market Leaderboard Q4 FY26](https://www.mercomindia.com/), 2026.*

The index does not score states on irradiance or climate, India's solar irradiance map is relatively uniform above 4.5 kWh/m²/day across most of peninsular and western India, making it a weak differentiator for EPC decision-making. The index focuses entirely on policy, infrastructure, and market factors that EPC teams can act on.

## The Qbits India Rooftop Solar Readiness Index: Methodology

### The 5-Factor Readiness Score

Each state is scored on five factors, each worth 20 points, for a maximum of 100. Scores are assigned based on field data from Qbits installation records, MNRE state-wise solar deployment reports, DISCOM annual reports, JMK Research state solar policy trackers, and industry body data from NSEFI.

| Factor | What is measured | Max score |
| --- | --- | --- |
| DISCOM net metering turnaround | Average working days from application to commissioning approval | 20 |
| State subsidy availability | Presence, quantum, and disbursement speed of state-level top-up subsidies | 20 |
| Installer density | ALMM-compliant empanelled installers per million urban population | 20 |
| Grid absorption capacity | Average feeder-level Solar Hosting Capacity (SHC) headroom, kW | 20 |
| Tariff payback attractiveness | Effective payback period at state upper-slab residential tariff | 20 |

**Scoring convention for net metering turnaround:**
- < 25 days → 20 pts
- 25–40 days → 15 pts
- 40–60 days → 10 pts
- 60–90 days → 5 pts
- > 90 days → 0–2 pts

**Scoring convention for tariff payback:**
- < 5 years → 20 pts
- 5–6 years → 16 pts
- 6–7 years → 12 pts
- 7–8 years → 8 pts
- > 8 years → 4 pts

The index is recalculated annually each June using data from the previous financial year plus Q1 of the current year. This edition uses FY 2025–26 + Q1 FY 2026–27 data.

## Top 5 States: High Readiness Markets

### Gujarat: Score: 87/100

Gujarat consistently leads India's rooftop solar market and retains the top position in 2026. GUVNL and the three distribution utilities (UGVCL, PGVCL, DGVCL) have built a genuinely digital net metering workflow. Median approval time as of Q1 FY27 is 21 days, the fastest in the country. The state runs the Suryashakti Kisan Yojana for agricultural feeders and a strong residential push under PM Surya Ghar, with some of the highest per-capita subsidy uptake nationally. Installer density is the second-highest in India at approximately 38 ALMM-empanelled firms per million urban population. The grid absorption capacity in Gujarat's urban feeders has been expanded through a five-year smart metering rollout covering 1.4 million meters. Upper-slab residential tariff of ₹5.50–₹6.50 per unit gives a payback of approximately 5.2 years for a 3 kW system.

**Gujarat score breakdown:**
- Net metering turnaround (21 days): 20/20
- State subsidy availability (active SKY + PM Surya Ghar): 18/20
- Installer density (38 per million): 17/20
- Grid absorption capacity (strong SHC headroom): 18/20
- Tariff payback (5.2 years): 14/20

### Karnataka: Score: 84/100

Karnataka's BESCOM has emerged as one of India's most technically advanced DISCOMs for solar integration. The state's Solar Rooftop Policy 2021–26 provides fast-tracked net metering approvals averaging 28 days, supported by a dedicated solar helpline and an online application portal. Bangalore's upper-slab tariff of ₹9.65 per unit gives Karnataka the single best tariff payback ratio in the country at approximately 4.2 years for upper-slab consumers, a conversion argument that essentially sells itself. The grid absorption capacity in BESCOM's urban feeders is well-supported by regular Hosting Capacity Analysis (HCA) updates. Karnataka's installer density leads the southern states.

### Maharashtra: Score: 79/100

Maharashtra is the largest rooftop solar market by installed MW in India, with MSEDCL processing an estimated 85,000 applications per year. Net metering turnaround averages 38 days, slower than Gujarat but within the range EPC businesses can plan around. The state offers a modest subsidy top-up under the Maharashtra Solar Policy for residential customers, and PM Surya Ghar uptake is high in Pune, Nashik, and suburban Mumbai. Grid absorption capacity in Mumbai (served by BEST and Tata Power) is the tightest in the country, limiting many proposals to 3 kW or 5 kW maximum without detailed feeder studies. Upper-slab tariff in MSEDCL territory averages ₹9.95 per unit, delivering a sub-5-year payback. The state scores somewhat lower on installer density outside the six major cities.

### Tamil Nadu: Score: 76/100

TANGEDCO has historically been the slowest southern DISCOM on net metering but has improved measurably since 2024. Median approval time is now 41 days, aided by the state's Solar Rooftop and Small Scale Generation Programme. Tamil Nadu's subsidy landscape combines PM Surya Ghar central funds with state-level TNEDA facilitation. Installer density in Chennai, Coimbatore, and Tirupur is high, though rural TANGEDCO feeders have constrained absorption headroom. Upper-slab residential tariff of ₹8.00–₹9.00 per unit sustains 4.8–5.4 year payback, making the state attractive for [solar EPC project development](/blog/inverter-tco/).

### Telangana: Score: 74/100

Telangana's TSSPDCL has emerged as a reformed performer, cutting median net metering turnaround from 68 days in FY24 to 36 days in FY26 following internal DISCOM restructuring. The state launched a dedicated Rooftop Solar Mission in early 2025 that provides empanelment fast-tracks for ALMM-listed installers. Hyderabad's upper-slab tariff exceeds ₹8.50 per unit, generating 5-year payback for residential customers. Grid absorption in Greater Hyderabad is adequate for systems up to 10 kW without feeder studies. The main limitation is installer density outside Hyderabad, rural Telangana lacks the subcontractor network depth of Tamil Nadu or Maharashtra.

## Bottom 3 States: Low Readiness Markets

### Bihar: Score: 38/100

Bihar presents the starkest contrast in the index. NBPDCL and SBPDCL process net metering applications in an average of 105 days (the slowest in the country) with multiple physical touchpoints required and no functional digital portal as of Q2 2026. The state has no active rooftop solar subsidy beyond the national PM Surya Ghar scheme, and ALMM non-compliance rates among local installers are high, disqualifying many projects from subsidy eligibility. Grid infrastructure in Bihar's rural feeders is not rated for net metering in large parts of the state. Retail tariff averages ₹6.00–₹7.00 per unit for upper-slab consumers, which together with slow commissioning and subsidy delays gives a real-world payback of 7.5–9 years. Installer density is the lowest in the country at under 4 ALMM-empanelled firms per million urban population.

**Bihar score breakdown:**
- Net metering turnaround (105 days): 0/20
- State subsidy availability (none beyond central): 6/20
- Installer density (< 4 per million): 5/20
- Grid absorption capacity (severely constrained): 6/20
- Tariff payback (8.5 years): 8/20, note: the tariff score is not zero because retail tariff has risen

### Jharkhand: Score: 41/100

Jharkhand's JSEB faces structural capacity constraints: net metering applications average 90 days, and the state has not published an updated Solar Hosting Capacity assessment for its feeders since 2022. The installer ecosystem is thin, with most ALMM-empanelled firms serving Ranchi and Jamshedpur only. PM Surya Ghar disbursement in Jharkhand takes 120–180 days on average after commissioning (the longest in the country) primarily due to JSEB's limited integration with the national PM Surya Ghar portal. Upper-slab retail tariff of ₹6.30 per unit gives a theoretical payback of approximately 7 years, but practical payback is closer to 8.5 years accounting for extended commissioning delays and grid curtailment events.

### Chhattisgarh: Score: 44/100

Chhattisgarh has one of India's lowest per-unit retail tariffs for residential consumers at ₹5.50–₹6.00 per unit, which limits tariff-payback attractiveness. Net metering applications average 82 days through CSPDCL, and the DISCOM has not digitised its solar application workflow. The state's installer density is thin outside Raipur and Bilaspur. On a positive note, Chhattisgarh has a high-irradiance climate averaging 5.2 peak sun hours per day (the highest of the three bottom-ranked states) which slightly reduces the system size required per kWh of generation. The state does not yet have an active rooftop solar subsidy programme beyond the national scheme. A Chhattisgarh-based EPC willing to invest in DISCOM relationship capacity can establish a durable first-mover position with limited current competition.

## National Trend Observations: India Rooftop Solar 2026

> **1.8 GW.** Residential rooftop solar capacity added in India in FY 2025–26, representing a 42% increase over FY 2024–25, the largest single-year residential solar addition ever recorded in the country. *Source - [JMK Research India Solar Annual Report 2026](https://jmkresearch.com/), 2026.*

Five structural shifts define the national trend picture in India rooftop solar 2026:

1. **PM Surya Ghar is compressing payback periods for upper-slab households.** The central subsidy of ₹30,000 for 1 kW, ₹18,000 for 2 kW increment, and ₹9,000 for 3 kW increment (maximum ₹78,000 for 3 kW) combined with [rising electricity tariffs](/blog/electricity-tariff-hikes-solar/) is pushing urban upper-slab payback below 5 years in 11 of the 18 states tracked in this index.

2. **ALMM Phase III compliance is becoming a de facto market gateway.** As of Q1 FY27, DISCOMs in Gujarat, Karnataka, Maharashtra, and Tamil Nadu are routinely rejecting net metering applications for non-ALMM inverters at the inspection stage. The [ALMM Phase III framework](/blog/almm-list-phase-iii-guide/) has moved from a regulatory requirement to a procurement filter that effectively determines which inverter brands participate in the PM Surya Ghar pipeline.

3. **DISCOM smart metering is the single biggest determinant of net metering speed.** States that have completed smart meter rollouts (Gujarat and Maharashtra in urban areas) process applications 3–4 times faster than states still operating electromechanical meters. The RDSS (Revamped Distribution Sector Scheme) funded infrastructure in 14 states is expected to close this gap by FY28, but EPCs should plan for the current disparity through at least FY27.

4. **Installer density is creating margin bifurcation.** In high-density states, commoditisation pressure is driving gross installer margins below 12% on standard residential projects. In low-density states (Bihar, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Assam) ALMM-compliant installers command 18–25% gross margins, but the slower working capital cycle and subsidy disbursement risk must be priced in.

5. **C&I rooftop demand is outpacing residential in eight states.** Factories, educational institutions, hospitals, and commercial buildings are driving above-average [on-grid solar adoption](/blog/best-on-grid-solar-inverter-india-2026/) in Rajasthan, Gujarat, Tamil Nadu, and Haryana, where commercial tariffs exceed ₹10 per unit and DG displacement ROI is compelling on timelines under 4 years. India's cumulative rooftop solar installed base crossed 30 GW in FY26, with C&I contributing roughly 55% of the total, according to the [MNRE Annual Report 2025–26](https://mnre.gov.in/) published in April 2026. For the complete national data set behind these figures, see [India Solar Statistics 2026](/blog/india-solar-statistics-2026-data/).

## Interpreting the Index: A Decision Framework for EPCs

The Qbits Index is designed to inform three categories of EPC strategic decision:

### Geographic Prioritisation

States scoring above 70 represent mature markets where EPC firms should compete on execution quality, ALMM compliance, and service SLA. In these competitive markets, tight [lead management](https://quickestimate.co/features/lead-capture/) and fast follow-up often decide who wins the deal, not just price. States scoring 50–70 represent transition markets where infrastructure is improving but EPC teams must budget 60–90 day project cycles. States scoring below 50 represent early-mover markets where the DISCOM relationship work and longer capital cycles are offset by higher margin potential and first-mover brand positioning.

### Inventory Planning

[Net metering turnaround time](/blog/how-to-apply-net-metering-india/) directly determines how long an inverter sits installed but uncommissioned, during which time the EPC has capital tied up in equipment, labour is complete, and the customer is waiting for grid approval. In Gujarat, this capital tie-up averages 21 days. In Bihar, it averages 105 days. For an EPC doing 50 projects per month, that difference represents approximately ₹85 Lakh to ₹2 Crore of additional working capital required, a material business risk for mid-size firms. Firms managing this exposure across many simultaneous projects often lean on dedicated [solar business software](https://surgepv.com/hub/solar-business/software-stack/) to track project cash flow state by state.

### Partner and Distributor Selection

The index state scores translate directly into distributor channel intelligence. A distributor covering Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, and Telangana has a fundamentally different sales cycle, inventory turn, and credit requirement profile than a distributor covering Bihar, Jharkhand, and Odisha. [Inverter distributor partners](/blog/inverter-suppliers-india/) serving low-index states should factor in 90–150 day working capital cycles rather than the 30–45 days typical of high-index states. EPCs evaluating expansion into a new state can also explore becoming a channel partner with an established turnkey installer network already active there.

## Grid Absorption Capacity: The Factor EPCs Underestimate

Of the five factors in the index, grid absorption capacity (also referenced in the literature as Solar Hosting Capacity (SHC)) is the least understood and most frequently overlooked by EPC teams until a project is rejected at the DISCOM inspection stage.

[Net metering](/glossary/net-metering/) regulations in India allow [DISCOMs](/glossary/discom/) to reject a rooftop solar interconnection application if the proposed system exceeds the feeder's SHC threshold. In practical terms, this means a 10 kW commercial system may be approved on one street and rejected on the next street in the same city, depending on which distribution transformer and feeder serves each consumer.

The [Central Electricity Authority](/glossary/cea/) published a Hosting Capacity framework in 2023 that requires state DISCOMs to calculate and publish SHC maps for their distribution feeders. As of Q2 2026, Gujarat, Karnataka, and Maharashtra have published partial SHC maps. The remaining fifteen states have not published updated SHC data, leaving EPC teams to navigate approvals empirically or through informal DISCOM contacts.

India's rooftop solar sector is expected to reach 100 GW of cumulative installed capacity by 2030 under MNRE's target framework, which requires scaling net metering connectivity from the current 2.1 million connections to approximately 15 million connections, a challenge that directly depends on DISCOM grid absorption improvements at feeder level, as noted by the [International Renewable Energy Agency in its India Country Report](https://www.irena.org/) published in March 2026. The [NSEFI](https://nsefi.in/) has separately estimated that DISCOM grid modernisation under RDSS could open an additional 18 GW of rooftop hosting capacity nationally by FY28.

Practical consequences for EPCs:
- **Size proposals conservatively in unknown feeder areas.** A 5 kW on-grid proposal is approved at significantly higher rates than a 10 kW proposal in states without SHC transparency.
- **Request a feeder load study before proposing above 7.5 kW** in states scoring below 60 on the index.
- **Use zero-export mode as a fallback.** [Zero-export inverters](/glossary/zero-export/) allow the installation to proceed even where export approval is delayed or denied, keeping the project viable while maintaining the customer's energy offset.

> **Only 3 of 18 states** tracked in the 2026 Qbits Index have published Hosting Capacity Analysis maps covering more than 50% of their urban distribution feeders, as required by the CEA 2023 framework. *Source - [CEA](https://cea.nic.in/), June 2026.*

EPCs that skip these steps experience significant financial stress in low-readiness markets.

## Common EPC Mistakes When Reading State Solar Data

State-level solar data published by [MNRE](/glossary/mnre/) and industry bodies is valuable, but several common misreadings lead EPCs to make sub-optimal resource allocation decisions:

- **Conflating cumulative installed base with current growth rate.** Maharashtra has the largest cumulative rooftop installed base but added fewer new MW in FY26 than Rajasthan. Installed base signals maturity; growth rate signals current opportunity.
- **Using average DISCOM turnaround rather than median.** In states with bimodal DISCOM performance (fast urban, very slow rural), the average turnaround masks the rural delay. Qbits Index uses median plus 80th percentile data to capture the distribution shape.
- **Ignoring subsidy budget exhaustion risk.** State-level subsidy top-ups are budget-capped and can be exhausted mid-year. EPCs in states with discretionary state subsidies (Kerala, Rajasthan) should track budget utilisation rates quarterly.
- **Overlooking [Performance Ratio](/glossary/pr/) and [Capacity Utilisation Factor](/glossary/cuf/) differences.** Two states with identical tariffs can have meaningfully different actual energy yields (and therefore payback periods) if one has significantly better irradiance or lower grid curtailment rates.
- **Treating published payback figures as guarantees.** [Payback period](/glossary/payback-period/) calculations are sensitive to actual tariff slab, system-level degradation, and local grid curtailment. Always present payback as a range to customers, not a single number.

## Where Qbits Fits in the India Rooftop Solar Market

EPC installers operating across multiple Indian states need an inverter partner whose product, compliance documentation, and service infrastructure match the demands of both high-readiness and low-readiness markets.
