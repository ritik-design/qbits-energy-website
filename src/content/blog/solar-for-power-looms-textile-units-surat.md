Solar for the textile industry in Surat is sized by the shift pattern, but the shift pattern does not set what each solar unit is worth. The GERC captive rules and the DGVCL tariff do that. A solar unit consumed inside the 07:00 to 18:00 window saves well below the headline energy rate, because a banking charge of Rs 1.10 to Rs 1.50 is taken back on every unit you use. A 24x7 unit and a single-shift unit therefore end up with similar paybacks per kilowatt. They differ on three other questions: how big a plant, what happens to your power factor, and what lint does to the roof.

This guide is for owners and managers of weaving sheds, air-jet and rapier loom units, processing houses and embroidery units in Surat who are about to ask an EPC (engineering, procurement and construction contractor) for a quote. It reads the regulation and tariff text, adds Surat yield data, and sets two paybacks side by side with every input shown. Nothing here comes from a Qbits customer, price or installed site, because we have none to cite. We also do not size the Surat textile industry, since no current primary figure turned up.

## Which tariff does solar for a Surat textile unit sit on?

The Gujarat tariff schedule splits low-voltage (LT) supply (contract demand up to 150 kVA) from high-tension supply (contract demand of 100 kVA and above). A small power loom unit is usually on the first. A larger weaving mill or processing house is usually on the second. The schedule's two ranges overlap between 100 and 150 kVA, so ask DGVCL which one your connection is billed under. Our [diamond polishing guide](/blog/solar-for-diamond-polishing-units-surat/) sets out the LT categories (Rate Non-RGP and Rate LTMD) line by line. This post reads the HT line, HTP-1, which is where most mills sit.

| Item | HTP-1 (HT, 100 kVA and above) |
| --- | --- |
| Demand charge per month | Rs 150 per kVA for the first 500 kVA of billing demand, Rs 260 for the next 500 kVA, Rs 475 above 1,000 kVA |
| Energy charge | 400 paise per unit up to 500 kVA of billing demand, 420 paise above that up to 2,500 kVA |
| Peak-hour addition, 07:00 to 11:00 and 18:00 to 22:00 | 45 paise per unit up to 500 kVA, 85 paise above |
| Discount, 11:00 to 17:00 | 60 paise per unit |
| Billing demand | Highest of actual maximum demand, 85% of contract demand, or 100 kVA |
| Supply-voltage rebate on energy charges | 1% at 11 or 22 kV, 1.5% at 33 or 66 kV |
| Power factor | Penalty below 90%, rebate above 95% (see the power factor section below) |

Source: GERC tariff schedule effective 1 April 2026, Rate HTP-1. Your bill adds FPPAS (fuel and power purchase price adjustment), electricity duty and other taxes, none of which are in the schedule.

Two further points. A unit that opts for HTP-IV, the night-only tariff for use between 22:00 and 06:00 at 225 paise per unit, has no daytime consumption for solar to offset, so rooftop solar does not apply to it. And the 60 paise discount on LT meters needs a meter that records time of day, which is worth checking on a power loom connection that has never needed one.

### Which GERC clauses matter for an HT textile mill?

A rooftop plant on a mill you own is a captive project under clause 9.2 of the GERC regulations, and net metering is permitted from 1 kW to 1,000 kW. Our [state-wise net-metering limits guide](/blog/net-metering-capacity-limits-commercial-by-state/) compares that cap with other states. Three clauses decide the maths for an HT textile unit. The table gives only those three; the diamond polishing guide linked above reads the whole of clause 9.2.

| Clause | Rule for HT supply | Effect on a textile unit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Same-day set-off (9.2.6) | A day's solar output is set off only against HT consumption between 07:00 and 18:00 on that same day, and the DISCOM buys whatever is left at the surplus rates | A mill shut on Sunday exports that day's output. A 24x7 mill never does |
| Surplus rate (9.2.9) | MSME manufacturing enterprises receive Rs 2.25 per unit for the first 5 years, then 75% of a GUVNL bid average. Other consumers receive 75% of that average | The current GUVNL average was not found, so the payback below leaves out a non-MSME surplus rate |
| Banking charge (9.2.11) | Demand-based consumers pay Rs 1.50 on each solar unit consumed. MSME manufacturing units, and consumers that are not demand-based, pay Rs 1.10 | The charge applies to every solar unit the mill uses, 24x7 or not |

Two other clauses apply in the background. On an LT demand-based connection (9.2.7) the set-off runs over the billing cycle, not the day, so a single-shift LT unit nets Sunday's output against weekday use. And clause 9.2.5 requires captive ownership and consumption to be proved each financial year to the Chief Electrical Inspector and the DISCOM, so keep those records.

The regulation text we read gives no definition of "MSME manufacturing" for these clauses. Weaving and processing are production activities, but DGVCL, not an EPC and not us, should confirm in writing which banking charge and surplus rate your Udyam registration and activity code attract. Clause 9.2 offers a commercial or industrial consumer no subsidy. If you prefer not to own the plant, clause 9.3 allows third-party sale, where a developer installs on your roof, under the same 07:00 to 18:00 window.

## How does the shift pattern change plant size for a power loom unit?

Only consumption between 07:00 and 18:00 can absorb solar in a textile unit. For a plant to be fully used, its output in the sunniest month must not exceed your in-window consumption in that month. The table applies that test to one illustrative load: 100 kW of average running load, three operating patterns, and Surat's March output after a 6% haircut for dust and downtime, which is about 149 kWh per kWp per month (explained in the yield section below).

| Operating pattern (illustrative) | In-window units a month | Plant that March output can fully absorb |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Single shift, 08:00 to 18:00, Monday to Saturday | 26,000 | About 174 kWp |
| Two shifts, 06:00 to 22:00, Monday to Saturday | 28,600 | About 191 kWp |
| 24x7, all days | 33,000 | About 221 kWp |

The in-window units are 100 kW times the hours the load runs inside 07:00 to 18:00 times the days. A second shift adds only about 10% to the ceiling, because its hours fall mostly outside the window. A 24x7 unit gains by running on Sundays. Night-shift consumption pays the 45 paise evening peak charge, but it cannot absorb any solar. Under HT, apply the same test day by day, because the set-off is same-day.

Three things that follow:

- **Size to the units you draw between 07:00 and 18:00, not to the roof or the total bill.** A unit that draws half its energy at night should expect a plant about half the size a total-consumption sum suggests.
- **Daytime humidification, chillers and compressors help.** Compressors for air-jet looms, humidification plant and cooling that run in daylight are your most solar-friendly loads. Log them by hour before you decide.
- **Orders move.** We have no current Surat order-book data and do not model one. Size to the load you run today, and if you are unsure, size smaller and add capacity later.

The same plant covers a smaller share of a 24x7 unit's bill than of a single-shift unit's. That share matters for power factor, as the section after the payback shows.

## Does a 24x7 or a single-shift unit get the better payback?

Both cases below are **illustrative**: no quote, no Qbits figure and no result from a real site. Swap in your own inputs.

| Input | Illustrative value | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Supply | HTP-1, 11 kV, MSME manufacturing unit | Typical mid-size mill |
| Plant | 100 kWp, the same on both units | Keeps the comparison to one variable |
| Unit A | 24x7, 100 kW running load | Fully absorbs the plant every day |
| Unit B | Single shift, Monday to Saturday, closed on Sundays | Sunday generation, 52 days of 365, is exported |
| Annual yield | 1,450 kWh per kWp, 145,000 kWh | PVGIS 1,550 less about 6% |
| Avoided energy rate | Rs 3.66 per unit | 75% of units at 340 paise (400 less 60, 11:00 to 17:00) and 25% at 445 paise (400 plus 45, 07:00 to 11:00) |
| Banking charge | Rs 1.10 per unit, with Rs 1.50 as a second case | MSME and non-MSME rates in clause 9.2.11 |
| Sunday surplus (Unit B) | Rs 2.25 per unit | The MSME surplus rate in clause 9.2.9 for the first 5 years |
| Other per-unit charges avoided | Rs 0, 1.50 and 3.00 per unit | Stand-ins for FPPAS and electricity duty. Read yours from the bill |
| Installed cost | Rs 35,000, 45,000 and 55,000 per kWp | Round numbers chosen to span a range, not market quotes |
| Running cost | 1.5% of installed cost a year | Cleaning, with more frequent washing assumed for lint and dust |
| Excluded | Demand-charge savings, supply-voltage rebate, power factor effects, tax depreciation, finance cost, degradation, inverter replacement | Not verified or case-specific |

Payback is installed cost divided by (units consumed times net saving per unit, plus units exported times Rs 2.25, less running cost). Unit A consumes all 145,000 units. Unit B consumes about 124,300 and exports about 20,700. The [100 kW plant cost post](/blog/hundred-kw-solar-plant-cost-factories-india/) lists the dated benchmarks behind round numbers like these and explains why we found no sourced 2026 market range.

| Installed cost per kWp | Other charges avoided: Rs 0 per unit | Rs 1.50 per unit | Rs 3.00 per unit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rs 35,000 (Rs 35 lakh for 100 kWp) | 11.0 / 11.2 years | 6.5 / 7.0 years | 4.6 / 5.1 years |
| Rs 45,000 (Rs 45 lakh) | 14.8 / 15.1 years | 8.6 / 9.3 years | 6.1 / 6.7 years |
| Rs 55,000 (Rs 55 lakh) | 19.0 / 19.5 years | 10.9 / 11.7 years | 7.6 / 8.4 years |

Each cell shows Unit A (24x7) first, then Unit B (single shift), at the Rs 1.10 banking charge. At Rs 1.50, the Rs 45,000 row becomes 18.3 / 18.2, 9.7 / 10.4 and 6.6 / 7.2 years.

Read it like this.

**The shift pattern moves payback by less than a year.** A Sunday unit is exported at Rs 2.25, against Rs 2.56 net when consumed at the MSME banking charge, so the closed day costs little. At the Rs 1.50 charge, the export is worth slightly more than a consumed unit. On an LT supply, where set-off runs over the billing cycle, Unit B would net Sundays and match Unit A. For a non-MSME unit the surplus is paid at 75% of a bid average we did not verify, and the gap could be wider.

**The other per-unit lines decide the answer.** On the energy schedule alone the payback is long, because Rs 3.66 less Rs 1.10 leaves Rs 2.56 a unit. Whether the bill's FPPAS and duty lines are levied on grid units, and at what level, moves the answer from about 5 years to about 19. Ask any EPC to rebuild the sum from your last twelve bills, line by line. The [C&I payback and IRR guide](/blog/solar-irr-payback-ci-india/) applies the same method to other tariffs.

**The shift pattern shows up in plant size and in the next section.** The plant a 24x7 unit can use is larger and the share of the bill it covers is smaller.

## Will solar hurt your power factor or cut your demand charge?

Solar lowers the kilowatt-hours a textile mill buys. It does not reliably cut the demand charge, and it can push power factor down. Your motors keep drawing the same reactive units, and a standard grid-tied inverter runs near unity power factor, so the monthly average power factor at your meter falls after solar goes in. The HTP-1 schedule charges 1% of the energy charges for every 1% (or part) that average power factor falls below 90%, down to 85%, and 2% for each point below 85%. It gives a rebate of 0.5% for each point above 95%.

This table is our own arithmetic on monthly totals, not a DGVCL figure. It keeps reactive units unchanged, assumes the units start at 0.95 power factor, and uses the payback example's loads.

| Case | Monthly load | Solar consumed | Solar share | Power factor after solar | Reading |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Unit A, 24x7 | 72,000 kWh | 12,083 kWh | 17% | 0.930 | No penalty. Any rebate above 0.95 is lost |
| Unit B, single shift | 26,000 kWh | 10,362 kWh | 40% | 0.878 | Penalty band. About 3% of energy charges, roughly Rs 1,900 a month at Rs 4.00 |

The figure for Unit B is about Rs 22,000 a year against a saving of about Rs 3 lakh, so it is not decisive. It is still avoidable, and it grows with the solar share. Ask three questions before signing: how DGVCL calculates average power factor at your meter, whether your existing capacitor bank is automatic and healthy, and whether the proposal includes the correction needed if power factor drops. Some inverters can also supply reactive power, but only where the exact datasheet says so. The options are laid out in our [reactive power guide](/blog/reactive-power-solar-inverters-india/). Check the datasheet of the model proposed, not the family name.

On LTMD, the schedule instead charges 10 paise for every reactive unit (kVARh). Solar does not change kVARh, so it neither saves nor costs anything there. At 0.90 power factor, that charge is about 4.8 paise per kWh consumed.

**Demand charge.** Billing demand under HTP-1 is never below 85% of contract demand. A unit with 400 kVA contract demand pays for at least 340 kVA a month, which is Rs 51,000 at Rs 150 per kVA, whatever solar does. Solar also cuts kVA by less than kW: a 400 kW load at 0.95 power factor is about 421 kVA, and removing 100 kW of it leaves about 328 kVA, a reduction of about 94 kVA, not 100. If your peak block falls at night or at a cloudy-hour start-up, solar will not touch it. Clause 11.7 says maximum demand is averaged over 30 or 15 minutes, or over 3 minutes if the consumer has parallel operation with the grid and has opted for it. Ask DGVCL what applies to you.

## What roof, lint and fire checks does a textile unit need?

Most textile units have large roofs, but not all of them suit panels. Walk through the checks below with the EPC before any layout is drawn.

| Roof type | What to check | What to ask for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| RCC slab | Waterproofing, parapet height, water tanks and stair headrooms that shade | Dead load of the structure per square metre, confirmed by a structural engineer, and a plan that avoids drilling through the waterproofing |
| GI or colour-coated sheet on steel purlins | Sheet age and corrosion, purlin spacing, wind uplift, every fixing as a potential leak above looms and fabric | Clamp-on fixings where the profile allows, a purlin load check, and a leak-test plan |
| Asbestos cement sheet | Old sheets are brittle and a person can fall through them. Drilling or cutting can release fibres | A written method statement, walkway boards, or a decision to replace the sheet before mounting |
| Saw-tooth (north-light) shed | Each tooth can shade the next row. Roof glazing and skylights should not be covered | A shadow study for December, not June, and a layout that leaves the daylight and ventilation as they are |

Read [how shading cuts generation](/blog/solar-panel-shadow-effect-generation/) before you accept a layout.

**Ventilation and humidity plant.** Weaving sheds depend on roof ventilators, exhaust fans and humidification returns. Panels must leave clear air paths and service access. Several independent MPPT (maximum power point tracking) inputs let you keep shaded and unshaded strings on separate trackers. The [QB 70/75/80/90/100/110KTLC-Pro](/our-products/QB-70-110KTLC-Pro/) is listed with 6 or 8 MPPTs by model and an IP66 enclosure. Check the current datasheet for the exact model.

**Lint and dust.** Cotton and synthetic lint, plus dust, settles on panels and mixes with humidity into a mat that rain does not always clear. We found no measured soiling figure for a Surat textile area, so the 6% haircut in this post is our assumption, not a measured loss. Log output against a cleaning schedule through your first dry season and set the washing interval from that data. Plan a water point and safe roof access at the design stage.

**Fire risk and cable routing.** Lint is combustible, and a roof full of DC cable is not a place for a pile of it. We know of no standard that sets a clearance distance for textile plants, so treat the following as design practice to put in writing with the EPC:

- Route DC cable in covered tray or conduit and keep it clear of places where lint collects.
- Use proper crimped connectors and check them at commissioning with a thermal image, because poor connectors are a common ignition point.
- Mount inverters in a clean, ventilated location, away from lint-laden shed walls, and keep their heat sinks clear.
- Add the plant to your housekeeping routine, tell your insurer, and confirm the plant's isolation and earthing arrangements with the Chief Electrical Inspector's approval.

**Processing units.** Dye houses and processing lines add steam, chemical vapour and humidity. Ask which coating and which fastener material the EPC proposes for the structure, and where the inverter sits relative to the vents.

## How much does a Surat roof yield, and which way should panels face?

PVGIS models a 1 kWp crystalline silicon system at Surat with 14% system losses and returns 1,550 kWh per year at a 15 degree south-facing tilt and 1,524 kWh at 10 degrees. These are modelled values from reanalysis data, not a measurement at your roof. PVGIS calculates its horizon from terrain, so it does not see the buildings next to you.

| Layout | Modelled kWh per kWp per year |
| --- | --- |
| South-facing, 15 degree tilt | 1,550 |
| South-facing, 10 degree tilt | 1,524 |
| East-west, 10 degree tilt (average of the two faces) | About 1,432, about 6% below south at the same tilt |

Low-tilt, east-west layouts suit flat roofs and some sheds because they pack more panels in the same area. Our [east-west design guide](/blog/east-west-roof-solar-design/) covers when that pays. The monthly shape matters as much as the annual total. At a 15 degree tilt, output runs about 155 kWh per kWp a month in March to May, about 98 kWh in June to September and 84 kWh in July. A plant sized on a yearly average makes about half as much in July as in March. The payback above uses 1,450 kWh per kWp a year, which is the 15 degree figure less about 6%.

## What are the DGVCL approval steps for a rooftop plant?

The Fourth Amendment (13 of 2024) to the GERC regulations sets a target time for each authority. These are regulatory targets, and no one guarantees them for your file.

| Step | Authority | Time limit in the regulation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Registration and certificate | GEDA or the designated agency | 5 days from a complete application |
| Approval of single line, earthing and wiring diagrams | Chief Electrical Inspector | 10 days |
| Technical feasibility report | DISCOM, then the field office | 3 days to request, 10 days for the field report |
| In-principle consent or estimate | DISCOM head office | 2 days after the report |
| Connectivity agreement | You and the DISCOM | Within 15 days of consent, or 30 days if system strengthening is needed |
| Project completion | You | 6 months from the letter |

For an HT consumer, system strengthening charges apply above 6 kW and up to 1,000 kW. State-owned DISCOMs recover them per kVA, as approved for new HT load. We did not look up the current figure. The feasibility report also checks whether total rooftop capacity on your transformer is within its rating, and clause 6.2 requires the plant capacity to stay aligned with the connection rules in the Supply Code, so ask DGVCL how that applies to your contract demand. Our [net-meter approval timeline](/blog/net-meter-approval-timeline/) covers how long approval takes in practice.

## What should a textile unit bring to an EPC?

1. Twelve bills, with the demand, energy, time-of-day, power factor, FPPAS and duty lines visible.
2. An hourly profile of a normal week, split between 07:00 to 18:00 and the rest, from your meter data or a logger.
3. Contract demand, supply voltage, tariff category and DGVCL's written position on MSME banking and surplus rates.
4. A roof drawing with sheet type, age, purlin spacing, neighbouring building heights and every ventilator and skylight.
5. A request that the proposal state the banking charge, the avoided rate by time band, the power factor effect and its fix, and the lint-cleaning plan.

## Is rooftop solar worth it for a textile unit?

For a textile unit with a steady daytime load, solar can make sense in Gujarat. The banking charge and the per-unit lines on your bill set the payback, not the yield and not whether you run one shift or three. The shift pattern does set the plant size, the Sunday export on HT, and how far power factor falls. Qbits supplies grid-tied string inverters, quoted per model and quantity.
