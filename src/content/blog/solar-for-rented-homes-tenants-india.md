Tenants account for approximately 30% of India's urban households - an estimated 70 million rented households in cities and towns. Most of these renters pay electricity bills of ₹1,500 to ₹5,000 per month, often at commercial tariffs if the meter is in the landlord's name. Yet the common assumption is that solar is only for property owners.

That assumption is wrong. Tenants in India have more solar options in 2026 than most people realise - from portable plug-in systems that require no permission to full rooftop installations negotiated into the lease. The path depends on your tenure, your landlord's cooperation, and your state's net metering rules.

> **Direct answer.** Tenants in India can access solar in four ways: portable solar systems (no permission needed), balcony-mounted panels (reversible installation), landlord-tenant solar agreements (where the landlord owns the system and the tenant gets cheaper electricity), or negotiating ownership rights in the lease. PM Surya Ghar subsidy goes to the electricity meter holder - negotiate subsidy sharing with your landlord before installation begins.

This guide maps every option available to Indian tenants and renters, with the negotiation language and policy references needed to make it happen.

## Why Tenants Need Solar Options

The barriers to tenant solar are real but not insurmountable. Understanding each barrier - and its workaround - is the starting point:

**Barrier 1: Property ownership**
Solar panels mounted on a roof create a permanent fixture in law. A tenant installing fixtures without permission can face lease termination or damage claims. The workaround: written landlord consent or portable systems that are not fixtures.

**Barrier 2: Electricity meter in landlord's name**
Most rental properties in India have the electricity meter in the landlord's name. The tenant pays the landlord a utility charge, which may or may not reflect actual grid consumption. Under this structure, [net metering](/glossary/net-metering/) credit flows to the landlord, not the tenant. The workaround: negotiate the tariff benefit into the lease, or get the meter transferred to the tenant's name.

**Barrier 3: PM Surya Ghar subsidy eligibility**
The [PM Surya Ghar](/glossary/pm-surya-ghar/) subsidy of ₹30,000–₹78,000 goes to the electricity consumer - not the property owner by default, but the meter holder. In most Indian rental situations, this is the landlord. Read the [PM Surya Ghar eligibility guide](/blog/pm-surya-ghar-eligibility/) to understand the exact criteria. The workaround: negotiate that the subsidy benefit is passed to the tenant as a rent credit or direct payment.

**Barrier 4: Uncertainty about tenancy duration**
Solar systems pay back in 5–7 years for a typical residential installation. A tenant with a 1-year lease has no incentive to invest. The workaround: lease extension clauses, portable systems, or landlord-ownership models where the tenant benefits from day one without capital investment.

## Option 1 - Portable Solar Systems for Tenants

The simplest solar option for a tenant requires no permission, no installation, and no DISCOM application. Portable solar systems are consumer electronics - like a phone charger - and can be used in any rental home.

### What a Portable Solar System Includes

- **Solar panel(s):** 100–400 Wp foldable or rigid portable panels. No mounting - lean against a wall or place on a balcony.
- **Portable power station:** A battery-inverter unit that stores solar energy and provides AC outlets and USB ports. 200–2,000 Wh capacity.
- **Connection:** A simple MC4-to-DC cable from panel to power station. Plug-in, no electrician required.

| System | Panel | Battery | Best for | Price (India) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Portable entry | 100 Wp panel | 200 Wh station | Phone, fan, lighting | ₹12,000–₹18,000 |
| Portable mid | 200 Wp panel | 500 Wh station | Laptop, fan, small TV | ₹22,000–₹35,000 |
| Portable premium | 400 Wp + 2 panels | 1,000–2,000 Wh | Fan + laptop + lighting all day | ₹45,000–₹80,000 |

**Limitations:** Portable systems cannot power air conditioners or large appliances. They are best for critical backup (phones, fans, lights) and reducing grid bill for small loads. No DISCOM connection - no net metering benefit.

**Best suited for:** Tenants who pay ₹500–₹2,000/month on electricity and want independence from power cuts without any installation complexity.

## Option 2 - Balcony Solar (Plug-In Solar India)

Balcony solar - called "plug-in solar" or "balkonkraftwerk" in Germany where it is mainstream - is growing in India. The system mounts panels on balcony railings or window sills using non-penetrating clamp mounts, and plugs directly into a household outlet.

### How Balcony Solar Works in India

1. Panels (typically 1–2 × 400 Wp) mount on balcony railing clamps. No drilling, no permanent attachment.
2. A micro-inverter converts DC power from the panels to AC.
3. The output plugs into a standard household socket.
4. The electricity flows into the home's wiring, powering nearby appliances automatically.
5. A small smart metre shows generation.

**Legal status in India (2026):**
Balcony solar systems below 1 kW connected through a household socket are not subject to DISCOM net metering regulations because they do not export to the grid - they simply reduce import. They are consumer electronics, not grid-connected solar installations. No NOC, no DISCOM approval, no landlord permission is legally required for the installation itself (though informing the landlord is advisable).

> **Balcony solar opportunity.** Germany legalized "Balkonkraftwerk" plug-in solar in 2023, triggering a market of 500,000+ installations within 18 months. India's urban apartment market - with millions of south-facing balconies - represents a similar opportunity for tenant solar without the installation and approval barriers. *Source - [IEA Renewable Energy Global Status Report 2024](https://www.iea.org/reports/renewables-2024){target="_blank" rel="noopener"}, 2024.*

**Practical considerations for Indian balcony solar:**
- Most Indian apartments have balconies with south or west-facing orientation - suitable for solar.
- A 400 Wp balcony panel generates approximately 1.5–2 kWh/day - enough to power a ceiling fan for 6–8 hours.
- The system offsets approximately ₹180–₹220/month of electricity at ₹8–10/unit tariff.
- Full payback on a ₹20,000–₹25,000 balcony system: 8–12 years at this offset rate.

## Option 3 - Landlord-Tenant Solar Agreement

The most financially significant option for a tenant who wants meaningful solar capacity (1–3 kW) is a landlord-tenant solar agreement - where the landlord owns the system and the tenant benefits from lower electricity costs.

### The Landlord-Tenant Solar Agreement Framework

A well-structured agreement benefits both parties:

**For the landlord:**
- Increased property value (solar-equipped rental homes command a 5–10% premium in urban markets).
- PM Surya Ghar subsidy eligibility (₹30,000–₹78,000 for a 1–3 kW system).
- Long-term maintenance of the property as a premium rental.

**For the tenant:**
- Reduced electricity costs from day one.
- Power backup during grid outages.
- No capital investment.

| Agreement Term | Landlord-Owns Model | Tenant-Installs Model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who pays for installation? | Landlord | Tenant |
| Who owns the system? | Landlord | Tenant (with permission) |
| Who gets PM Surya Ghar subsidy? | Landlord (meter holder typically) | Tenant (if meter in tenant's name) |
| Tenant electricity benefit | Reduced bill via lower tariff or rent offset | Direct bill reduction |
| What happens when tenant leaves? | System stays | Tenant removes or sells |

**Negotiating the landlord-owns model:**

The pitch to the landlord:
1. The landlord invests ₹1–2 Lakh (after subsidy) in a 2 kW system.
2. The tenant signs a 3-year lease at a slightly higher monthly rent (₹500–₹1,000/month to contribute to payback).
3. The tenant gets electricity at the solar rate (₹4–5/unit) instead of the grid rate (₹8–10/unit).
4. At the end of the tenancy, the landlord owns an appreciating asset that increases rental value.

This model is gaining traction in Pune, Bangalore, and Hyderabad rental markets where tech-sector tenants are aware of solar benefits and willing to pay a green premium. ## Option 4 - Negotiating PM Surya Ghar with Your Landlord

PM Surya Ghar Muft Bijli Yojana provides subsidies of ₹30,000 for the first kW, ₹30,000 for the second kW, and ₹18,000 for the third kW (maximum ₹78,000 for 3 kW). For a tenant whose landlord owns the meter, here is how to negotiate:

**Script for tenant negotiation with landlord:**

*"Under PM Surya Ghar, you as the meter holder can get a ₹78,000 government subsidy on a 3 kW solar system. The system will cost approximately ₹90,000 after subsidy. It generates electricity worth ₹6,000–₹7,000 per year (600 free units). I'll commit to a 3-year lease renewal if we install solar - and I'll pay ₹500/month extra rent to contribute to the remaining cost. You get a 3-year committed tenant, a paid-off solar asset in 10 years, and a higher-value property."*

This framing aligns the landlord's incentive (property value, tenant commitment) with the tenant's incentive (lower bills, backup power).

## Common Mistakes Tenants Make with Solar

- **Installing a permanent system without landlord consent** - mounting panels on the roof without written permission creates legal exposure and can result in lease termination or forced removal of the system.
- **Not clarifying subsidy ownership before installation** - many tenants invest in a solar discussion with the landlord only to discover the landlord claims the PM Surya Ghar subsidy as the meter holder, without sharing the benefit with the tenant.
- **Choosing a permanent system for a short lease** - a 1-year tenant installing a ₹1.5 Lakh on-grid system will not recover the investment before moving. Portable or balcony systems are better for short-tenure rentals.
- **Not getting the ownership agreement in writing** - verbal agreements about who owns the solar system at end of tenancy are routinely disputed. Document ownership in the lease addendum with the exact clause: "Solar system installed at [address] remains the property of the tenant and may be removed by the tenant at end of tenancy."
- **Ignoring the net metering meter transfer** - a tenant who installs solar with the meter in the landlord's name loses all net metering export benefit to the landlord. Transfer the meter to the tenant's name before applying for net metering.

## Planning Your Solar Exit - What Happens When You Move

A tenant who has invested in solar - whether portable, balcony-mounted, or a permanent installation with landlord consent - needs a clear exit plan before committing. The transition plan determines whether the investment pays off or becomes a dispute.

### Portable Solar Kit: Clean Exit

A portable solar kit is the simplest transition. The kit consists entirely of movable equipment - panels, battery, cables - that packs into cases or bags and moves to the next rental. Reinstallation at the new property takes one afternoon. No permission at the new property is required. The entire investment travels with you. This is why portable is the preferred starting point for any tenant who is not certain about long-term tenure.

### Balcony Panel with Reversible Mount: Straightforward Exit

Balcony clamp-mount systems leave no permanent marks. Remove the brackets, take the panel, and restore the balcony to original condition in under an hour. The panel and microinverter or battery unit move to the next rental. At the new property, check balcony orientation and railing strength before reinstalling. South-facing or west-facing balconies work best in India.

### Permanent System with Landlord Consent: Exit Requires Negotiation

For a permanent grid-connected or hybrid solar system installed with the landlord's written consent, the exit requires advance planning:

- **If the lease states tenant owns the system** - negotiate with the landlord at least 3 months before vacating. Options: sell the system to the landlord at depreciated value, transfer it to the next tenant (if the next tenant agrees and takes over the maintenance obligation), or remove it (verify who pays for removal and roof restoration).
- **If the lease is silent on ownership** - courts in India have generally treated structural improvements as becoming part of the property, which means the landlord benefits at end of tenancy without compensation to the tenant unless otherwise agreed. Avoid this scenario by getting ownership terms documented before installation.
- **If the system has outstanding loan** - inform the lender before vacating. Most solar loans are secured against the system as collateral. Transfer the loan or pay it off before leaving the property.

> **Document early, dispute never.** Every solar dispute between a tenant and landlord in India traces back to a verbal understanding that was not written down. A one-page lease addendum covering system ownership, removal rights, and what happens on early termination eliminates 95% of disputes before they begin. *Source - [MNRE Rooftop Solar Guidelines](https://mnre.gov.in/){target="_blank" rel="noopener"}, 2024.*

### Transition Checklist for Tenants Moving with Solar

**6 months before moving:**
- Review lease addendum for ownership terms.
- Notify landlord if system is to be removed.
- Get written confirmation of removal rights.

**3 months before moving:**
- For permanent systems: get a service engineer to assess reinstallation feasibility at the new property.
- For portable: verify the new property has suitable outdoor space and orientation.
- For systems with loans: contact lender about loan transfer or prepayment.

**On vacating day:**
- Take photographs of the roof or balcony before and after removal.
- Get written acknowledgement from the landlord that removal was done to their satisfaction.
- Keep all inverter warranty documents, manufacturer registration, and service contact details - they follow the system, not the property.
