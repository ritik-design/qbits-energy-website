The UAE is four solar markets sharing a currency. DEWA credits Dubai customers at their own slab rate with indefinite rollover. Abu Dhabi's ADDC and AADC give kWh offset only, no cash, at up to 5 MW per premises. EtihadWE in the Northern Emirates issues credits that expire every December. Sharjah's SEWA has no published distributed solar programme at all. > **The configuration requirement.** Most platforms let you set a country. The UAE needs you to set a utility, because the credit mechanism, the capacity ceiling and whether credits expire are all utility-level properties. A tool with one national tariff field cannot represent the market.

## What Is Genuinely UAE-Specific

**Four credit mechanisms, not one.** Covered above. The DEWA arrangement is unusually generous because crediting at the consumer's own slab means a high-consumption villa earns upper-slab value on exported units, which rewards sizing to the roof. The EtihadWE arrangement does the opposite.

**Credit expiry as a sizing input.** Under EtihadWE, calendar-year credits expire at year-end, so oversizing generates summer surplus that is written off in December. A tool that treats all UAE credit as rolling will recommend the same system in Ras Al Khaimah as in Dubai, and one of those recommendations wastes energy annually for the life of the system.

**Abu Dhabi's tariff categories.** The roughly four to one spread between subsidised nationals and expatriates means the same array has completely different economics depending on who occupies the building. The uncomfortable but honest consequence is that solar for a subsidised national household in Abu Dhabi does not pay back on economics alone within a reasonable horizon, and the right software lets you demonstrate that rather than papering over it.

**ECAS and approved lists.** ECAS certification is mandatory, alongside IEC 61215, 61730, 62109, 62116, 61727 and 60255. Dubai maintains an approved equipment list and Abu Dhabi requires both the design consultant and installation contractor to be listed.

**Scope 2 reporting.** The Climate Law, Decree-Law 11 of 2024 effective May 2025, requires it with fines from AED 50,000 to 2 million. For corporate buyers this is frequently the actual driver.

**Flat roofs and dust.** Parapet self-shading and heavy soiling dominate the technical picture. Our [solar shading analysis software UAE](/blog/solar-shading-analysis-software-uae/) guide covers both.

## Worked Example: 300 kW Commercial, Dubai Versus Northern Emirates

An illustrative calculation, not a specific customer project. Inputs: 300 kW DC, 1,750 kWh per kW specific yield, 525,000 kWh generation, 380,000 kWh self-consumed, 145,000 kWh exported, commercial tariff at 44 fils per kWh.

| Line | Dubai, DEWA | Northern Emirates, EtihadWE |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Generation | 525,000 kWh | 525,000 kWh |
| Avoided import at 44 fils | AED 167,200 | AED 167,200 |
| Exported | 145,000 kWh | 145,000 kWh |
| Credits realised | 145,000 kWh, rolls indefinitely | ~95,000 kWh, remainder expires |
| Export value realised | AED 63,800 | AED 41,800 |
| Year 1 benefit | AED 231,000 | AED 209,000 |

The AED 22,000 annual gap comes entirely from credits expiring unused at the calendar year end. A proposal copied from a Dubai template shows AED 231,000 in both columns and is wrong by roughly 10% in one of them, every year, for the life of the system.

The design response matters as much as the number. On the EtihadWE profile the correct answer is either a slightly smaller array or load shifting to raise self-consumption, and neither recommendation is available to a team whose software does not know that credits expire.
