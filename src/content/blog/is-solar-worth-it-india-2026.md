## The Solar Decision Test: 5 Yes/No Questions

This five-question framework is the fastest diagnostic for whether solar makes financial sense in your specific situation. Answer each question honestly. The scoring is at the end.

### The Solar Decision Test: 5 Yes/No Questions

1. **Do you own the roof?**: Net metering registration, DISCOM connection, and PM Surya Ghar subsidy are all issued to the property owner. Tenants face insurmountable legal hurdles without the owner's active participation. *Yes = 1 point.*

2. **Is your monthly electricity bill above ₹3,000?**: At ₹3,000/month (roughly 350–400 units in most states), a 3 kW system pays back in under 7 years. Below ₹2,000/month, the required system is so small that installation economics rarely justify the project. *Yes = 1 point.*

3. **Will you stay in the property for 5+ years?**: Solar is a 25-year investment with a 5–7 year payback. Selling before payback means either negotiating the asset value into the sale price or absorbing the sunk cost. *Yes = 1 point.*

4. **Do you have usable south- or west-facing roof area with minimal shade?**: A 3 kW system requires approximately 24–30 sq ft of shadow-free roof space. Heavily shaded rooftops (surrounded by tall buildings), north-facing rooftops, and roofs with water tanks or satellite dishes covering the primary face significantly reduce viable capacity. *Yes (even partial) = 1 point.*

5. **Is your grid relatively stable, or do you accept that on-grid solar provides no backup?**: On-grid solar (the most common and subsidy-eligible type) produces zero power during grid outages. If you experience more than 2–3 hours of daily load-shedding, you need a hybrid system, which costs 30–40% more. *Yes (either stable grid or budget for hybrid) = 1 point.*

**Scoring:**
- 5/5, Solar is strongly recommended. Proceed to get multiple quotes.
- 4/5, Solar is worth exploring. Identify which "No" applies and evaluate its impact.
- 3/5, Solar may work but requires careful system design. Get a detailed site assessment.
- 2/5 or below, Solar is unlikely to be financially optimal at this time. Revisit in 2–3 years or when the limiting factor changes.

## Who Solar Is Definitely Worth It For

The ideal solar buyer in India in 2026 has a specific profile. The characteristics below are ranked by financial impact.

- **Bills above ₹5,000/month**: Each rupee of monthly bill is a rupee that solar can eliminate. Larger bills mean larger viable systems and faster payback.
- **Own home in a city or tier-2 town with functional DISCOM net metering**: Gujarat, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, Telangana, and Rajasthan DISCOMs are relatively responsive; net metering approval is typically 30–60 days.
- **5+ year ownership horizon**: Long enough to see payback and begin the pure-savings phase.
- **Air conditioner user**: AC is typically 40–60% of a household electricity bill in summer. Solar generation peaks precisely when AC usage peaks, a natural match that maximises self-consumption.
- **Awareness of tariff trends**: India's residential electricity tariffs have risen at 5–8% per year for the past decade. Each tariff increase makes your solar savings larger without any change to the system. A system sized for today's tariff automatically becomes more valuable as tariffs rise.

## Who Solar May Not Be Ideal For

Solar is not universally the right investment. Several situations make it financially unattractive or logistically impractical.

### Tenants

A tenant cannot obtain net metering in their own name, cannot claim PM Surya Ghar subsidy (issued to the DISCOM connection holder, the landlord), and cannot take the system when they move. The few situations where tenants and landlords co-invest in solar with written agreements are workable but require careful legal documentation.

### Very Low Electricity Bills

If your monthly bill is ₹1,000–₹1,500, the system you need is 1–1.5 kW. At this size, the per-kW installation cost is actually higher due to fixed overheads (DISCOM application, mounting structure, inverter minimum), and the payback period extends to 8–10 years. The economics improve significantly only when bills rise or when tariff hikes occur.

### Heavily Shaded or North-Facing Rooftops

A roof that faces north, or that is completely surrounded by higher buildings, will generate 40–70% less than an ideal south-facing roof. No amount of quality equipment compensates for poor solar access. A professional [site survey](/glossary/site-survey/) with shade analysis is the minimum due diligence before any investment decision.

### Power-Cut-Prone Areas Without Budget for Hybrid

If you live in an area of UP, Bihar, or interior Maharashtra with 4–8 hours of daily load-shedding and you want solar to help during outages, you need a hybrid system with a battery, which costs ₹4–5 lakh for a 3 kW configuration. If that budget is not available, an on-grid-only system will disappoint because it shuts down during every power cut. See [on-grid vs hybrid vs off-grid](/blog/on-grid-vs-hybrid-vs-off-grid-decision-guide/) for the full comparison.

## What Happens After Payback: The Pure Savings Phase

The discussion of solar "worth" typically stops at payback. The more important question is what happens after payback.

A 3 kW system in Karnataka that pays back in 5.5 years:

- **Years 1–5.5**: Savings accumulate to match the capital investment (₹2.5 lakh post-subsidy)
- **Years 5.5–12**: Savings continue (approximately ₹4,000–5,000/month) with no further capital outlay other than annual cleaning and occasional maintenance. Total: ₹3–4 lakh in pure return.
- **Year 12**: Inverter warranty expires. Budget ₹40,000–₹60,000 for a new inverter if needed.
- **Years 12–25**: Panels continue generating at 88–92% of original capacity. Total return in this phase: ₹5–8 lakh at 2026 tariffs (higher if tariffs continue rising, as they historically do).

**Total financial return over 25 years** (conservative): ₹8–12 lakh on a ₹2.5 lakh post-subsidy investment. That is a 3.2–4.8x return, comparable to a well-performing equity mutual fund but without market risk.

## Common Objections and Honest Responses

**"Solar is too expensive."**
Post-subsidy, a 3 kW system costs ₹2.2–3 lakh. Many buyers finance it through home improvement loans or NBFC solar loans at 10–12% per year. At ₹4,000/month savings and a ₹12,000/month EMI on a 3-year loan, the net monthly cash outflow during the loan period is only ₹8,000, and the loan ends 2 years before the system pays back on full savings.

**"My building is too old for solar."**
Structural suitability depends on roof construction, not building age. A structural engineer can evaluate load-bearing capacity for ₹2,000–₹5,000. Most concrete rooftops in Indian construction can handle solar mounting loads. The bigger concern for older buildings is electrical wiring, the distribution board should be upgraded if it dates from pre-2000 construction.

**"My neighbour's system underperformed."**
This is often an installer quality issue, not a solar technology issue. Underperforming systems typically have oversized inverters (so they never operate at efficient MPPT range), ALMM non-listed panels with higher degradation, or poor DC cable sizing that creates resistive losses. See [solar inverter buying mistakes](/blog/solar-inverter-buying-mistakes/) for the checklist that prevents this.
