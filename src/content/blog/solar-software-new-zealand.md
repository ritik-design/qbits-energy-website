New Zealand quietly became one of the more demanding solar design markets on 1 July 2026. Since that date the four largest retailers, holding roughly 87 percent of connections between them, must offer a plan where the export rate varies through the day. The rule sets the shape of the price and explicitly not its level, so retailers place the peaks where they choose and there is no floor. The peaks generally fall in the evening, when a solar array produces nothing. A tool holding one flat buyback rate cannot describe that market, and the households it quotes will end up exporting into the trough and buying their evening energy at retail. This guide covers what the New Zealand stack has to do.

## What Is Genuinely New Zealand-Specific

**The peaks fall when the array is asleep.** This is the central design fact. On a time-varying plan the highest rates are generally paid in the evening, and solar arrays generate nothing then. An array without storage exports overwhelmingly into the midday trough and imports at retail through the evening peak.

**The advertised rate is usually unreachable.** A headline "up to 40 cents" is a peak-hour figure. A proposal applying it to total export overstates the return substantially, and the honest output is an effective achieved rate calculated from exported volume by time of day.

**A two to eight fold spread between plans.** From around 5 cents at the low end among majors to peaks reaching 40 cents in winter on some plans. That range makes the plan choice a larger lever than most hardware decisions.

**No feed-in tariff, so no floor.** Every rate is a commercial term and can change. A twenty-year projection built on today's peak rate carries a disclosure obligation, and it argues for prioritising self-consumption value, which is insulated from retailer decisions.

**Keep the hierarchy straight.** Direct self-consumption avoids retail import, which generally exceeds any export rate. The battery's best job is covering the household's own evening demand; exporting into the peak is what it does with the remainder. The exception is unusually high peak plans where the ordering can flip for part of the evening, and only an hourly model finds the crossover.

**AS/NZS 4777.2 and AS/NZS 5033,** shared with Australia, which is convenient for trans-Tasman operators.

## Worked Example: The Trap The July Change Created

An illustrative comparison, not a specific customer project. A 6 kW array, household with modest daytime and strong evening consumption, three configurations.

| | A: flat 17c plan, no storage | B: time-varying plan, no storage | C: time-varying plan with storage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Midday surplus | Exported at 17c | Exported into the trough | Stored |
| Evening household demand | Imported at retail | Imported at retail | Served from battery |
| Evening export | None | None | Residual into the peak |
| Export value captured | Moderate and predictable | Poor | Strong |
| Suits | Households that cannot add storage | Nobody, on this load profile | This household |

Column B is the trap. A household moved onto a time-varying plan without storage is worse off than on a decent flat plan, because all its export lands in the trough while it buys evening energy at retail. The headline peak rate is real and entirely unreachable.

Column C is what the plan structure is designed to reward, and it is why plan advice and storage advice cannot be separated. An installer who recommends the time-varying plan and leaves the battery for a later conversation has actively made their customer worse off, which is a genuinely new failure mode in this market and one worth briefing your sales team on.
