A 3 kW rooftop solar system is benchmarked at about **₹1,45,000 before subsidy**, and an eligible household receives **₹78,000** of central assistance against it. Both numbers come from the PM Surya Ghar scheme's own published formula rather than a seller's website.

The more useful fact about 3 kW is structural. This is exactly where central financial assistance stops. Assistance pays 60% of benchmark cost up to 2 kW, then 40% of the additional cost for the 2 kW to 3 kW slice, and then nothing. The fourth kilowatt earns zero.

That single cap explains why 3 kW dominates Indian residential rooftop. It is the last size where every watt you add still pulls money back from the scheme.

This guide shows what 3 kW physically is, how much it generates with the calculation exposed, the benchmark arithmetic and what you are left paying, the component cost structure as percentage shares, the Domestic Content Requirement trade-off, the on-grid against hybrid decision, and what happens economically the moment you cross the cap.

## What a 3 kW solar system actually is

A 3 kW system means 3 kilowatt-peak (**kWp**) of rated direct-current module capacity, measured under standard test conditions. It is a nameplate rating and not an hourly output. The number describes the array, not the inverter, and the two are usually not identical.

At the 540 Wp to 590 Wp module wattages common in the Indian residential market, 3 kWp is 5 or 6 panels. This is an industry-observed range, not a specification. Five 600 Wp modules make 3.0 kWp exactly, while six 550 Wp modules make 3.3 kWp, so real systems often carry a nameplate a little above the round number.

Panel area alone is roughly 13 m² to 15.5 m². That is derived from typical module dimensions near 2.28 m by 1.13 m, so about 2.58 m² per panel.

You cannot build on panel area. Layouts need walkway access, a tilt gap behind each row, and clearance from parapets and water tanks. Plan for **22 m² to 30 m² of shadow-free roof**, which is roughly 235 to 325 sq ft.

Shadow-free is the harder constraint. Series-wired modules are limited by their weakest member, so one water tank shadow crossing one panel can drag down the whole string for part of the day.

## How much a 3 kW system generates: a worked example

Annual output follows one formula: rated capacity multiplied by daily peak sun hours, multiplied by 365, multiplied by the **performance ratio** (the share of theoretical output surviving real-world losses). Below is the calculation with every input stated. Treat it as illustrative arithmetic, not a site estimate.

| Input | Value used | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rated capacity | 3 kWp | The system under discussion |
| Peak sun hours | 4.5 per day | A mid-range Indian value; real sites run roughly 4 to 6 |
| Performance ratio | 0.75 | Covers temperature, soiling, wiring, and inverter losses |
| Days | 365 | Full year |

**Worked example:** 3 kWp × 4.5 peak sun hours × 0.75 = **10.125 kWh per day**, about 10.1 units. Over a year that is 10.125 × 365 = **3,696 kWh**, or roughly 308 units a month.

That 308 figure matters. PM Surya Ghar was designed around households consuming up to 300 units a month, and the Press Information Bureau release describes a 3 kW system for exactly that profile (2024). The scheme cap and the typical Indian household load were matched deliberately.

Change one input and the answer moves a long way. At 5.5 peak sun hours the same array yields about 4,517 kWh a year. At a 0.68 performance ratio from heavy soiling or partial shade, it falls to about 3,351 kWh. Any installer quoting a generation number should show you these three inputs on request.

## Why central assistance stops at exactly 3 kW

The cap is written into the formula, not applied afterwards. Central financial assistance is 60% of benchmark system cost for capacity up to 2 kW, plus 40% of the additional cost for the 2 kW to 3 kW slice, capped at 3 kW. The published slabs are ₹30,000 at 1 kW, ₹60,000 at 2 kW, and ₹78,000 at 3 kW and above, according to the Press Information Bureau Cabinet release on PM Surya Ghar (2024).

Read the last slab carefully. It says 3 kW **and above**. A 6 kW household system receives the same ₹78,000 as a 3 kW one.

Two further details from the [MNRE residential CFA guidelines](https://mnre.gov.in/en/notice/operational-guidelines-for-implementation-of-the-component-central-financial-assistance-to-residential-consumers-of-pm-surya-ghar-muft-bijli-yojana/) change how the cap behaves in practice:

1. **Assistance is computed on rated DC module capacity**, and is provided irrespective of inverter size. So a 3.3 kWp array paired with a 3 kW inverter is assessed on the 3.3 kWp, then capped.
2. **Special-category states use different rates**, ₹33,000 and ₹19,800 in the same guidelines. The cap structure is the same; the money is not.

The guidelines also set a 15-day clock after distribution company approval and a 30-day clock for grievances, and they oblige registered vendors to provide a 5-year Comprehensive Maintenance Contract from commissioning. Ask to see that contract as a document, not as a verbal assurance.

There is one behavioural fingerprint of the cap worth noting. As at 20 March 2026 the scheme had installed 26.21 lakh rooftop systems totalling 9.56 GW, according to the Ministry of New and Renewable Energy. Dividing those two published figures gives an average system of about 3.65 kW, which is arithmetic rather than a quoted statistic. Households cluster at and slightly above the cap, which is exactly what you would expect when marginal assistance drops to zero at 3 kW.

For current slab amounts and the application sequence, our [PM Surya Ghar subsidy amount guide](/blog/pm-surya-ghar-subsidy-amount/) tracks the published position, and slabs do change.

## The benchmark cost of 3 kW, and what you are left paying

Most published 3 kW prices trace back to undated vendor listings. The scheme benchmark is different, because the formula and its outputs are both published, so the benchmark can be recovered by arithmetic.

Our [1 kW price guide](/blog/1kw-solar-system-price-india/) works that derivation through in full. The short form is that ₹30,000 as 60% of benchmark at 1 kW implies **₹50,000 per kW** for the first 2 kW, and the ₹18,000 step from the 2 kW to the 3 kW slab as 40% of the additional cost implies **₹45,000** for the third kilowatt.

This is derived arithmetic from the published PIB (2024) formula. It is not a quoted MNRE price, and it is a whole-system benchmark covering modules, inverter, structure, cabling, labour, and commissioning rather than a component price.

| Line | Arithmetic | Amount |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First 2 kW at benchmark | 2 × ₹50,000 | ₹1,00,000 |
| Third kW at benchmark | 1 × ₹45,000 | ₹45,000 |
| Benchmark system cost | ₹1,00,000 + ₹45,000 | ₹1,45,000 |
| Assistance on first 2 kW | 60% × ₹1,00,000 | ₹60,000 |
| Assistance on third kW | 40% × ₹45,000 | ₹18,000 |
| Total central assistance | ₹60,000 + ₹18,000 | ₹78,000 |
| Benchmark net to consumer | ₹1,45,000 − ₹78,000 | ₹67,000 |

Assistance therefore covers about 53.8% of benchmark cost at 3 kW. No other residential size gets a larger absolute transfer from the central scheme.

Financing sits next to this number. Collateral-free loans were available at the repo rate plus 50 basis points, which was 5.75% per annum at that date, for tenures up to 10 years, according to the Ministry of New and Renewable Energy's March 2026 Lok Sabha reply. The rate floats with the repo, so confirm the current figure with the lender. Secondary sources quoting roughly 7% are usually individual lender products, not the scheme rate.

The same March 2026 material records that MNRE waived the technical feasibility requirement, introduced auto load enhancement up to 10 kW, folded the net metering agreement into the National Portal application, and runs a grievance call centre on 15555 in 12 languages.

## What a market retail kit tells you, and what it does not

Retail kit listings and installed system quotes are different products, and confusing them is the most common error on this keyword. A kit is boxed hardware. An installed system includes mounting, electrical protection, labour, commissioning, and utility work.

Two market-observed data points. The [Waaree official store](https://shop.waaree.com/) displayed a Radiance Lite 3 kW on-grid kit with five 600 W to 615 W modules at ₹1,21,499, and a Radiance 3.2 kW single-phase bifacial DCR kit at ₹1,43,499, on 23 September 2026.

| Reference | Amount | What it is | What it does not include |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Benchmark, whole system | ₹1,45,000 | Derived from the PIB (2024) formula | Nothing; it is a full-system reference |
| Retail kit, 3 kW class | ₹1,21,499 | Market-observed listing, 23 Sep 2026 | Installation, roof work, approval scope |
| Retail kit, 3.2 kW class | ₹1,43,499 | Market-observed listing, 23 Sep 2026 | Installation, roof work, approval scope |

The gap is the lesson. The whole-system benchmark sits about 19% above the cheaper kit listing, and that difference is broadly the cost of turning boxes into a commissioned, metered installation. A quote that matches a kit price for a full installation is either excluding scope or running an unusually thin structure specification.

These are two specific retail products on one date. They are not a market price band, and they prove nothing about warranty, stock, delivery, or scheme eligibility. Recheck the box contents at checkout.

## What the 3 kW price is built from

Fixed rupee line items go stale within months and vary by city, roof, and component tier. Percentage shares of system cost are more durable. The table below is an industry-observed indicative split and not a quotation for any specific product.

| Cost component | Indicative share of system cost | What moves it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solar modules | 45% to 55% | Cell technology, Domestic Content Requirement status, brand tier |
| Inverter | 10% to 15% | On-grid against hybrid, monitoring features, warranty terms |
| Mounting structure | 8% to 12% | Galvanising grade, roof type, elevated or ballasted design |
| Cabling and protection | 6% to 10% | Cable run length, earthing, surge protection, isolators |
| Labour and commissioning | 8% to 12% | Site access, floor height, local wage rates |
| Net metering and paperwork | 3% to 6% | Distribution company fees, meter cost, liaison effort |

At 3 kW the module block dominates, so module choice moves the total more than anything else. The bottom three rows are close to fixed in absolute rupees, which is why 3 kW beats 1 kW on cost per kilowatt in the real market even though the scheme benchmarks the third kilowatt lower.

Converting every bid to rupees per watt is the fastest way to compare unlike quotes. Our guide on [reading an inverter rate properly](/blog/solar-inverter-ka-rate/) sets out that method, including the traps in per-watt comparison.

## DCR against non-DCR at 3 kW

**Domestic Content Requirement (DCR)** means cells and modules manufactured in India rather than assembled here. Subsidy-linked residential systems generally have to use DCR modules, and that carries a real premium you will see in the quote.

DCR TOPCon modules traded at about ₹22.3 per Wp against about ₹14.2 per Wp for non-DCR TOPCon, according to the JMK Research module price tracker (April 2026). Those are market-observed ex-works module prices, not installed system prices.

Apply that to 3,000 Wp. DCR modules are about ₹66,900 of module cost against about ₹42,600 for non-DCR, a gap of roughly ₹24,300.

Set that against ₹78,000 of central assistance and the decision is not close. DCR wins by roughly ₹53,700 at this size. The margin is wider than at 1 kW, because assistance scales up to the cap while the module premium scales with array size at a lower rate per rupee of subsidy.

The picture inverts only if you are ineligible or not applying. A non-DCR system can be cheaper up front and will not carry central assistance.

One correction worth carrying into vendor conversations. Inverters do not need ALMM listing for PM Surya Ghar. ALMM List-I covers modules and List-II covers cells; MNRE publishes no inverter list. Vendor claims about an inverter being on "List-II" are simply wrong.

## On-grid against hybrid at 3 kW, and the battery problem

An **on-grid** system exports to the utility and uses net metering to offset imports. It gives you no power during an outage, because anti-islanding protection deliberately shuts the inverter down to protect line workers. That is a safety requirement rather than a product limitation.

A **hybrid** system adds a battery and dedicated backup circuits. At 3 kW the battery becomes the dominant cost driver above the array, and it can approach or exceed the combined cost of the inverter, structure, and cabling. That is an industry-observed pattern, not a quoted figure.

| Consideration | On-grid at 3 kW | Hybrid at 3 kW |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Backup during an outage | None | Only on designed backup circuits |
| Main cost driver above the array | Inverter and metering | Battery bank |
| Central assistance | Applies to the eligible grid-connected system | Storage treatment is scheme-dependent |
| Distribution board work | Minimal | Backup circuit segregation required |
| Ongoing replacement cost | Inverter, eventually | Battery, on its own cycle life |

The energy budget is the honest test. A 3 kW array producing roughly 10.1 kWh on a good day has to serve daytime loads, export, and battery charging from the same pool. Size the backup around the loads you genuinely need during a cut, then check whether 3 kW can refill that store on a cloudy day.

## The myth that 3 kW is the best value per kilowatt

Here is the tradeoff nobody selling 3 kW systems mentions. On absolute rupees, 3 kW is unbeatable, because ₹78,000 is the largest transfer the central scheme makes. On net cost per kilowatt at benchmark, 3 kW is actually the worst of the three subsidised sizes.

| Size | Benchmark gross | Central assistance | Net at benchmark | Net per kW |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 kW | ₹50,000 | ₹30,000 | ₹20,000 | ₹20,000 |
| 2 kW | ₹1,00,000 | ₹60,000 | ₹40,000 | ₹20,000 |
| 3 kW | ₹1,45,000 | ₹78,000 | ₹67,000 | ₹22,333 |
| 4 kW (extrapolated) | ₹1,90,000 | ₹78,000 | ₹1,12,000 | ₹28,000 |

The 4 kW row extrapolates the ₹45,000 incremental kilowatt benchmark beyond the cap, because MNRE publishes no benchmark above 3 kW. Treat it as an illustration of the cap's shape, not a scheme figure.

Net cost per kilowatt rises at 3 kW because the third kilowatt is subsidised at 40% instead of 60%. So why does 3 kW still win for most homes? Three reasons, in order of weight.

1. **The benchmark's flat ₹50,000 per kW does not model real fixed costs.** The net meter, the approval work, the earthing, the surge protection, and the labour mobilisation are near-identical at 1 kW and 3 kW. In the market, those fixed costs fall hard per kilowatt as you scale up, which is why the scheme itself benchmarks the third kilowatt lower.
2. **3 kW matches the load the scheme was built around.** About 308 units a month against a design target of up to 300 units is a close fit. A 1 kW system addresses roughly a third of that.
3. **It is the last size where marginal assistance exists at all.** Above 3 kW the marginal subsidy rate is zero, not 40%.

A 3 kW system does not promise a zero electricity bill either. Fixed charges, night-time consumption, and monsoon months all persist, and your distribution company's settlement rules decide how much of your export you actually recover.

## What happens economically above the cap

The moment you cross 3 kW you buy capacity at full price. On the extrapolated benchmark, moving from 3 kW to 4 kW takes your out-of-pocket cost from about ₹67,000 to about ₹1,12,000. That is roughly 67% more money for 33% more capacity.

That does not make larger systems wrong. It makes them a different decision, judged on cost per unit generated rather than on subsidy.

Three cases genuinely justify going past the cap:

1. **Consumption well above 300 units a month.** Air conditioning, water heating, or an electric vehicle can push a household past what 3 kW can offset.
2. **A sanctioned load that supports it.** MNRE's March 2026 changes introduced auto load enhancement up to 10 kW, which removed one common obstacle to larger residential systems.
3. **Roof area you will never use otherwise.** Marginal structure and cabling cost on an already-mobilised site is lower than a second project later.

Three cases do not justify it. A belief that subsidy scales beyond 3 kW, a vendor pushing capacity you cannot consume, and an export arrangement your distribution company has not approved in writing.

If your consumption points upward, price the [5 kW system](/blog/5kw-solar-system-price-india/) beside 3 kW on the same dated scope, and read the [net metering guide](/blog/net-metering-india-complete-guide/) before you assume the surplus is worth its nameplate.

## How GST sits on a 3 kW quote

Two rates collide inside one rooftop quote, which is why two vendors can show different tax on the same hardware. Renewable energy devices sit at 5% under Schedule I entry 437, while heading 8504 static converters sit at 18% under Schedule II entry 477, per Notification 9/2025-Integrated Tax (Rate) dated 17 September 2025 and effective 22 September 2025.

The subsidy does not help here. Section 15(2)(e) of the CGST Act excludes government subsidies from the value of supply, so ₹78,000 of central assistance does not reduce the GST on your invoice. Tax is charged on the contract value, and the assistance arrives separately after commissioning.

Ask for tax shown line by line with HSN codes, since Notification 78/2020-Central Tax dated 15 October 2020 governs HSN digits on invoices. A single tax-inclusive total hides which rate was applied to what. Our [GST on solar guide](/blog/gst-on-solar-inverters-2026/) covers the composite supply treatment and the worked tax arithmetic.

## A worked payback example with stated assumptions

This is arithmetic, not a forecast, and not a promise of any outcome. Paybacks move with your tariff, your actual generation, and your distribution company's settlement rules.

**Stated assumptions:** generation of 3,696 kWh a year from the earlier worked example; central assistance of ₹78,000; an avoided tariff of ₹8 per kWh, which you must replace with your own slab rate from your distribution company's tariff order; a ₹3 per kWh surplus rate in the partial-offset cases.

| Case | Gross cost | Net after ₹78,000 | Annual value | Simple payback |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| At benchmark, full offset | ₹1,45,000 | ₹67,000 | ₹29,568 | 2.3 years |
| At a higher quote, full offset | ₹1,80,000 | ₹1,02,000 | ₹29,568 | 3.4 years |
| At benchmark, partial offset | ₹1,45,000 | ₹67,000 | ₹24,024 | 2.8 years |
| At a higher quote, partial offset | ₹1,80,000 | ₹1,02,000 | ₹24,024 | 4.2 years |

The partial-offset rows assume 70% of units offset at ₹8 and 30% exported at ₹3. That is 2,587 × 8 plus 1,109 × 3, which gives ₹24,024.

The spread is the point. The same system pays back in 2.3 years or 4.2 years depending on two assumptions you control by asking. The table also excludes annual degradation, cleaning, an eventual inverter replacement, and tariff revisions, so the honest range is wider than four rows suggest.

## Why two honest 3 kW quotes differ, and what to ask

Quote-to-quote variation at this size is mostly structural rather than margin games. Six drivers explain most of the gap between honest bidders.

1. **Module tier and DCR status.** The largest single swing, worth roughly ₹24,300 at 3,000 Wp on the JMK Research April 2026 module prices alone.
2. **Nameplate creep.** A 3.3 kWp array is 10% more modules than a 3.0 kWp array, and both get sold as "3 kW".
3. **Structure specification.** A hot-dip galvanised elevated structure costs more than a light surface frame and survives coastal air far better.
4. **Cable run length.** A 40 m roof-to-meter distance needs heavier conductors than a 10 m run to hold voltage drop inside limits.
5. **Scope of approval work.** Some quotes include liaison, meter charges, and inspection follow-up. Others exclude all of it.
6. **Tax treatment.** Two bids can look 15% apart purely on whether tax and delivery are inside the headline figure.

Take these questions to every vendor meeting and get the answers in writing.

1. What exact module model and wattage, what total kWp, and is it DCR-compliant?
2. What is the exact inverter model, its rated AC output, and its direct-current input window?
3. What is the estimated annual generation, and what peak sun hours and performance ratio produced it?
4. Is the quoted figure before or after assistance, and what is the gross contract value?
5. What structure material and galvanising grade, and what is the roof waterproofing scope?
6. Who files the application, handles inspection, and arranges the net meter, and which charges are excluded?
7. What are the module, inverter, and workmanship warranties, stated separately with their terms?
8. Is the 5-year Comprehensive Maintenance Contract attached as a document?

Vendor empanelment gates the whole subsidy, so verify it before paying an advance. Our [empanelled vendor checklist](/blog/empanelled-vendor-pm-surya-ghar/) covers how to confirm it on the national portal.

## The Bottom Line

The defensible 3 kW anchor is a benchmark near ₹1,45,000, derived from the scheme's published 60% and 40% formula, leaving about ₹67,000 after ₹78,000 of central assistance. Market quotes above that have to justify themselves line by line.

The structural finding is the cap. At 3 kW you collect the largest transfer the central scheme makes, and every kilowatt after it is bought at full price. That is why 3 kW became the default residential size, and why the published average system sits just above it.
