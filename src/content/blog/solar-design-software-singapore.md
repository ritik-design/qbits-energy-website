Singapore solar has a decision most markets do not: which export scheme the customer sits under. SCT pays a fixed rate near S$0.20 per kWh and settles simply through SP Services. ECIS pays the variable USEP wholesale rate, typically 10 to 20 cents per kWh, requires EMC Market Participant registration, and caps at 10 MWac per installation. One is predictable, the other is exposed to wholesale price movement and can only be estimated properly with an hourly generation profile matched against a price curve. Add a 1 MWac licensing threshold that adds three to six months of EMA processing, and Singapore rewards design software that can model the specific commercial arrangement rather than a generic export assumption.

## SCT Versus ECIS, And Why It Is A Modelling Question

| Scheme | Rate | Registration | Cap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| SCT | Approximately S$0.20 per kWh, fixed | SP Services direct | None stated |
| ECIS | USEP variable, typically 10 to 20 cents per kWh | EMC Market Participant | 10 MWac per installation |

> **What most Singapore proposals get wrong.** They quote ECIS at an average USEP figure and present it as though it were a fixed rate. Averaging a variable price against a variable generation profile produces a number that is right only by coincidence, because the two are correlated. Solar exports most at midday, and midday USEP is not the daily average.

SCT is the simpler product and the honest default for customers who want certainty. At roughly S$0.20 per kWh it also sits above the low end of the USEP range, so it is not obviously the worse commercial choice. ECIS makes sense where the customer has the appetite and the administrative capacity for Market Participant registration and believes wholesale prices will favour them.

The design consequence is that ECIS customers need hourly modelling and SCT customers do not. A tool that only produces annual totals can serve an SCT customer adequately and cannot serve an ECIS customer at all. SurgePV's [SCT guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/singapore/guides/sct) and [ECIS guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/singapore/guides/ecis) cover the registration mechanics for each.

## Licensing, Standards And The Roof Constraint

**The 1 MWac threshold.** Below it, no EMA Generation Licence. At or above it, a full licence through the ELISE portal at elise.ema.gov.sg, estimated at three to six months. On a project sitting near the boundary that timeline is a genuine reason to design just below it, and the design tool should make the AC rating visible rather than burying it. See SurgePV's [EMA licensing guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/singapore/guides/ema-licensing).

**Licensed Electrical Worker.** Required to submit the Electrical Installation application on every system regardless of size.

**Standards.** SS 638 governs wiring. Inverters need IEC 62109-1 and 62109-2 plus anti-islanding to IEEE 1547 or IEC 62116. Modules need IEC 61215 or 61646 for performance and IEC 61730 for safety.

**Roof scarcity.** This is the practical constraint that shapes Singapore design work. Usable rooftop is limited, frequently shaded by adjacent towers, and often already carrying plant. Inter-building shading from high-rise neighbours is a first-order effect rather than an edge case, which raises the value of accurate 3D site modelling and module-level shading well above what it would be in a market with open roof. Our [solar shading analysis software](/blog/solar-shading-analysis-software/) guide covers why module-level hourly resolution matters, and [3D solar design software](/blog/3d-solar-design-software/) covers the site modelling side.

JTC also imposes mandatory deployment obligations on certain industrial premises, covered in SurgePV's [JTC solar guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/singapore/guides/jtc-solar), which creates a class of project driven by requirement rather than return.

## Worked Example: 800 kWp Industrial Rooftop, SCT Versus ECIS

An illustrative calculation, not a specific customer project. Inputs: 800 kWp DC, 720 kWac, 1,250 kWh per kWp specific yield, 1,000,000 kWh generation, 70% self-consumed, 300,000 kWh exported, import at S$0.28 per kWh.

| Line | Under SCT | Under ECIS |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Self-consumed | 700,000 kWh | 700,000 kWh |
| Avoided import at S$0.28 | S$196,000 | S$196,000 |
| Exported | 300,000 kWh | 300,000 kWh |
| Export rate | S$0.20 fixed | USEP variable, modelled hourly |
| Export revenue | S$60,000 | S$45,000 to S$60,000 |
| Year 1 total | S$256,000 | S$241,000 to S$256,000 |
| AC rating vs 1 MWac threshold | 720 kWac, no licence | 720 kWac, no licence |

Two things stand out. First, self-consumption dominates in both cases, so the scheme choice affects a minority of the revenue and the customer should not agonise over it. Second, the ECIS range is genuinely a range, and a proposal presenting a single ECIS figure is presenting a point estimate from a distribution. The honest presentation shows the range and the assumptions behind it. Note also the 720 kWac rating sits comfortably below the licensing threshold, which is worth stating explicitly because it removes three to six months from the programme.
