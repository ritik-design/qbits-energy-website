## What Philippine Installers Need From Design Software

**Export priced at the blended generation rate.** This is the single most important setting. The BGC covers generation charges only, excluding transmission, distribution, system loss, and the various universal charges that make up the rest of a Philippine electricity bill. Exported energy earns the generation component. Imported energy costs all of it. SurgePV's [ERC net metering guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/philippines/guides/erc-net-metering-rules) sets out the methodology.

**Year-end forfeiture modelled honestly.** Monthly credits roll over, but may be forfeited at year-end settlement. A system sized well above annual consumption is therefore accumulating credits it may never realise. This is a real design constraint, not a footnote, and it argues against the oversizing habit carried over from retail-netting markets.

**PEC compliance at design time.** The Philippine Electrical Code is based on NEC 2008. Anti-islanding must disconnect within 2 seconds of a grid outage. Array frames and metal enclosures require earthing. DC arc fault protection applies on longer cable runs. Bidirectional metering must be accommodated. A Certificate of Compliance signed by a licensed RME or PEE is required, covered in SurgePV's [Certificate of Compliance guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/philippines/guides/certificate-of-compliance).

**Tropical thermal modelling.** High ambient temperature and humidity produce module derating that temperate-climate defaults understate. The thermal model matters more here than in most markets, and it feeds directly into the yield figure the customer is buying.

**The new 1 MW ceiling.** The April 2026 DOE circular raising the non-residential cap from 100 kW to 1 MW opens a project class that previously had to be artificially split. Design tools that still enforce a 100 kW assumption in their net metering logic will misprice these.

## Why Self-Consumption Decides A Philippine Project

> **What most Philippine proposals get wrong.** They present net metering as though exported energy were worth what imported energy costs. It is worth roughly half. A system that exports heavily is selling at the generation rate and buying back at the full tariff, and the difference is not a rounding error.

The design response is straightforward once the economics are stated plainly: size to consumption rather than to roof area, and shift load into daylight hours where the customer's operation allows it. Commercial buyers are particularly well placed here, because a factory or a mall runs its heaviest load during exactly the hours the array produces most. That alignment is why Philippine C&I solar pencils better than residential on the same tariff.

For residential, the picture is different and batteries enter earlier than they would in a retail-netting market. Combined with grid reliability concerns, the resilience case and the economic case point the same way. Our [solar plus storage design software](/blog/solar-plus-storage-design-software/) comparison covers the dispatch modelling required to size one properly.

Either way, the calculation needs hourly resolution. Annual kWh totals cannot distinguish a factory that consumes everything it generates from a household that exports two thirds of it, and those two projects have very different returns on identical hardware. Our [PV yield simulation software](/blog/pv-yield-simulation-software/) guide covers the methodology.

## Worked Example: 100 kW Rooftop, Factory Versus Warehouse

An illustrative calculation showing why load profile dominates in a BGC market. Inputs: 100 kW DC, 1,400 kWh per kW specific yield, 140,000 kWh generation, retail import at PHP 12 per kWh, export credited at PHP 5.50 per kWh.

| Line | Factory, daytime shift | Warehouse, low daytime load |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Generation | 140,000 kWh | 140,000 kWh |
| Self-consumed | 126,000 kWh (90%) | 49,000 kWh (35%) |
| Exported | 14,000 kWh | 91,000 kWh |
| Value of avoided import at PHP 12 | PHP 1,512,000 | PHP 588,000 |
| Export credit at PHP 5.50 | PHP 77,000 | PHP 500,500 |
| Year 1 benefit | PHP 1,589,000 | PHP 1,088,500 |

Same array, same roof size, same city. The factory returns 46% more because it consumes what it generates. A tool reporting annual kWh gives both projects the same headline and the same implied payback, which is wrong for both. The warehouse is not a bad project, but it is a different project, and the honest recommendation there is a smaller array plus load shifting, or storage if the resilience case supports it.
