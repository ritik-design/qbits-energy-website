## What Is Solar Energy: The Physics in Plain Language

The sun is not merely warm, it is a continuous nuclear fusion reactor releasing 173,000 terawatts of energy toward Earth every second. To put that in perspective, humanity's entire annual energy consumption could be powered by less than one hour of the solar energy hitting our planet. The challenge is not scarcity; it is conversion.

Solar panels perform that conversion through the **photovoltaic effect**, a phenomenon first documented by French physicist Edmond Becquerel in 1839. Here is how it works, step by step, without any engineering jargon.

### From Sunlight to Silicon

A solar cell is a thin wafer of silicon (the same element found in sand) treated with two types of chemical impurities to create a negative layer (N-type) and a positive layer (P-type) sandwiched together. This sandwich is called a P-N junction.

When a photon of light strikes this junction, it transfers its energy to an electron in the silicon crystal. That energised electron breaks free from its atom and starts moving, and moving electrons are, by definition, electric current. Every photon that arrives produces a tiny nudge of current. Billions of photons per second hitting a standard 400 Wp panel produce enough nudges to power a ceiling fan, a refrigerator compressor, or a laptop charger.

### From Cell to Module to Array

A single silicon cell produces roughly 0.5–0.6 volts, far too little for household use. Solar manufacturers connect 60, 72, or 96 cells in series inside a laminated glass-and-aluminium frame to produce a **solar module** (commonly called a panel) rated at 300–600 Wp and 30–50 volts. Multiple modules wired together form a **string**, and multiple strings wired into a **combiner box** form an **array**.

The current produced by all these cells is **direct current (DC)**: the same type of current in a battery. Household appliances run on **alternating current (AC)** at 230 V, 50 Hz in India. The conversion job belongs to the [solar inverter](/glossary/solar-inverter/), the most critical component in the system.

> **173,000 terawatts.** The amount of solar energy continuously reaching Earth's surface, approximately 10,000 times global electricity demand. *Source - [International Energy Agency, World Energy Outlook](https://www.iea.org/), 2025.*

### The Role of the Inverter

The inverter takes the DC output from the solar array, converts it to grid-compatible 230 V AC, and feeds it to the distribution board in your home. Modern inverters also perform **Maximum Power Point Tracking ([MPPT](/glossary/mppt/))**: a real-time algorithm that adjusts the operating voltage of the panels to extract maximum power as cloud cover, temperature, and angle of sunlight change throughout the day.

A high-quality inverter with 98% peak efficiency loses only 2% of the DC energy it receives during the AC conversion, the difference between a 3 kW array generating 12 units and generating 11.76 units on the same day.

## The Three Types of Solar Systems in India

Understanding which system type fits your situation is as important as understanding the technology itself. The three types differ in cost, capability, and whether they keep your lights on during a power cut.

### On-Grid Solar (Grid-Tied)

This is the most common choice for Indian residential buyers, accounting for the large majority of rooftop installations. An on-grid system has no battery. It sends all generated power to your home's distribution board; surplus power (when generation exceeds consumption) flows out to the DISCOM grid through a [net metering](/glossary/net-metering/) arrangement and earns you credits on your electricity bill.

**Pros:**
- Lowest cost per kW, typically ₹55,000–₹75,000 per kW installed
- Eligible for PM Surya Ghar subsidy (up to ₹78,000 for 3 kW)
- Fastest payback: 4–6 years in most Indian states
- No battery maintenance

**Cons:**
- Zero power during grid outages (inverter shuts down for safety)
- Bill savings depend on net metering tariff approved by your state regulator

On-grid systems suit homeowners in states with reliable grids: Karnataka, Gujarat, Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, Telangana. For a detailed comparison, see [on-grid vs hybrid vs off-grid](/blog/on-grid-vs-hybrid-vs-off-grid-decision-guide/).

### Hybrid Solar (Grid-Tied with Battery)

A hybrid system connects both to the grid and to a battery bank. During the day, solar charges the battery and powers your home. At night, the battery supplies power. During a grid outage, the battery and panels together keep your essential circuits running.

**Pros:**
- Power during outages, critical in UP, Bihar, parts of Maharashtra
- Stores afternoon solar surplus for evening use, maximising self-consumption
- Battery can be added to an existing on-grid system later in some configurations

**Cons:**
- 25–40% higher upfront cost than on-grid
- Battery replacement in 7–12 years (lithium iron phosphate) adds lifecycle cost
- Longer payback: 6–8 years

Hybrid systems are worth considering if your area experiences more than 2 hours of daily power cuts, or if your grid is particularly unreliable. See the [hybrid inverter](/hybrid-inverter/) page for available Qbits models.

### Off-Grid Solar

Off-grid systems operate entirely without a DISCOM connection. They use a large battery bank to store energy for nights and cloudy days, and size the panel array conservatively to guarantee consistent charge even in low-irradiance months.

Off-grid systems are designed for remote locations (agricultural pump houses, mountain hamlets, forest guard posts) where extending the grid is not economically viable. The cost per kWh generated is significantly higher than on-grid due to the oversized battery and panel requirements.

For urban and semi-urban homeowners with a functional DISCOM connection, off-grid is almost never the right choice. The economics of on-grid or hybrid systems are far stronger.

## Solar Energy in 3 Minutes: The Beginner's Flow

Every beginner asking "what is solar energy" ultimately wants to know: how does sunlight on my roof become a lower electricity bill? This framework answers that question in six logical steps.

### The Solar Energy in 3 Minutes: The Beginner's Flow

1. **Sunlight hits the panel**: Photons excite silicon electrons; each cell produces ~0.5 V of DC current.
2. **Modules aggregate the voltage**: 60–96 cells in series per module produce 30–50 V; strings of modules reach 300–800 V.
3. **The MPPT inverter converts DC to AC**: The inverter tracks the optimal operating voltage second-by-second and converts DC to 230 V AC at 50 Hz.
4. **AC power flows to the distribution board**: Your appliances draw from it exactly as they draw from the grid.
5. **Surplus flows out through the net meter**: When generation exceeds consumption, the bidirectional net meter records export units; your DISCOM credits these against night-time consumption.
6. **Your bill shrinks**: You pay only for the net units consumed (grid import minus solar export), sometimes reaching zero or generating a credit balance.

The flow above summarises the journey from photon to bill reduction. Every component in that chain (panel, string, inverter, net meter) has a quality specification that determines how much of Step 1 survives to become savings in Step 6.

## What Solar Energy Does Not Do: Honest Limitations

A beginner's guide is incomplete without addressing the limitations that sales pitches often skip.

- **Solar does not power your home at night (on-grid)**: Without a battery, your panels produce zero output after sunset. You draw from the grid exactly as before, but your net meter credit offsets those units.
- **Panel output degrades over time**: Quality panels [degrade](/glossary/degradation/) at 0.5–0.7% per year. After 25 years, output is 85–90% of the original. This is accounted for in the manufacturer's linear power warranty.
- **Roof area is a limiting factor**: Each kW of solar requires roughly 8–10 sq ft of shadow-free roof area. A 3 kW system needs approximately 24–30 sq ft of usable south- or west-facing roof space, a figure worth confirming with a proper site survey rather than a rough measurement.
- **DISCOM approval takes time**: Net metering approval from your state DISCOM can take 15 days to 3 months depending on the state. Kerala is notoriously slow; Gujarat and Maharashtra are faster.
- **Not all installers are equal**: A poorly installed system with undersized DC cables, improperly matched strings, or an uncertified inverter can underperform by 15–25% and void warranties. The [ALMM list](/blog/almm-list-phase-iii-guide/) is the government's quality filter; insist on ALMM-listed equipment installed by a vetted local solar installer.

## Common Beginner Myths About Solar Energy in India

Three myths consistently appear in WhatsApp groups and online forums.

**Myth 1: "Solar only works in Rajasthan."**
False. Kerala, with its heavy monsoon, still generates 1,100–1,300 kWh per kW per year, strong enough for a 5–6 year payback given the state's high tariffs. Solar works everywhere in India; only the sizing and yield estimates change.

**Myth 2: "Solar will make my electricity bill zero."**
Sometimes true, often not. If your monthly bill is ₹5,000 and you install a well-sized 5 kW system with net metering, your net bill can approach zero or even produce a credit balance. If you install a 1 kW system against a ₹10,000 bill, you will only offset a portion. Right-sizing to your consumption is critical.

**Myth 3: "I need to store all my energy in batteries."**
Not necessary for on-grid systems. Net metering effectively turns the DISCOM grid into a free, unlimited battery. You export surplus during the day and import at night at no additional cost, only the net difference appears on your bill. Batteries add cost; they are only justified if power cuts are frequent or grid access is unreliable.
