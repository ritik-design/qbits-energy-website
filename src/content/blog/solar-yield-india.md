## What kWh per kWp per Year Actually Means: and Why It Matters

Kilowatt-hours per kilowatt-peak per year (kWh/kWp/yr) answers a simple question: how many units of electricity does one kilowatt of installed solar capacity generate over a full year, accounting for all real-world conditions?

The metric is built on [global horizontal irradiance (GHI)](/glossary/ghi/) (the total solar radiation hitting the ground at your location) combined with the system's [performance ratio (PR)](/glossary/pr/), which captures every loss between the sun and the meter. The relationship is:

**Specific Yield (kWh/kWp/yr) = Annual GHI (kWh/m²) × Performance Ratio**

For a site in Jaipur with 2,050 kWh/m² annual GHI and a PR of 0.80, the expected yield is 1,640 kWh/kWp. For a site in Kolkata with 1,580 kWh/m² GHI and the same PR, the yield drops to 1,264 kWh/kWp.

The practical value of this metric is scale-independence. Your 5 kWp home system and a 1 MWp industrial rooftop in the same city should deliver essentially the same kWh per kWp. When a specific-yield number deviates significantly from the state benchmark, something is wrong with either the installation quality or the estimate.

| Metric | Formula | Use case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Specific Yield (kWh/kWp/yr) | Annual generation ÷ plant capacity | Quote verification, payback calculation |
| CUF (%) | Specific Yield ÷ 8,760 × 100 | Utility tenders, performance contracts |
| Performance Ratio (%) | Actual yield ÷ Irradiance-based ideal yield | Plant quality benchmarking |
| Peak Sun Hours (PSH) | Annual GHI ÷ 1,000 W/m² | Quick sizing estimates |

These four metrics describe the same plant from different angles. Specific yield is the most useful for quote verification because it is directly comparable to the kWh promises in an installer's proposal, and it feeds directly into the [solar inverter payback period calculation](/blog/solar-inverter-payback-period-in-india/).

## India's Solar Yield Range: the National Picture

India spans 30 degrees of latitude and contains climatic zones from hyper-arid desert to humid tropical coast. This creates a 50 percent spread in annual solar yield, from around 1,200 kWh/kWp in the north-eastern states to 1,800 kWh/kWp in western Rajasthan.

> **1,200 to 1,800 kWh/kWp/year.** That is India's rooftop solar yield range, based on NREL NSRDB long-term irradiance data. The median for the Indo-Gangetic plain and peninsular India sits at 1,400 to 1,550 kWh/kWp, meaning most Indian buyers should plan around 1,400 to 1,500 kWh/kWp for a well-designed system. *Source - [NREL National Solar Radiation Database (NSRDB)](https://nsrdb.nrel.gov/), 2026.*

The national average is often cited at 1,500 kWh/kWp, which is a reasonable planning number for the Deccan plateau and most of peninsular India. However, applying a national average to a specific project in Kolkata or Shillong will overestimate generation by 15 to 20 percent, a meaningful error when calculating payback or sizing battery storage.

The data sources behind these benchmarks matter. The three authoritative sources for Indian solar irradiance are:

- **NREL NSRDB**: satellite-derived, 4 km resolution, covers all of India, publicly accessible.
- **IRENA Global Atlas**: peer-reviewed global irradiance database used in sovereign energy planning.
- **NIWE Solar Resource Atlas**: India-specific ground-station data from the National Institute of Wind Energy.

For C&I projects above 100 kWp, bankable yield studies typically use Solargis or NSRDB data validated against the nearest NIWE ground station. For residential and small commercial projects, the state benchmarks in the next section provide an accurate planning basis.

### Why Rajasthan Leads by Such a Wide Margin

Western Rajasthan is one of the top ten solar resource zones in the world. Jaisalmer district averages over 6 peak sun hours per day, annual GHI exceeds 2,100 kWh/m², and clear-sky days number over 300 per year. The Thar Desert's dry air and low humidity mean minimal diffuse scattering, so a larger fraction of the irradiance arrives as direct beam, the most productive component for fixed-tilt panels. SECI utility tenders in Rajasthan are bid at yields of 1,800 to 1,950 kWh/kWp for tracker-mounted bifacial systems.

### Himachal Pradesh: Altitude Partially Compensates

Himachal Pradesh sits in the lower yield tier at 1,300 to 1,400 kWh/kWp, but the state deserves a separate note. High-altitude sites above 1,500 metres gain two advantages that partially offset lower irradiance: significantly reduced cell temperatures (cooler air means modules operate closer to STC conditions) and reduced aerosol/dust load. A system at 2,000 metres elevation in the Spiti Valley can achieve PR values of 83 to 86 percent, 5 to 7 percent above a comparable Rajasthan rooftop system. The irradiance deficit ultimately dominates, but the yield gap between Himachal and the national average is smaller than the GHI gap would suggest.

### The Monsoon Effect on Southern and Eastern States

Kerala, West Bengal, and Odisha receive less annual irradiance not because of latitude alone, but because of extended monsoon seasons. Kerala's southwest monsoon runs from June through September, four months of heavy cloud cover. West Bengal experiences both southwest monsoon and post-monsoon cloud persistence through October. These states see annual irradiance of 1,500 to 1,680 kWh/m², translating to yield benchmarks of 1,200 to 1,450 kWh/kWp. The economic case for solar remains strong in these states (grid tariffs are high enough to justify the investment) but payback periods run 1 to 2 years longer than in western India.

## The 3-Factor Yield Verification Method

### The 3-Factor Yield Verification Method

This method gives any buyer (from a homeowner with a 5 kWp quote to a factory manager evaluating a 500 kWp proposal) a three-step process to verify whether an installer's generation promise is credible before signing anything.

1. **State Benchmark Check**: Divide the quoted annual generation (kWh) by the system capacity (kWp). Compare the result to your state's benchmark range from the table above. The quotient should fall within the range, or the installer must provide a documented reason it does not. A number more than 10 percent above the range upper bound is a red flag.

2. **Loss-Adjusted Reality Check**: Take the state mid-point benchmark and apply realistic loss factors for your specific site. Subtract 5 to 10 percent for soiling (monthly cleaning assumed), 2 to 5 percent for partial shading if any nearby obstructions exist, 1 to 3 percent for inverter losses, and 1 to 2 percent for DC cable and mismatch losses. The resulting net figure should be close to what the installer has promised. If their promise equals the gross benchmark without acknowledging any losses, the estimate is optimistic.

3. **Orientation and Tilt Verification**: Ask for the proposed panel direction (azimuth) and tilt angle. South-facing at latitude tilt is the reference condition for all benchmarks above. An east-west split loses 5 to 8 percent. A flat (zero-tilt) installation loses 5 to 8 percent. A north-facing installation in India loses 15 to 25 percent. If the system will be installed at a non-standard orientation, the yield promise must be proportionally lower than the benchmark.

A quote that passes all three checks is credible. A quote that fails any one of them deserves a written clarification before you proceed. Print this three-step checklist and bring it to your installer meeting.

This method is particularly valuable when comparing two installer quotes for the same roof. If installer A promises 7,500 kWh/year and installer B promises 9,000 kWh/year for identical 5 kWp systems on the same Maharashtra rooftop, the state benchmark (1,500 to 1,600 kWh/kWp) puts the credible range at 7,500 to 8,000 kWh. Installer A is within range. Installer B has inflated the estimate by at least 12 percent, which translates directly to an inflated payback promise.

## How Soiling, Shading, and Temperature Reduce Real-World Yield

Benchmark yields describe a clean, unshaded, correctly oriented system under average irradiance conditions. Real-world systems deviate from all three. Understanding the loss budget lets you calculate the net yield your system will actually deliver.

### Soiling Losses: India's Biggest Yield Killer

India's combination of dust, agricultural residue, bird activity, and industrial fallout makes soiling the single largest source of generation loss for most rooftop systems. Research published by [IRENA on renewable power generation costs](https://www.irena.org/publications/2025/Jun/Renewable-power-generation-costs-in-2024) and field studies from India-based EPCs document the following loss ranges:

- **Rajasthan / Gujarat (arid)**: 3 to 5 percent per month without cleaning; annual soiling loss of 10 to 15 percent with monthly cleaning.
- **Punjab / Haryana (agricultural)**: 4 to 6 percent per month in post-harvest periods; 8 to 12 percent annually with bi-weekly cleaning during peak dust months.
- **Karnataka / Tamil Nadu (moderate)**: 2 to 4 percent per month; 6 to 10 percent annually with monthly cleaning.
- **Kerala / West Bengal (humid)**: 1 to 3 percent per month; 4 to 8 percent annually; humidity keeps dust from adhering as aggressively but promotes biological growth.

Monthly cleaning is the recommended standard for most Indian locations. If your installer is promising benchmark yield without confirming a cleaning schedule, the actual generation will fall short.

### Shading Analysis: the 5 Percent Trap

Even partial shading has a disproportionate impact on yield because traditional string inverters shut down the entire string when one module is shaded. A single chimney shadow hitting 10 percent of one module can reduce string-level output by 30 to 50 percent during the shaded hours. Annual shading loss ranges from under 1 percent for an unobstructed open rooftop to 8 to 12 percent for an urban rooftop surrounded by parapet walls and nearby buildings.

The [shading analysis](/glossary/shading-analysis/) must be conducted at the design stage, not after installation. If your installer has not performed a shadow study using a solar pathfinder tool or software simulation, require one before proceeding.

### Temperature Derating: Why Rajasthan is Not as Dominant as Raw GHI Suggests

Solar modules are rated at 25 °C standard test conditions. Indian summers routinely push rooftop module temperatures to 65 to 75 °C in peak hours. The temperature coefficient of most mono-PERC and TOPCon modules is −0.30 to −0.35 percent per degree Celsius. At 70 °C module temperature, output is reduced by 13 to 16 percent relative to STC.

This temperature derating partially explains why Rajasthan's yield benchmark (1,700 to 1,800 kWh/kWp) does not simply scale proportionally with its GHI advantage. Hot summer afternoons in Jodhpur see module temperatures exceeding 75 °C, reducing instantaneous efficiency substantially. The higher total irradiance still wins out, but by a smaller margin than the raw irradiance numbers suggest. This is also why inverter temperature handling matters: an inverter that [derates output in high ambient temperatures](/blog/inverter-overheating/) (common with poorly designed units operating in 45 °C ambient) adds to the effective loss.

- **Inverter efficiency loss**: 1 to 3 percent annually (weighted average efficiency vs peak nameplate).
- **DC cable losses**: 0.5 to 1.5 percent (function of cable sizing and run length).
- **Module mismatch**: 0.5 to 2 percent (worse in partial-shade or mixed-orientation arrays).
- **Availability losses**: 0.5 to 1.5 percent (inverter downtime, maintenance shutdowns).

Combined system losses for a well-designed rooftop system total 10 to 18 percent. A net yield of 82 to 90 percent of the gross benchmark is realistic.

## Worked Example: Verifying a C&I Quote in Pune

A manufacturing unit in Pune receives a proposal for a 100 kWp on-grid rooftop system with the following generation promise: 1,60,000 kWh in year one.

**Step 1, State Benchmark Check**

Maharashtra benchmark: 1,500 to 1,600 kWh/kWp/year. Quoted yield: 1,60,000 ÷ 100 = 1,600 kWh/kWp. This sits at the top of the Maharashtra benchmark range, technically within range, but at the optimistic edge.

**Step 2, Loss-Adjusted Reality Check**

| Loss factor | Estimate for this site |
| --- | --- |
| Soiling (monthly cleaning planned) | −8% |
| Shading (low parapet, minimal obstruction) | −2% |
| Inverter losses (quality unit, 96% WA efficiency) | −2% |
| DC cable and mismatch losses | −1% |
| **Total losses** | **−13%** |
| **Net yield after losses** | **1,392 kWh/kWp → 1,39,200 kWh/year** |

The installer's promise of 1,60,000 kWh implies zero soiling and minimal other losses, which is not realistic for a Pune industrial rooftop. A realistic estimate is 1,39,200 to 1,44,000 kWh in year one. The buyer should request a revised estimate that explicitly accounts for loss factors.

**Step 3, Orientation and Tilt Verification**

The roof is flat with a south-facing option. The installer proposes 10-degree ballasted tilt (slightly below the optimal 19 degrees for Pune's latitude of 18.5°N). The tilt adjustment reduces yield by approximately 2 to 3 percent versus optimal tilt. This is acceptable for a flat-roof installation and does not change the benchmark materially.

**Conclusion**: The quote is overstated by approximately 11 to 14 percent. Requesting a revised generation estimate with explicit loss assumptions is reasonable. If the installer cannot or will not provide one, that is valuable information about the quality of their engineering.

The inverter accounts for 1 to 3 percentage points of annual yield, which sounds small but adds up. Consider two 50 kWp systems in Gujarat (benchmark 1,650 kWh/kWp), one with a 98 percent peak efficiency inverter operating at 96 percent weighted average, and one with a 95 percent peak efficiency unit at 92 percent weighted average:

## Regional Yield Comparison: High, Mid, and Lower Performers

### High-Yield States (Rajasthan and Gujarat)

Rajasthan and Gujarat represent India's solar belt. Both states combine high GHI, low humidity, and grid infrastructure built for large-scale solar. C&I buyers in these states benefit from the fastest payback periods in the country, a well-designed 100 kWp system in Ahmedabad or Jaipur achieves payback in 4 to 5 years at current commercial tariffs of ₹8 to 10 per unit. ### Mid-Yield States (Maharashtra, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Delhi)

These states cover the majority of India's C&I solar installed base. Maharashtra's Pune-Nashik industrial corridor, Karnataka's Bengaluru technology hubs, and Tamil Nadu's Coimbatore manufacturing belt all operate in the 1,400 to 1,600 kWh/kWp range. Payback at current commercial tariffs (₹8 to 12 per unit in these states) runs 5 to 7 years for well-designed systems. Delhi and NCR are notable for relatively high commercial tariffs that partially compensate for the modest yield advantage over peninsular states.

### Lower-Yield States (West Bengal and Himachal Pradesh)

West Bengal's 1,200 to 1,300 kWh/kWp benchmark does not mean solar is unviable, West Bengal's commercial tariffs of ₹8 to 10 per unit still make solar financially attractive, with payback periods of 7 to 9 years. The key is setting accurate generation expectations from the start. A buyer in Kolkata presented with a yield promise of 1,600 kWh/kWp is being misled by roughly 25 percent, which is a 2-year payback overstatement. Himachal Pradesh's altitude advantage (higher PR) partially closes the gap with peninsular states, but the benchmark remains 1,300 to 1,400 kWh/kWp for most of the state.

## Common Mistakes When Evaluating Solar Generation Estimates

Getting the yield estimate right at the proposal stage prevents downstream disputes and financial disappointment. These are the most frequent errors buyers and installers make:

- **Using the national average for every state**: A flat 1,500 kWh/kWp for a Kolkata system overstates generation by 15 to 20 percent and shortens the quoted payback by 1 to 2 years. State-specific benchmarks are not optional.
- **Ignoring degradation in long-term projections**: Module degradation runs at 0.40 to 0.55 percent per year for quality mono-PERC and TOPCon panels, per [IEC 61215 and IEC 61730 long-term reliability data](https://www.iec.ch/). A 25-year generation promise that uses year-one yield for every year overstates lifetime output by 8 to 12 percent. The [module degradation](/glossary/degradation/) rate should appear explicitly in the detailed financial model.
- **Treating a shaded roof as unshaded**: Installers sometimes apply benchmark yield to rooftops with chimneys, water tanks, adjacent buildings, or satellite dishes without modelling shadow impact. A 5 percent shading loss that is not disclosed is a 5 percent shortfall in savings relative to promise.
- **Confusing DC capacity with AC output**: Some quotes use DC kWp for sizing and AC kW for generation estimates inconsistently. Confirm that capacity and yield are measured on the same basis. The [DC oversizing ratio](/blog/dc-oversizing-in-solar/), typically 1.1 to 1.3× in Indian conditions, affects the relationship between nameplate and real-world output.
- **Accepting a verbal promise without written specifications**: The generation estimate, assumptions, loss factors, and state benchmark used must appear in the written proposal. A verbal promise of 1,800 units per month carries no contractual weight and cannot be verified against actual monitoring data.

## Understanding the Yield-Payback Connection

Every rupee in the financial case for solar depends on the yield estimate being accurate. The [solar inverter payback period](/blog/solar-inverter-payback-period-in-india/) calculation follows directly from the yield number:

**Annual savings = Net Yield (kWh/kWp) × System capacity (kWp) × Electricity tariff (₹/kWh)**

**Simple payback = Total system cost ÷ Annual savings**

For a 10 kWp system in Pune costing ₹4.5 Lakh:
- Gross benchmark yield: 1,550 kWh/kWp
- Net yield after 12% losses: 1,364 kWh/kWp
- Annual generation: 13,640 kWh
- Annual savings at ₹9/unit: ₹1,22,760
- Simple payback: 3.67 years

If an installer uses 1,700 kWh/kWp (Rajasthan-level yield for a Pune system):
- Annual generation: 17,000 kWh
- Annual savings: ₹1,53,000
- Quoted payback: 2.94 years, nearly 9 months faster than the realistic figure

That 9-month optimism feels small at signing. Three years into operation, when actual generation is running 20 percent below promise, it does not feel small to the buyer. Accurate yield benchmarks protect buyers from this outcome and protect installers from disputes. The [net metering framework](/blog/net-metering-india-complete-guide/) in most states also depends on accurate yield estimates to determine the export credit the buyer will receive, making precision doubly important.

> **India added 24.5 GW of new solar capacity in 2025**, according to [JMK Research India's solar market tracker](https://jmkresearch.com/), bringing cumulative installed capacity above 100 GW. The scale of this expansion means hundreds of thousands of buyers are receiving generation promises every month, and most have no tool to verify them. *Source, JMK Research, 2026.*

The [Capacity Utilisation Factor (CUF)](/glossary/cuf/) provides a related check: a system promised at 1,500 kWh/kWp annual yield implies a CUF of 1,500 ÷ 8,760 = 17.1 percent. MNRE benchmarks for grid-connected rooftop solar in peninsular India target CUF of 15 to 19 percent. A quoted CUF outside this range for a standard rooftop deserves explanation.

## Seasonal Variation: Why Monthly Data Matters as Much as Annual

Annual yield benchmarks are useful for financial planning, but monthly yield variation is what operational monitoring must track. Every Indian location has a seasonal pattern shaped by monsoon, winter haze, and summer irradiance peaks.

| Month | Rajasthan (% of annual/12) | Maharashtra | Karnataka | West Bengal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| January | 80% | 85% | 90% | 85% |
| February | 90% | 92% | 95% | 88% |
| March | 105% | 105% | 108% | 98% |
| April | 115% | 110% | 105% | 105% |
| May | 115% | 108% | 100% | 108% |
| June | 100% | 88% | 80% | 75% |
| July | 90% | 78% | 75% | 65% |
| August | 88% | 78% | 78% | 62% |
| September | 92% | 85% | 85% | 75% |
| October | 100% | 98% | 100% | 98% |
| November | 90% | 95% | 98% | 92% |
| December | 80% | 84% | 87% | 80% |

Values expressed as percentage of monthly average generation (annual yield divided equally across 12 months = 100%).

The practical implication: if a monitoring system shows June generation at 90 percent of the monthly average for Rajasthan, that is a green flag. If a Karnataka system shows June at 90 percent when the expected figure is 80 percent, the data suggests excellent performance relative to seasonal norms. Compare actual monthly generation to the seasonal pattern, not to the annual average, and the monitoring data becomes far more diagnostic.

[Peak sun hours](/glossary/peak-sun-hours/) data from NSRDB and NIWE confirms these seasonal patterns quantitatively. Using monthly-resolved irradiance data rather than annual averages improves the precision of both yield estimation and performance monitoring.

---

Solar yield in India is not a single number, it is a state-specific, site-specific, and management-specific outcome that can vary by 50 percent across locations and by 15 to 20 percent within a single state based on design quality and maintenance. The benchmarks in this guide give buyers the reference data needed to evaluate promises critically. The 3-Factor Yield Verification Method gives them the process. The combination turns a technically complex question into a 10-minute desk exercise that any procurement team can perform before signing a proposal.
