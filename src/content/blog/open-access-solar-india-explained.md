## What Open Access Solar Actually Is

Open access is the legal right of a large consumer to buy power from a generator other than the local DISCOM. The shared transmission and distribution network delivers it. The framework comes from the Electricity Act 2003, which mandated non-discriminatory grid access for eligible consumers.

In the solar context, it works like this. A developer builds a solar park in a high-irradiance district, say Pavagada in Karnataka or Bhadla in Rajasthan. Your factory in Bengaluru or Surat signs a power purchase agreement (PPA) with that developer. The plant injects power into the grid. The grid operator schedules it against your consumption. Your DISCOM bill then reflects energy drawn from the open access source instead of DISCOM supply.

You pay three parties. The developer gets the PPA tariff, typically Rs 3.00 to Rs 4.00 per unit for solar in 2026. The transmission and distribution utilities get network charges for carrying the power. The state regulator sets surcharges designed to protect DISCOM economics. Your DISCOM relationship does not end. It still maintains your connection, bills you, and supplies whatever the solar plant does not cover.

Two policy forces are pushing demand beyond pure savings. Large designated consumers now face non-fossil consumption obligations under the Energy Conservation (Amendment) Act 2022. Renewable purchase obligations on DISCOMs are also tightening, which nudges regulators to keep open access viable. Green hydrogen and green ammonia producers get a full exemption from CSS and additional surcharge under the 2022 rules, according to PIB (2022). For a standard factory, the driver is simpler: a grid tariff rising 3% to 5% a year against a solar PPA that is flat or near-flat.

## Who Qualifies: The 100 kW Rule

The Green Energy Open Access Rules 2022 came from the Ministry of Power. They cut the minimum load for green open access from 1 MW to 100 kW, according to PIB (2023). The threshold applies to contract demand or sanctioned load. This one change pulled lakhs of mid-sized consumers into the eligible pool. Hotels, hospitals, cold storage units, and small factories all qualify now.

State-by-state variations still apply. Tamil Nadu historically allowed open access from 63 kVA. Some states apply the 100 kW threshold per connection. Others allow aggregation across multiple connections under common ownership. A few states have been slow to align their regulations with the central rules. The practical threshold on the ground can lag the policy on paper.

Beyond the load threshold, you need a clean payment record with the DISCOM and no outstanding regulatory disputes. Most states process the no-objection certificate through a single-window green open access portal. Approval timelines run 15 to 60 days depending on the state.

The application follows a standard sequence. You apply on the state nodal portal with your sanctioned load, proposed capacity, and the generator's project details. The DISCOM verifies technical feasibility at your connection point. The state load despatch centre confirms scheduling capability. Connectivity approval follows. Long-term open access runs 12 years or more, medium-term runs 3 months to 3 years, and short-term runs up to 1 month. Most solar PPAs use the long-term route.

One practical note. If your contracted demand is 95 kW today, enhancing it to 100 kW is usually cheap and fast. We have seen buyers cross the eligibility line this way in under a month.

Keep every approval document. The connectivity approval, the open access registration, and the scheduling agreement all get referenced later during banking settlement and any dispute with the DISCOM. Buyers who file these carefully resolve billing disputes in weeks. Those who cannot produce them wait for months.

## The Charge Stack: What Open Access Really Costs

The PPA tariff is only the starting point. Landed cost equals the PPA tariff plus every regulated charge between injection point and meter. Here is each component, with 2026 ranges from state tariff orders.

- **Wheeling and transmission charges.** The fee for using the network. Karnataka charges HT consumers Rs 0.29 per kWh, one of the lowest in India. Gujarat charges around Rs 0.63, Maharashtra Rs 0.60, and Tamil Nadu Rs 1.03 for FY 2026-27, according to the respective state tariff orders (2025, 2026).
- **Cross-subsidy surcharge (CSS).** The largest and most contested charge. Industrial tariffs cross-subsidise domestic and agricultural consumers. CSS compensates the DISCOM when a high-paying consumer leaves. It runs Rs 1.10 to Rs 2.10 per kWh for HT industrial consumers. Karnataka is Rs 2.08 and Tamil Nadu Rs 2.06 for FY 2026-27. Captive and group captive projects are exempt from CSS entirely.
- **Additional surcharge (ASC).** Covers the DISCOM's stranded fixed costs from long-term PPAs it signed for consumers. Where levied, it adds Rs 0.40 to Rs 1.41 per kWh. Several states waive it if you keep paying fixed demand charges.
- **Banking charges.** Solar generates during the day; factories run at night. Banking lets you park surplus daytime units with the DISCOM and draw them later. The fee runs roughly 2% to 12% of banked energy. Gujarat levies Rs 1.50 per kWh. Maharashtra uses a monthly time-of-day mechanism at roughly Rs 1.25 per unit.
- **Scheduling, SLDC fees, and electricity duty.** Smaller line items, typically Rs 0.05 to Rs 0.30 per kWh combined. They still belong in the model.

The result: a Rs 3.75 PPA tariff lands at Rs 5.00 in a group captive structure in a friendly state. In a third-party structure in a high-CSS state, it lands at Rs 7.50 or more. The charge stack is the deal, not the tariff.

## Group Captive vs Third-Party PPA: The Core Decision

Every open access transaction uses one of three structures. The choice determines which charges apply.

| Dimension | Third-Party PPA | Group Captive | Single Captive |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Equity required | None | 26% collectively | 26% |
| Consumption rule | None | 51% collectively | 51% of output |
| Cross-subsidy surcharge | Payable | Exempt | Exempt |
| Additional surcharge | Payable | Often waived | Often waived |
| Typical landed cost (friendly state) | Rs 6.00 to 7.00/kWh | Rs 4.50 to 5.50/kWh | Rs 4.50 to 5.50/kWh |
| Capital commitment | Zero | Rs 1 to 1.5 Crore per MW | Full project cost |
| Contract tenure | 10 to 25 years | Plant life (25 years) | Plant life |
| Best for | Zero-capex buyers | Consortia of 2 to 10 consumers | Large single industrials |

**Third-party PPA** is the zero-capex route. A developer funds, builds, owns, and maintains the plant. You sign a long-term PPA, pay per unit, and bear the full charge stack. It behaves like the [RESCO or OPEX model](/blog/solar-resco-model-india-guide/) moved off-site. For the financing logic behind zero-capex structures, see [zero-cost solar RESCO and OPEX models](/blog/zero-cost-solar-resco-opex-india/).

**Group captive** requires consumers to hold at least 26% equity in the project company. They must also consume at least 51% of generation. These are the two captive tests under the Electricity Rules. The payoff is exemption from CSS, worth Rs 1.50 to Rs 2.00 per kWh in most states. A typical shape is five factories each subscribing 1 to 3 MW.

A recent policy shift makes group captive easier. The Ministry of Power now lets the 51% consumption test be met collectively by the group, according to Mercom India (2026). Earlier, strict per-member proportionality applied. One member's minor deviation could strip captive status from the whole project and trigger retroactive CSS. That enforcement risk has materially reduced.

**Single captive** suits large consumers, typically 5 MW and above, who can deploy Rs 3.5 to 4.5 Crore per MW of equity. The [solar IRR and payback analysis for C&I](/blog/solar-irr-payback-ci-india/) models the ownership returns.

## Savings Math: A Worked 1 MW Example

Numbers make this concrete. Here is a hypothetical but representative calculation. It covers a 1 MW open access contract for an HT industrial consumer in Maharashtra, drawing 15 Lakh units per year from the plant. Grid tariff is assumed at Rs 9.25 per unit, a typical landed HT industrial rate including demand charges and duty.

| Input | Third-Party PPA | Group Captive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PPA tariff (Rs/kWh) | 3.75 | 3.25 (levelised, net of equity returns) |
| Cross-subsidy surcharge | 1.62 | 0 (exempt) |
| Wheeling + transmission | 0.90 | 0.90 |
| Additional surcharge | 0.60 | 0 (waived) |
| Banking + scheduling + misc | 0.35 | 0.35 |
| **Landed cost (Rs/kWh)** | **7.22** | **4.50** |
| Grid tariff avoided (Rs/kWh) | 9.25 | 9.25 |
| Saving per unit (Rs) | 2.03 | 4.75 |
| Annual saving at 15 Lakh units | Rs 30.5 Lakh | Rs 71.3 Lakh |
| Upfront equity | Rs 0 | ~Rs 1.2 Crore |

Inputs shown: PPA tariff, state charges from the MERC tariff order framework, consumption volume, and grid tariff. Swap in your own state numbers before deciding. The direction of the result holds across most states. Group captive roughly doubles per-unit savings because CSS is the dominant wedge.

Two sensitivities deserve attention before you treat this table as a promise. First, the PPA escalation clause. A Rs 3.75 tariff escalating at 3% annually crosses Rs 5.00 by year 10. If grid tariffs rise slower, a third of the headline saving erodes. Negotiate a flat tariff or cap escalation below 2%. Second, capacity utilisation. If offtake drops to 10 Lakh units in a demand slowdown, third-party savings fall proportionally. Group captive fixed costs, like equity servicing and O&M share, do not. Size the contract to your contracted minimum load, not your optimistic peak.

## State-Wise Friendliness in 2026

Open access economics are decided in state tariff orders, not in the PPA. Here is the current read on major industrial states, compiled from SERC orders and market trackers.

- **Karnataka.** The friendliest large market. Wheeling is Rs 0.29 per kWh for HT, the developer ecosystem is mature, and solar land is abundant. KERC has published a draft roadmap to phase out cross-subsidy and CSS by FY 2029, according to Mercom India (2025). The long-term regulatory direction favours buyers.
- **Gujarat.** Stable and predictable. HT energy charges have stayed flat at Rs 4.30 per unit for three consecutive years under GERC. The grid baseline lands at Rs 7.50 to 8.50. Banking at Rs 1.50 per kWh is the main cost to model. Strong for rooftop-plus-open-access hybrid strategies.
- **Maharashtra.** Large C&I base with moderate charges. Wheeling is Rs 0.60 HT and CSS sits near Rs 1.60. Third-party PPAs still save Rs 2 or more per unit. Group captive is compelling.
- **Tamil Nadu.** Rich solar resource but a heavy charge stack. Wheeling is Rs 1.03 and CSS is Rs 2.06 for FY 2026-27 under TNERC. Third-party economics are thin. Group captive and intra-state captive structures dominate here.
- **Rajasthan, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh.** Attractive on the generation side with reasonable consumer-side charges. Telangana's landed costs fell in Q4 2025 on lower open access charges, according to Mercom India (2026).
- **Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Odisha.** Emerging markets. Odisha offers a 50% CSS concession for renewable open access under its 2022 RE policy, according to the OERC order for FY 2025-26. UP remains paperwork-heavy but the demand base is large.

The overall trend is worth noting. Landed open access costs rose in 12 of 15 tracked states in Q4 2025, according to Mercom India (2026). PPA tariffs firmed up across the market. The window where every state deal pencilled easily is closing. State selection now matters as much as tariff negotiation.

These figures shift every April when new tariff orders take effect. Build an annual review into your procurement calendar. Pull the latest SERC tariff order each year, update the charge stack in your model, and re-check the saving delta. A contract signed in 2026 will live through twenty such orders.

## Banking, Scheduling, and the Operational Reality

Two operational topics sink more open access deals than tariff disputes.

**Banking limits.** Most states cap banking at 10% to 30% of injected energy. Banked energy is settled monthly or annually. Carry-forward across financial years is often prohibited. If your load is flat across 24 hours but solar peaks at noon, you will bank 20% to 40% of generation. You pay banking charges on all of it. Model this before signing. In high banking charge states, pairing open access with a rooftop system for daytime baseload is usually cheaper.

**Scheduling and deviation.** Open access consumers above the threshold must submit day-ahead schedules. Deviations beyond permitted bands attract deviation settlement mechanism (DSM) charges. A good developer or aggregator manages scheduling on your behalf. Confirm this is written into the contract, not assumed.

> **What most C&I buyers get wrong.** They compare the PPA tariff to the grid tariff and stop. The real comparison is landed cost to grid tariff. Landed cost includes CSS, wheeling, banking on the banked share, and scheduling fees. We have seen proposals showing Rs 4 per unit savings shrink to Rs 1.20 once the full charge stack and a 30% banked share were modelled. Always ask the developer for a landed cost calculation with state tariff order references, not the headline tariff.

Metering configuration also matters. Open access projects use [gross metering or net metering variants](/blog/gross-metering-vs-net-metering/) at the injection and drawal points. The choice interacts with how banking is settled. The [ALMM List II exemption for net metering and open access](/blog/almm-list-ii-exemption-net-metering-open-access/) explains where module sourcing rules apply to open access projects.

## The Honest Tradeoffs

Open access is not free money. Five genuine tradeoffs deserve a frank look.

**Regulatory risk.** Charges are set annually by state regulators and can move against you mid-contract. CSS has risen in several states even as the Centre pushes for phase-down. A 25-year PPA signed on today's charge stack carries re-pricing risk no contract clause fully removes.

**Tenure lock-in.** Third-party PPAs run 10 to 25 years with termination penalties. If your factory relocates or scales down, exit is expensive. A grid tariff drop is rare but possible in states adding cheap renewable supply themselves.

**Approval friction.** DISCOMs lose revenue on every open access unit. Some process applications slowly. Timelines of 15 to 60 days on paper can stretch to 6 months in slower states. A developer with an operating track record in your state is worth a premium.

**Rooftop is still simpler.** For loads your roof can serve, on-site solar avoids the entire charge stack. The [Tirupur textile unit case study](/blog/case-study-tirupur-textile/) shows a C&I rooftop delivering sub-3-year payback with no regulator in the loop. Open access should cover what the roof cannot, not replace what it can. The [LCOE comparison for solar in India](/blog/lcoe-solar-india/) quantifies the gap between behind-the-meter and grid-wheeled solar.

**Tax treatment differs by structure.** In group captive, your equity sits in a project SPV. Returns come as dividends or buyback, taxed differently from a simple operating expense. A third-party PPA payment is a clean input cost. Run both structures past your CFO and tax advisor before signing. The [solar inverter depreciation rules in India](/blog/solar-inverter-depreciation-in-india/) cover the accelerated depreciation angle, which applies to captive equity but not to third-party PPAs.

## How to Evaluate an Open Access Proposal in 2026

When a developer proposal lands on your desk, run this checklist before commercial negotiation starts.

1. **Landed cost, not PPA tariff.** Demand a line-item landed cost calculation referencing your state's current tariff order. It should cover PPA tariff, wheeling, CSS, ASC, banking on the modelled banked share, scheduling, and duty. Compare it against your actual landed grid tariff, not the energy charge alone.
2. **Structure fit.** If you can deploy Rs 1 to 1.5 Crore per MW of equity and find co-consumers, group captive almost always wins on per-unit cost. If not, choose a third-party PPA with a capped escalation clause. Keep escalation at 2% to 3% annually, below expected grid tariff growth.
3. **Generation guarantee.** The contract should guarantee minimum annual generation at a defined P90 yield, with liquidated damages for shortfall. Otherwise you draw the gap from the grid at full tariff.
4. **Inverter and plant hardware.** Ask what inverters the plant uses. For plants in the 100 kW to multi-MW range built with string inverters, efficiency and grid tolerance drive plant availability. Qbits TLS and HT series string inverters run up to 100 kW and above. They carry 98% peak efficiency, IP66 protection, 180 to 270 V grid tolerance, and a 12-year full replacement warranty. That warranty directly protects the generation guarantee in developer-owned plants.
5. **Exit and transfer clauses.** Read the termination, change-in-law, and assignment clauses. Change-in-law protection for charge increases is the most valuable clause in a long-tenure PPA.
