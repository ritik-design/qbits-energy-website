Singapore gives solar customers a choice most markets do not, and it is a choice made almost entirely on modelling. The Solar Capability Transfer scheme pays roughly S$0.20 per kWh at a fixed rate. The Enhanced Central Intermediary Scheme pays the variable USEP wholesale rate, typically 10 to 20 cents per kWh, with EMC Market Participant registration and a 10 MWac cap. One offers certainty, the other exposure to wholesale movement, and the difference over a system's life is larger than most design decisions will produce. Yet most quoting tools cannot represent a variable wholesale rate at all. This guide covers what the Singapore stack has to do.

> **TL;DR**
> - SCT pays roughly S$0.20 per kWh fixed, registered with SP Services, no stated installation cap.
> - ECIS pays the variable USEP rate, typically 10 to 20 cents per kWh, needs EMC Market Participant registration, capped at 10 MWac.

> **What makes the Singapore stack unusual.** In most markets the proposal tool applies a tariff the customer already has. Here it has to help the customer choose between two schemes with different risk profiles, one of which is tied to a wholesale price. ## What Is Genuinely Singapore-Specific

**Two export schemes with different risk profiles.** SCT's fixed S$0.20 sits above the typical ECIS range, so on a straightforward comparison SCT looks better for most customers. ECIS becomes interesting at scale, where wholesale exposure may be acceptable and the administrative burden of EMC registration is proportionally smaller. The honest analysis shows both with the volatility visible, rather than picking one.

The modelling requirement is specific: representing ECIS properly means carrying a variable rate rather than an average, because averaging a volatile price hides exactly the risk the customer is being asked to accept.

**The 1 MWac licence threshold.** Below it, no EMA Generation Licence. At or above, a full licence via the ELISE portal taking an estimated three to six months. A design that crosses the line during scoping has changed programme substantially, and that should surface in the tool rather than in a conversation with EMA.

**Universal LEW requirement.** Every installation needs a Licensed Electrical Worker to submit the Electrical Installation application. No size exemption, so it belongs in the standard project template.

**Density and inter-building shading.** Singapore's obstruction is usually the neighbouring tower, frequently off-property and outside anyone's control. That makes shading analysis both harder and more consequential than on a typical suburban roof, and it argues for hourly module-level modelling rather than a geometric estimate.

**SS 638 and the IEC set.** Wiring, inverter and module standards respectively.

## Where Singapore Teams Overbuy And Underbuy

**Underbought: variable-rate modelling for ECIS.** The clearest gap. A tool that can only hold a fixed export price cannot represent one of the two available schemes.

**Underbought: inter-building shading at module level.** Given the built environment, this is where design accuracy is won or lost, and geometric estimates on a dense site are frequently wrong in both directions.

**Underbought: threshold awareness at 1 MWac.** A three to six month approval process is not something to discover late.

**Overbought: platforms optimised for large ground-mount.** Singapore's constraint is roof area and neighbouring towers, not land. Tools built around utility-scale row optimisation solve a problem the market does not have.

**Overbought: seats for viewers.** Universal.

## Worked Example: The Same Rooftop Under Both Schemes

An illustrative comparison, not a specific customer project. A commercial rooftop exporting a meaningful share of its generation.

| | Under SCT | Under ECIS |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Export rate | Roughly S$0.20 per kWh, fixed | USEP, typically 10 to 20 cents, variable |
| Predictability | High | Exposed to wholesale movement |
| Registration | SP Services | EMC Market Participant |
| Installation cap | None stated | 10 MWac |
| Administrative burden | Lower | Higher |
| Typical better fit | Most commercial and smaller installations | Larger installations able to carry price exposure |

On the headline numbers SCT's fixed rate sits above the middle of the ECIS range, which makes it the straightforward choice for most customers. The reason to model both anyway is that the comparison is not purely about the expected value. It is about whether the customer wants a known number for twenty years or is willing to trade certainty for potential upside, and that is their decision to make with good information rather than yours to make on their behalf.

The modelling point worth stressing is the second row. Presenting ECIS at an averaged rate makes it look like a slightly worse fixed scheme, which misrepresents it entirely. The whole character of ECIS is its variability, and a proposal that averages it away has removed the only thing the customer needs to think about.
