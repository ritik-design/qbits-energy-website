## What Aurora Solar Is and Who It Was Built For

Aurora is a cloud platform for designing residential solar systems remotely and selling them. You enter an address, the platform pulls imagery and LIDAR data, builds a 3D model of the roof with obstructions and ridges, lays out modules, runs a shading and production analysis, applies the local utility tariff, and produces a customer-facing proposal.

The design target was clear from the start: a US residential installer who wants to quote a homeowner without a truck roll. Everything in the product points at that user. The remote site assessment exists to remove the site visit. Sales Mode exists to close in the living room. The tariff library exists because US residential payback is a utility bill calculation, not an energy calculation.

That focus is why Aurora is good. Tools that try to serve every market and every project size at once end up shallow in all of them. Aurora went deep on one buyer and won that buyer. It is also, as we will get to, exactly why the fit degrades once you step outside that profile.

## Aurora Solar Pricing in 2026

Aurora does not publish a public price list, so its pricing is quote-based and reported figures vary. Third-party trackers and buyers reporting their own quotes put the core design product at roughly $159 to $259 per user per month depending on tier, contract length, and negotiation. On an annual basis that would be about $1,908 to $3,108 per user. None of that is a quotation, and the only figure that binds anyone is the one Aurora puts in writing for your seat count.

The complication is the packaging. Aurora describes AI Mode, the automated design assistant, and Sales Mode, the customer-facing presentation and close workflow, as separately licensed rather than bundled into the base design product, and Lite Mode seats for canvassers as lower cost with a restricted feature set. So a team rarely buys one thing at one price. A designer needs the full design seat and probably AI Mode; a salesperson needs Sales Mode; a canvasser needs Lite. ## What Aurora Solar Genuinely Does Well

Credit where it is due. These are real strengths, not politeness.

**Remote roof modelling.** Aurora's roof capture from aerial imagery and LIDAR is the feature that built the company. In our own use it has been accurate enough that teams stop measuring standard residential roofs in person, and remote assessment is now normal practice across the US market. Aurora did as much as anyone to drive that shift.

**Shading analysis that people trust.** The shading engine produces results that survive scrutiny from finance partners and engineering reviewers. That trust matters more than the underlying math, because a number nobody argues with saves you a week per deal.

**The sales presentation.** Sales Mode is built by people who understand that the person clicking through the proposal is a homeowner on a sofa, not an engineer. Layouts, savings graphs, and financing comparisons are legible to a non-technical buyer. Most engineering-first tools are bad at this and know it.

**US code and tariff depth.** NEC rules and US utility tariffs are wired in properly rather than bolted on. Rapid shutdown, string sizing at design temperature, and jurisdiction-specific requirements are handled as first-class concerns. The tariff library is one of the better ones in the market.

**Brand trust.** Aurora output carries weight with US lenders, utilities, and national sales channels. That is not a technical advantage, but it is a commercial one, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. If your finance partner is used to seeing Aurora reports, that friction removal has real value.

## Where Aurora Solar Falls Short

**Per-seat monthly pricing scales badly.** This is the complaint we hear most, and it is structural rather than a matter of the number being high. Design software cost should track project volume, not headcount. Aurora's model ties cost to seats, so a team that adds a junior designer, a coordinator who occasionally needs to open a project, or a seasonal seller pays full freight for partial use. Teams end up sharing logins, which is both a contract problem and an audit problem.

**The advertised price is rarely the paid price.** Splitting AI Mode and Sales Mode out of the base licence is a legitimate commercial choice, and it lets a design-only team avoid paying for a sales tool. But it makes budgeting hard. Buyers anchor on $159, build a plan around it, and discover the working configuration costs closer to double. Ask for the full quote with your actual seat mix before you compare anything.

**Positioned around US net metering, which most markets do not have.** This is the limitation that decides the buy for anyone outside the United States. Aurora's financial modelling is built for a market where excess generation is credited against a retail utility bill under a net metering or net billing rule, and where NEC governs the electrical design. India runs PM Surya Ghar subsidy math and state DISCOM net metering caps. Australia runs AS/NZS and state feed-in tariffs. Germany runs Einspeisevergütung. None of those map cleanly onto the US assumptions, so international teams typically rebuild the financial half of every proposal in a spreadsheet. That is not a bug in Aurora. It is a product that was scoped for one market being asked to work in another.

**Heavier than simple residential work requires.** A 6 kW system on a clean gable roof does not need remote LIDAR reconstruction, an AI design pass, and a multi-scenario financing presentation. For installers whose mix is dominated by straightforward jobs, a lot of the platform is capability you carry rather than capability you use, and the interface complexity slows new hires down.

> **What most buyers get wrong.** They evaluate Aurora on design accuracy, which it will pass, and skip the seat-mix question, which is what actually decides whether the contract hurts. Price the configuration your team will really run, including the people who open projects twice a month, before you compare it to anything else.

## Who Should Buy Aurora Solar

Buy Aurora if most of these describe you.

- You run residential solar in the United States and volume is 150 or more installs a year.
- Your sales motion happens in front of the customer and a strong presentation converts.
- Your team is already trained on Aurora, and retraining cost is real.
- A lender, national channel, or utility partner prefers or expects Aurora output.
- Your seat count is stable, so per-seat pricing is predictable rather than punishing.

At that profile, $14,000 to $18,000 a year is a small fraction of revenue and the time saved on remote assessment pays it back. Arguing against Aurora for this buyer would be a sales pitch, not a review.

## Who Should Not Buy Aurora Solar

- **Teams of one to five doing moderate volume.** The per-seat cost is a much larger share of overhead, and the automation you are paying for saves less time when you design ten systems a month rather than eighty.
- **Anyone selling outside the United States.** The compliance and tariff foundations do not transfer. You will do the financial work twice.
- **Commercial and utility-heavy pipelines.** Aurora can handle C&I, but the platform's investment has gone into residential. Engineering teams generally get more from HelioScope or PVsyst on large layouts and yield reporting.
- **Teams whose bottleneck is proposals, not design.** If your designs are simple and your problem is turning them into documents fast, you are buying a lot of engineering depth to solve a document problem.
- **Anyone who needs a bankable yield report as standard output.** Where a financing agreement names PVsyst, teams typically produce that report separately, which means two licences and two workflows. Check what your finance chain will accept before assuming one tool covers it.

## Verdict

Aurora Solar is a good product that is priced for a specific customer, and it is worth the money for that customer. If you are a high-volume US residential installer with a stable team and a sales motion that closes in the home, Aurora will not be the thing holding you back, and the remote assessment alone justifies the contract.

For everyone else, the case gets harder to make honestly. The per-seat structure punishes small and variable teams. The add-on model means you cannot trust the headline price when you budget. And the whole product is engineered around US net metering and NEC, which is a real advantage inside the United States and a real handicap anywhere else. Those are not fixable by negotiation; they are what the product is.

That is why our recommendation for most buyers is SurgePV. Not because Aurora is bad, but because the arithmetic on a five-seat team is roughly $6,495 against a five-figure Aurora total on the reported bands, for the same core workflow, with proposals and multi-market compliance included instead of purchased separately. If your volume and market make Aurora's premium pay for itself, buy Aurora with confidence. If it does not, you are paying for depth aimed at someone else's business.

Three things to do next:

- Get a full Aurora quote with your real seat mix, including AI Mode and Sales Mode, before comparing anything.
- Rebuild three recent projects in a competing tool and compare output quality, not feature lists.
- Decide whether the tool needs to handle any market outside the United States in the next two years, because that answer decides the shortlist on its own.
