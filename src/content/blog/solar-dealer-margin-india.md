There is **no dependable single solar dealer margin percentage for India**. Public pages often use the word `margin` for different things: product resale spread, installation margin, project gross margin, commission, back-end rebate, or net business profit.

As of 26 September 2026, Qbits does not publish a fixed dealer margin. Several other solar companies publish either a broad percentage, a qualitative promise, or a payout mechanism. Those disclosures are not directly comparable. A dealer should use them only to frame questions, then model the actual written offer.

The page metadata showed an update on 27 July 2026 |
| [Spectra Solar channel-partner page](https://spectrasolarpower.com/channel-partner-with-us) | It says the agreed margin is credited after the customer has paid the project amount in full, with possible bonuses tied to collections and quarterly targets | It does not publish the agreed percentage. This is a project-closing channel model, not proof of an inventory dealer's inverter-resale margin |

This is why an attractive percentage can still be unusable. The reader may not know what was sold, which costs sit above or below the line, whether the amount is earned immediately, or whether the statement applies to that manufacturer's current programme.

The Tata Power figure is the clearest example. The source labels it as project-execution margin. A rooftop project can include modules, inverter, structure, wiring, design, labour, approvals, travel, and follow-up work. Applying that percentage to an inverter purchase and resale would change both the revenue base and the cost base.

The honest conclusion is narrow: some public pages describe margin potential, but none of the disclosures above gives a comparable current inverter-dealer rate card.

## Discount, markup, margin and profit are different numbers

Before comparing offers, name the number being compared. Four labels are often treated as interchangeable even though they answer different questions.

| Term | What it measures | Why it can mislead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Discount from list price | Difference between a stated list price and the dealer invoice price, divided by the list price | The list price may not be the price customers will pay |
| Markup | Difference between sale value and cost, divided by cost | Markup and gross margin use different denominators, so the percentages are not equal |
| Product gross margin | Difference between net sale value and landed product cost, divided by net sale value | It excludes operating costs unless they are deliberately included in landed cost |
| Contribution | Net sale value less product cost and the variable costs of making that sale | It still excludes fixed overhead and some finance or tax effects |
| Net profit margin | Profit after the business's full recognised costs, divided by revenue | It depends on the dealer's own operation, not just the manufacturer offer |

For a product-resale decision, start with values excluding recoverable taxes and have an accountant confirm the treatment. Let:

- `S` be the net sale value actually achievable;
- `P` be the supplier invoice value on the same tax basis;
- `F` be inward freight, insurance, unloading, and other product-landing costs;
- `V` be sale-specific costs such as delivery, payment fees, and variable service expense; and
- `R` be a rebate already earned under the written rules and reasonably collectable.

Then a working contribution calculation is:

`Dealer contribution = S - P - F - V + R`

`Contribution margin = Dealer contribution / S`

Do not put a target rebate into `R` merely because it appears in a sales presentation. First verify the qualifying products, measurement period, target, exclusions, return adjustments, credit-note process, and payment trigger.

## Why a large list-price discount can produce a small realised margin

A list price is a reference value. It is not evidence of what the market will pay. If local competitors sell below that list price, the dealer's real revenue base is lower.

The same problem appears with turnover. High turnover can coexist with low profit when stock moves slowly, credit costs rise, customer collections slip, price cuts occur, or service work is unpaid. Neither a list-price discount nor annual sales volume proves the profit left in the business.

Use three sale-value cases instead of one optimistic number:

1. the normal achieved value supported by recent quotations;
2. a lower value needed to clear ageing stock or meet local competition; and
3. a return or cancellation case where freight, damage, restocking, or credit-note delays apply.

Repeat the calculation for each product family. A blended headline percentage can hide a poor result on the models you expect to sell most.

## The written margin schedule should answer these questions

A usable commercial offer needs more than one percentage. Ask the manufacturer to fill every row or mark it unavailable.

| Commercial term | Evidence to request | Decision risk if it remains vague |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product scope | Exact models, customer types, channels, and effective date | A headline rate may apply only to selected products or sales |
| Price base | Dealer invoice schedule and the value used as the percentage denominator | Margin cannot be recalculated or compared |
| Upfront discount | Invoice treatment, quantity slab, and tax basis | A list-price discount may be mistaken for realised margin |
| Back-end rebate | Formula, target, measurement period, exclusions, and approval owner | Forecast profit may depend on a threshold that is missed |
| Payout trigger | Invoice, delivery, customer payment, collection, installation, or quarter close | Earned income may arrive after the dealer needs cash |
| Reversals | Treatment of returns, cancellations, credit notes, bad debt, and target shortfalls | Previously booked margin may be reversed |
| Freight and damage | Who pays dispatch, insurance, unloading, redelivery, and transit loss | Landed cost is understated |
| Price changes | Notice period, open-order treatment, and price protection for stock | Existing inventory can lose value after a price cut |
| Credit | Limit, due date, security, interest, suspension, and withdrawal rules | Credit is mistaken for profit or permanent funding |
| Stock exit | Rotation, obsolete models, return conditions, deductions, and termination stock | Slow stock becomes trapped cash |
| Warranty and service | Claim intake, diagnosis, parts, labour, travel, freight, reimbursement, and evidence | The dealer absorbs support costs not shown in the product spread |
| Channel conflict | Direct sales, online pricing, other partners, lead ownership, and territory rules | The achievable selling value falls below the model |
| Settlement records | Statements, credit notes, portal reports, audit rights, and dispute process | The dealer cannot reconcile what was earned and paid |

The [solar dealer agreement checklist](/blog/solar-dealer-agreement-checklist/) covers how these schedules connect to territory, stock, termination, liability, and post-termination obligations. Commercial terms belong in the approved documents, not only in messages or calls.

## Model cash timing as carefully as the percentage

Two offers with the same stated margin can create different cash pressure. One may apply the benefit on the invoice. Another may issue a credit note after a quarter. A third may pay only after the end customer pays in full.

Build a dated cash-flow line for each step:

1. purchase order and any deposit;
2. supplier invoice and tax event;
3. dispatch, freight, and receipt;
4. customer invoice and collection;
5. return or service cost, if any;
6. target measurement and approval;
7. rebate credit note or payment; and
8. reconciliation and dispute deadline.

Then stress-test delayed customer collection, slower stock movement, a missed rebate slab, a price revision, and a warranty case. Use only the actual proposed terms. The application form asking about working capital does not mean Qbits has approved credit, stock returns, a margin, or any other commercial facility.

## What to ask Qbits before making a dealer commitment

The current public Qbits page is an application route. It is not a commercial offer. If a dealer application moves into discussion, request a dated written pack covering:

- the proposed role and the exact products and customers in scope;
- the current invoice-price schedule and its validity period;
- each discount, rebate, target, approval, and reversal formula;
- payout and credit-note timing;
- payment terms, security, interest, and credit suspension;
- freight, damage, returns, stock rotation, and price protection;
- territory, online sales, direct accounts, and lead ownership;
- warranty, technical-support, and service cost allocation; and
- termination treatment for stock, open orders, receivables, and unpaid rebates.

Keep the dealer discussion separate from a distributor, EPC, or system-integrator offer. The [role comparison guide](/blog/dealer-vs-distributor-vs-epc/) explains why the revenue base, inventory duty, project responsibility, and service exposure change with the role.

## A margin offer is decision-ready only when you can reproduce it

The practical test is simple: can your finance team take one completed sale, apply the written schedule, and reach the same amount as the manufacturer's statement?

If the answer depends on an undefined list price, an oral target, an unnamed approval, or a future credit note with no due date, the margin is not ready for a business decision. Record it as unresolved.
