Most solar software reviews grade tools on engineering accuracy, which is the wrong first question for a lot of buyers. A residential deal rarely collapses because the shading model was off by two percent. It collapses because the homeowner did not understand what they were signing, could not compare a loan against a lease, and quietly went cold. Enact Solar is one of the few platforms that treats that comprehension problem as the main event rather than an afterthought. This review looks at what Enact is, what it does genuinely well, where its depth runs out, and who should and should not be buying it in 2026.

## What Enact Solar Actually Is

Enact Systems sells a cloud platform that follows a solar project from the first homeowner conversation through to long-term system monitoring. You put in an address, build a 3D roof model from satellite imagery and surface data, lay out modules, run shading, apply a tariff, and generate a proposal with e-signature. After the install, the same account tracks production and system health, and the homeowner gets an app.

That end-to-end span matters more than any single feature. Most tools in this category stop at the signed proposal. Enact deliberately keeps going, because its view is that the customer relationship, not the design file, is the asset.

The company states coverage in more than 35 countries. That is a meaningful differentiator against proposal tools scoped to a single market, and it changes who the product is for. Enact is aimed less at the US-only residential shop that Aurora was designed around, and more at installers who operate across borders or in markets that the large US platforms never modelled properly.

## The Problem Enact Is Actually Solving

Here is the part that gets underrated in software reviews. Homeowner comprehension is a real commercial problem, and it is expensive.

A residential solar buyer is being asked to make a twenty-year financial decision using vocabulary they have never used before. Net metering. Time-of-use. Degradation. Escalator. Payback period. Most of them have also been quoted by two other companies whose numbers do not agree, using different assumptions that nobody explained.

The predictable result is delay. Not rejection, delay, which is worse, because it consumes sales time without producing revenue.

Enact's design answer to this is to make the money legible. Cash versus loan versus lease versus PPA, side by side, with a cash-flow view rather than a single payback number. Savings driven by an actual tariff rather than a flat rate assumption. Battery value shown as charge and discharge behaviour across a day, which is the only way most people ever understand why storage is worth anything on a time-of-use plan.

That is a genuine product insight, and it is fair to give Enact credit for building around it instead of shipping another design canvas.

## What Enact Solar Does Well

**Financing comparison as a first-class feature.** Loan, lease, and PPA sit next to cash in the same view. Plenty of tools can produce a payback figure. Fewer make the choice between financing structures easy to reason about, and that choice is often the actual decision the homeowner is stuck on.

**Tariff-aware savings rather than flat-rate estimates.** Enact models complex tariffs and net billing rather than multiplying generation by one average rate. This matters because the difference between those two methods can be large on a time-of-use plan, and a proposal built on a flat rate tends to fall apart the moment the customer compares it to a real bill.

**Battery behaviour you can show, not just describe.** Visualising charge and discharge cycles for a chosen day is a small feature with a large sales effect. Storage is the hardest part of a residential proposal to justify verbally. A picture of the battery filling in the afternoon and discharging through the evening peak does the work that three paragraphs of explanation cannot.

**Proposal output that is presentable.** Customisable templates, dynamic graphs, 3D imagery of the actual roof, and DocuSign for signature. This is not the deepest proposal builder on the market, but it produces documents that look professional and that a non-technical reader can follow. That is the bar that matters here.

**Life-after-install coverage.** Asset management across inverter brands, production monitoring, and health alerts. For an installer whose growth depends on referrals and service revenue, a customer who still opens your app in year three is worth more than one who forgot your name in month two.

**Breadth of market coverage.** Stated presence in more than 35 countries is a real advantage over tools scoped to one regulatory regime. If you sell in a market the US-first platforms ignore, that alone may shorten your shortlist.

## Where Enact Solar Falls Short

Every criticism below follows from the same trade-off. A product that puts the sales conversation first has less room for engineering surface. That is a legitimate choice, not a failure, but it decides who should buy.

**Engineering depth is lighter than dedicated design tools.** If your work involves complicated roof geometry, unusual stringing, detailed electrical documentation, or layouts that need to survive an engineering review, test that specific work before you commit, because the product is positioned around the sales conversation rather than the engineering deliverable. Teams in that position often keep a second tool and accept the double entry. That is an assessment of positioning rather than a measured audit, so confirm the current engineering feature set with Enact directly.

**Coverage of a market is not the same as depth in it.** This is the nuance most comparison tables get wrong. Enact does operate across many countries, so blanket claims that it is US-only are inaccurate. But breadth and depth are different things. Tariff structures, incentive schemes, and compliance rules are deep, local, and change often, and no vendor maintains equal depth everywhere. Outside a vendor's core markets, expect the local library to be thinner and to need checking. The honest test is not "does it support my country" but "does it get my state's tariff, my incentive scheme, and my interconnection rules right today". Verify that with a real project rather than a sales slide.

**Not a substitute for bankable simulation.** If a financier or an independent engineer names a specific yield report, the question stops being about software quality and becomes about contract acceptance. Enact does not position itself as the tool that produces that artefact, so plan for a separate step rather than discovering the gap during a financing review.

**Residential gravity.** The product is marketed most heavily around deals where a financing comparison closes the sale, which mostly means residential and smaller commercial. Larger C&I work is supported, but it is not the case the public positioning leads with, so test a large project before you buy seats for one.

**Pricing is hard to pin down.** Enact does not publish a complete public price list, and third-party trackers report figures that do not agree with each other. That is a common pattern in this category, but it means you cannot budget from a web search. Get a written quote against your real seat count.

## Who Should Buy Enact Solar

Buy it if most of these describe your business.

- Your pipeline is mostly residential and small commercial, and deals stall at the homeowner decision rather than at the design stage.
- Financing options are central to how you sell, and you need loan, lease, and PPA compared credibly in front of the customer.
- You attach storage often and struggle to explain its value on a time-of-use tariff.
- You want post-install monitoring and a customer app without buying and integrating a separate platform.
- You operate in a market the large US-first platforms never modelled well, and breadth of country coverage matters to you.
- You are a small team that would rather have one account covering sale to service than three tools stitched together.

For that buyer, Enact is a reasonable purchase and the criticism in this review is mostly irrelevant to you.

## Who Should Not Buy Enact Solar

- **Engineering-led teams.** If your output is drawings, stringing detail, and documentation that has to survive review, check whether Enact covers it today, because on current positioning most teams in that position run a second tool, and paying for a sales layer you are not using is poor economics.
- **Anyone whose projects are financed against a named yield report.** That is a contract requirement and no proposal tool solves it.
- **C&I-heavy pipelines.** Possible, but not the product's strength. Run a real large project through it before deciding.
- **Teams that need certainty on local compliance depth in a specific market.** Confirm your own market with a live project rather than assuming that stated coverage means full depth.
- **Buyers who want a single tool for both bankable engineering and sales.** That combination exists elsewhere, and Enact is not trying to be it.

## Verdict

Enact Solar is a better product than the standard software-review framing gives it credit for, because it is competing on a dimension most reviews do not measure. Homeowner comprehension is where residential deals are lost, and Enact treats that as the problem to solve rather than as a template to skin. The financing comparison, the tariff-aware savings, and the visual battery behaviour are real strengths, and the post-install monitoring layer is genuinely uncommon in this category.

The limits are the direct cost of that focus. Engineering depth is lighter than tools built for engineers, and teams doing complex or lender-facing work will keep a second licence. Country coverage is broad, but breadth and local depth are not the same thing, so verify your own market rather than trusting a count. And it is not the tool that produces a bankable yield report, which is a contract question rather than a quality one.

So the recommendation splits cleanly. If your bottleneck is the customer conversation, Enact is a sensible buy and you should test it against your own close rate. If your bottleneck is engineering output, or if you want the proposal and the design to come from a single record with multi-market compliance behind it, SurgePV is the more efficient shape at $1,299 to $1,899 per user per year. If a lender names a tool, buy that tool and stop comparing.

Three things to do next:

- Get a written quote from Enact against your real seat count, because published third-party figures disagree and none of them is your invoice.
- Run one real project in your own market end to end and check the tariff, the incentive, and the compliance handling against what you know to be true locally.
- Decide honestly whether your losses come from bad designs or from confused customers, because that answer picks the tool on its own.
