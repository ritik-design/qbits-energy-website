Solar for the ceramic industry in Morbi is an easy match in one respect and a hard one in two others. Kilns, spray dryers, ball mills and presses run around the clock, so every unit the roof produces gets used on site, on the same day, inside the 07:00 to 18:00 set-off window. The hard parts are these. A roof can only carry a plant sized for a fraction of that load. The GERC rules also charge a banking fee of Rs 1.10 to Rs 1.50 on every solar unit you consume. And solar leaves the gas or propane bill alone, which is the larger part of a ceramic unit's energy cost.

This guide is for owners and managers of tile, wall tile and sanitaryware units on an HT (high-tension) connection from PGVCL who are about to ask an EPC (engineering, procurement and construction contractor) for a quote. It reads the tariff and regulation text, adds PVGIS yield for Morbi, and works one payback with every assumption on the page. We have no Qbits customer, price or installed site to cite, so none appears. We also make no claim about demand for solar in Morbi, since no dated source for one turned up.

## What can solar change for the ceramic industry's energy bill?

A rooftop plant makes electricity and does not fire a kiln. The energy mix of the Morbi cluster is set out in a cluster profile on the SamEEEksha platform, which comes from the BEE-SME programme and was contributed by SEE-Tech Solutions and TERI. The profile is undated and older than today's cluster: it counts 459 industrial units, against the roughly 790 gas-using units Gujarat Gas reported in February 2026. Read it for proportions only.

| Fuel in the profile | Share of cluster energy |
| --- | --- |
| Natural gas | 51% |
| Lignite | 29% |
| Charcoal | 10% |
| Electricity | 9% (about 1.2 billion kWh a year) |
| Diesel | 1% |

The same profile says energy is 30% to 35% of the total cost of ceramic production, that more than half the thermal energy comes from natural gas, and that charcoal and lignite were mainly used in spray dryers. It lists the kiln and the spray dryer as using both thermal and electrical energy, and the air compressor, glazing, slip section and press as electrical. Those electrical loads are what a rooftop plant offsets.

The fuel picture has moved since then. DeshGujarat reported on 26 February 2026 that the National Green Tribunal's Pune bench dismissed appeals by 337 Morbi and Wankaner units against Rs 122.05 crore of environmental damage compensation for using banned coal gasifiers, and that units could resume only after switching to piped natural gas. DeshGujarat also reported on 15 April 2026, citing a Gujarat Gas press release, that about 377 units were on piped gas and about 415 on propane before the crisis in February, and that about 142 units were operating that day.

Two lessons follow for a solar decision.

- **Judge the saving against the electricity line only.** If a proposal shows solar cutting "energy cost" by a percentage, ask which line of the bill it means. The gas or propane invoice does not move.
- **Plan for stoppages.** A wire report dated 19 May 2026 quotes the state Energy Minister, Rushikesh Patel, saying that 83 units were operating in Morbi on 31 March 2026 and more than 670 by 17 May, after many units paused production in the second half of March. A plant that sits idle for weeks still has a roof full of panels. Under GERC's HT rule, that output is exported at the surplus rate, which we cover below.

## What is a solar unit worth under PGVCL's HT tariff?

A ceramic unit contracting 100 kVA or more for regular supply is on Rate HTP-1 in the GERC schedule effective 1 April 2026. These are its figures. Smaller sanitaryware and ancillary units on LT supply are on other rates in the same schedule.

| Item | Rate HTP-1 |
| --- | --- |
| Demand charge, billing demand up to contract demand | Rs 150 per kVA a month for the first 500 kVA, Rs 260 for the next 500 kVA, Rs 475 above 1,000 kVA |
| Demand charge, above contract demand | Rs 555 per kVA a month |
| Energy charge, entire consumption | 400 paise per unit up to 500 kVA of billing demand, 420 paise from above 500 kVA to 2,500 kVA, 430 paise above 2,500 kVA |
| Peak-period addition, 07:00 to 11:00 and 18:00 to 22:00 | 45 paise per unit up to 500 kVA of billing demand, 85 paise above 500 kVA |
| Discount, 11:00 to 17:00 | 60 paise per unit |
| Billing demand | Highest of actual maximum demand, 85% of contract demand, or 100 kVA |
| Rebate for supply at 11 kV or 22 kV | 1% on energy charges (1.5% at 33 or 66 kV) |
| Power factor | Penalty of 1% of the energy charge for each 1% below 90% down to 85%, then 2% for each 1% below 85%. Rebate of 0.5% for each 1% above 95% |

Source: GERC tariff schedule effective 1 April 2026, paras 11.1 to 11.10. The schedule leaves out FPPAS (fuel and power purchase price adjustment), electricity duty and other taxes, which your bill adds.

Solar makes most of its units between 11:00 and 17:00, which is the discounted window, so a solar unit saves less than the headline energy charge. We binned PVGIS's hourly series for Morbi for 2015 to 2019. About 74% of annual generation falls between 11:00 and 17:00, about 20% between 07:00 and 11:00, and about 6% in the shoulder hour from 17:00 to 18:00 and either side of the window. The binning depends on how the timestamps are read, so treat those shares as good to a few percentage points.

| Time band | Share of solar output | Rate, billing demand up to 500 kVA | Rate, billing demand above 500 kVA |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 07:00 to 11:00 | 20% | 445 paise | 505 paise |
| 11:00 to 17:00 | 74% | 340 paise | 360 paise |
| Other hours in the set-off window | 6% | 400 paise | 420 paise |
| Weighted value of a solar unit | | Rs 3.65 | Rs 3.93 |

We left out the 1% supply-voltage rebate, which would trim those figures by a few paise. A payback sum built on the headline 420 paise overstates the saving by about 60 paise a unit.

**Solar does not reliably cut the demand charge.** Billing demand is at least 85% of contract demand. A unit with 1,000 kVA contract demand pays for at least 850 kVA every month, which is 500 times Rs 150 plus 350 times Rs 260, or Rs 1,66,000 a month, whatever the roof does. Maximum demand can also be set in a quarter-hour when a cloud crosses the array while the presses start, so we count no demand saving.

## Which GERC rules apply to a ceramic unit's rooftop plant?

A rooftop plant on a ceramic unit you own is a captive project under clause 9.2 of the GERC regulations. Net metering runs from 1 kW to 1,000 kW, and gross metering is allowed above 10 kW up to 1,000 kW. Clause 9.2.3 and the capacity clause set no limit below sanctioned load or contract demand for captive consumers. Our [state-wise net-metering limits guide](/blog/net-metering-capacity-limits-commercial-by-state/) shows how Gujarat's ceiling compares with other states. Three clauses drive the sums for an HT ceramic unit, and the table keeps only those. The [diamond polishing guide](/blog/solar-for-diamond-polishing-units-surat/) reads all of clause 9.2 line by line.

| Clause | Rule for HT supply | Effect on a ceramic unit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Same-day set-off (9.2.6) | A day's solar output is set off against HT consumption between 07:00 and 18:00 on that same day, and the DISCOM buys the remainder at the specified rates | Set-off is per day, not per billing cycle. On a normal 24 by 7 day nothing is left over |
| Surplus rate (9.2.9) | MSME manufacturing enterprises receive Rs 2.25 per unit for 5 years, then 75% of a GUVNL bid average. Other consumers receive 75% of that average | A stoppage day exports at this rate. The current GUVNL average was not found, so no non-MSME figure is given |
| Banking charge (9.2.11) | Demand-based consumers pay Rs 1.50 per solar unit consumed. MSME manufacturing units, and consumers that are not demand-based, pay Rs 1.10 | The charge falls on every solar unit you use and comes straight off the saving |

Captive status must also be proved each financial year to the Chief Electrical Inspector and the DISCOM (clause 9.2.5). A failed test turns the energy into third-party sale with cross subsidy and additional surcharge, so keep ownership and consumption records.

Two cautions apply. The regulation text we read does not say how "MSME manufacturing" is decided for the banking charge, so ask PGVCL in writing which charge your Udyam registration and activity code attract. We also read the amendments up to November 2025 and found nothing that changes clause 9.2.11, but we did not search separate GERC orders. If PGVCL quotes a different number, use theirs.

The rule that matters most for a 24 by 7 plant is the one that never bites. Under same-day set-off, a stoppage day sends that day's solar output to the grid at the surplus rate, which costs a kiln operation little. Here a ceramic unit does better under these rules than the Surat units in our [power loom and textile guide](/blog/solar-for-power-looms-textile-units-surat/).

## Is the roof or the load the limit for a 24 by 7 ceramic plant?

Take an illustrative HT unit with 1,000 kVA contract demand and an average draw of 600 kW across all 24 hours. That is an assumption for arithmetic, not a Morbi norm, and a real unit's draw swings with press cycles, mill loading and kiln stoppages.

| Quantity | Illustrative figure | How it was worked out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Consumption inside 07:00 to 18:00 | 6,600 kWh a day | 600 kW times 11 hours |
| Output of a 500 kWp plant in the sunniest months | About 2,400 kWh a day | 500 kWp times about 160 kWh per kWp per month, less 10%, divided by 30 |
| Share of in-window consumption absorbed | About 36% | 2,400 divided by 6,600 |
| Plant needed to absorb all in-window units | About 1,400 kWp | 6,600 divided by about 4.8 kWh per kWp a day |
| Solar share of the whole year's grid units | About 13% | 7,00,000 units divided by 52.6 lakh units |

So the self-consumption problem that shapes a day-shift plant does not arise. The question becomes how many kWp of roof you can safely and usefully fill. If your meter data shows a lower and more variable draw than 600 kW, run the same test on your own half-hourly data, using the lowest-load day of the month, not the average.

## What do dust and heat do to panels on a ceramic roof?

**Roof area.** Production sheds, press halls and warehouses give a ceramic unit more roof than most factories, but not all of it is usable. Subtract the roof lights, ventilators, spray dryer and kiln stacks, access walkways and the zones you decide to keep clear of hot exhaust. Ask the EPC for a drawing that shows panel count and kWp on each roof section, not a single headline figure. Panel count is kWp divided by panel wattage, and you can check it yourself.

**Roof type and load.** A metal sheet roof on steel trusses, an RCC slab on an office block and an older cement-sheet roof each need different fixings. Ask for the dead load of the proposed structure in kg per square metre, the clamp type for the sheet profile, and wind uplift. Have a qualified structural engineer sign the check, including the age and corrosion of sheets and purlins. Roofs that are fragile underfoot need walkways, and the cleaning crew needs a safe way on and off.

**Orientation and shading.** Several roof sections facing different ways will need several MPPT inputs. Keeping sections on separate MPPT (maximum power point tracking) inputs is easier with an inverter that has 6 or 8 of them by model, such as the [QB 70 to 110KTLC-Pro](/our-products/QB-70-110KTLC-Pro/), which is listed with an IP66 enclosure. For the temperature range and any derating, read the datasheet curve for the exact model.

**Dust.** Kiln halls, spray dryers, body preparation and glaze lines all make fine dust. We found no published soiling measurement for Morbi, so we give no loss percentage. A named study does show that the kind of dust matters as well as the amount. John, Warade, Tamizhmani and Kottantharayil (IEEE Journal of Photovoltaics, 2016) deposited dust collected at Indian sites onto silicon cells in a lab at the same density of 3 g per square metre. Mumbai dust cut output by 17.1% and Jodhpur dust by 9.8%. That is lab data from other places, so it says nothing about ceramic dust, but it is a reason not to borrow a "typical" figure.

Our worked payback takes 10% off the PVGIS yield for dust, heat and downtime together. That 10% is our assumption, not a measurement.

**What to do about it.** Log array output against a cleaning schedule through your first dry season and set the interval from your own data. Compare an array near a spray dryer stack with one far from it, and plan a water point, drainage and safe access at the design stage.

**Heat.** The air above a kiln hall can be hot. Module output falls as cell temperature rises, at the power temperature coefficient printed on the module datasheet. We have no measured figure for Morbi roofs, and PVGIS's 14% loss assumption is a generic one. Ask the EPC how it treated roof-level heat and whether it left air gaps under the modules. Put the inverters in shade, with airflow, away from kiln walls and flues, and check the inverter's temperature derating curve against the hottest place it will hang.

## How do you keep a rooftop plant safe near kilns?

The points below are design practice, not a Gujarat rule we read, so treat them as questions for your safety officer and the Chief Electrical Inspector.

- Keep the array and all DC cable runs away from kiln walls, flues, hot ducts and exhaust plumes. Ask which cable insulation rating the EPC has used where a run passes near a hot surface.
- Keep the array, cable trays and isolators clear of gas lines, propane storage, regulator stations and vents. Piped gas and propane are the fuels the 2026 reports describe, and a leak path next to a DC arc is the scenario to design out.
- Run DC cable in sheathed or conduit routes with no loose loops, and mark the isolation points. Dust collects on trays and connectors, so ask how they will be inspected.
- Give your fire adviser and your insurer the single line diagram before approval, not after. Ask them whether the plant needs a notice to either.

## How do grid faults and inverter trip settings affect a Morbi plant?

We found no PGVCL outage or voltage record for the Morbi industrial areas and we do not assert anything about the quality of supply there. If your unit sees dips, sags or short outages, you can still read the rules and your own logs.

CEA's distributed generation regulations, regulation 11(6) as amended in 2019, require an inverter-based plant below 33 kV to disconnect on over-voltage above 110% or under-voltage below 80% with a clearing time of up to two seconds, and on frequency at or above 50.5 Hz or below 47.5 Hz within 0.2 seconds. It must not reconnect until voltage and frequency are within limits and stable for at least sixty seconds, and it must cease to energise within two seconds of an unintended island. The licensee may prescribe narrower limits.

For a ceramic unit that has three practical consequences.

1. Every voltage event that crosses a trip limit costs at least a minute of generation. A cluster that sees many short events will see a lower yield than PVGIS.
2. You cannot widen the protection limits to stop nuisance trips. The settings belong to the approved grid profile. If trips are frequent, take the inverter event log to PGVCL and your EPC and have them look at the feeder, not the setting.
3. Solar will not run through an outage. If your kilns depend on a DG set for outages, ask the EPC for the DG synchronisation design before you sign, because on-grid inverters and a DG set need a deliberate arrangement.

Large motor loads and solar inverters also change power factor. A solar inverter at unity power factor reduces kWh without reducing the reactive demand, which can lower the average power factor on the HTP-1 penalty slab. Our [reactive power guide](/blog/reactive-power-solar-inverters-india/) sets out how to handle that.

## When is group captive or open access better than rooftop solar?

If your roof carries only a small part of the load, an off-site plant can supply the rest. A group captive plant is owned by an SPV, and you and other consumers hold equity and draw power through the grid.

The test for captive status was rewritten this year. The Electricity (Amendment) Rules 2026, published on 13 March 2026, substituted Rule 3. It keeps the two core thresholds: the captive users must hold not less than 26% of the ownership and consume not less than 51% of the electricity generated in the financial year.

For an association of persons, which includes an SPV, an individual user's consumption is admissible only up to 100% of its proportionate share of ownership, and a user holding 26% or more is exempt from that proportionality test. Verification is by the state nodal agency. If the plant fails, the whole generation is treated as sale by a generating company and cross subsidy and additional surcharge apply.

We checked [our group captive guide](/blog/group-captive-solar-msme/) against that Rule. It is a useful map of the structure, but two things in it need care. It states a 10% variation in proportionate consumption, which belongs to the rule before March 2026. And its landed-cost and payback figures, such as Rs 5 a unit and 3.5 to 5 years on equity, are indicative and are not sourced to a Gujarat order. Do not carry those numbers into a Morbi decision.

The wider charge stack is in our [open access solar guide](/blog/open-access-solar-india-explained/). We did not read GERC's open access regulations for this post, so we give no Gujarat wheeling, cross subsidy or banking figure for an off-site plant. Ask for them in writing from any developer, with the PGVCL order they come from.

An off-site plant does not compete for roof space, but it commits you to a share of equity and annual consumption tests. A rooftop plant is the faster route for the part of your load your roof can carry.

## What is the payback of a 500 kWp plant on a 24 by 7 ceramic unit?

Every figure below is **illustrative**: it is no quote, no Qbits number and no result from a real site. Replace each input with your own.

| Input | Illustrative value | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Supply | HTP-1, 1,000 kVA contract demand, billing demand above 500 kVA, 24 by 7 | Matches the load table above |
| Plant size | 500 kWp | Chosen as a roof-limited size, about 36% of in-window consumption |
| Annual yield | 1,400 kWh per kWp, 7,00,000 kWh a year | PVGIS 1,551 at a 10 degree tilt, less about 10% for dust, heat and downtime |
| Avoided energy rate | Rs 3.93 per unit | The weighted value in the time-band table, before any rebate |
| Banking charge | Rs 1.50 per unit | The demand-based rate. Rs 1.10 is shown as a second case |
| Other per-unit charges avoided | Rs 0, 1.50 and 3.00 per unit | Stand-ins for FPPAS and similar lines. Read yours from the bill |
| Installed cost | Rs 30,000, 40,000 and 50,000 per kWp | Round numbers chosen to span a range, not market quotes |
| Running cost | 1.5% of installed cost a year | Cleaning, with more frequent washing assumed for dust |
| Excluded | Demand-charge savings, tax depreciation, finance cost, degradation, inverter replacement, export on stoppage days | Not verified or case-specific |

Payback is installed cost divided by (units generated times net saving per unit, less running cost). Net saving per unit is Rs 3.93 less the banking charge plus the other per-unit charges avoided.

| Installed cost per kWp | Other charges avoided: Rs 0 per unit | Rs 1.50 per unit | Rs 3.00 per unit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rs 30,000 (Rs 1.5 crore for 500 kWp) | 10.2 years | 5.9 years | 4.2 years |
| Rs 40,000 (Rs 2.0 crore) | 14.3 years | 8.2 years | 5.7 years |
| Rs 50,000 (Rs 2.5 crore) | 18.9 years | 10.5 years | 7.3 years |

At the MSME banking charge of Rs 1.10, the Rs 40,000 row moves to 11.9, 7.3 and 5.3 years.

Read it like this. On the tariff schedule alone, a solar unit is worth Rs 2.43 after banking, and the payback is long. The answer moves a great deal with the per-unit lines your bill adds to the energy charge, so ask the EPC to rebuild the sum from your last twelve bills, line by line, including FPPAS and electricity duty. The [C&I payback and IRR guide](/blog/solar-irr-payback-ci-india/) applies that method to other tariffs. Tax depreciation is excluded here because we have not verified the current rate and your tax position is your own.

A stoppage day is not a disaster under these rules. On the Rs 2.25 MSME surplus rate, an exported unit earns almost what a consumed unit nets at Rs 3.93 less Rs 1.50, which is Rs 2.43, before the other charges. For a non-MSME unit the surplus rate is 75% of a GUVNL bid average that we did not find, so ask PGVCL for it.

## What is the PGVCL approval path for an HT consumer?

The Fourth Amendment (13 of 2024) to the GERC regulations sets the clock below. The days are regulatory targets for each authority, and nobody guarantees them for your file.

| Step | Authority | Time limit in the regulation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Registration and certificate | GEDA or the designated agency | 5 days from a complete application |
| Approval of single line, earthing and wiring diagrams | Chief Electrical Inspector | 10 days |
| Application with capacity and documents | PGVCL | You submit |
| Technical feasibility report | PGVCL, then the field office | 3 days to request, 10 days for the field report |
| In-principle consent or estimate | PGVCL head office | 2 days after the report |
| Connectivity agreement | You and PGVCL | Within 15 days of consent, or 30 days if system strengthening is needed |
| Project completion | You | 6 months from the letter |
| Meter installation | PGVCL | 5 days after documents |
| Commissioning certificate | GEDA | 3 days after intimation |

The feasibility report names the 11 kV feeder and transformer capacity. For HT consumers installing above 6 kW up to 1,000 kW, state-owned DISCOMs recover system strengthening charges per kVA, on the basis of the charge for new or additional HT load. We did not look up the current figure.

At completion the DISCOM asks for ownership proof, the Chief Electrical Inspector's approval, meter and CTPT test certificates, and IEC test reports for modules, inverters, cables, transformer, RMU and meters. The inverter needs anti-islanding (it must shut down when the grid is dead), and plants above 10 kW need a second protection such as a no-volt relay. Our [net-meter approval timeline](/blog/net-meter-approval-timeline/) shows how the process runs in practice.

## What should a ceramic unit bring to an EPC?

1. Twelve bills, with the demand, energy, ToD, FPPAS, power factor and duty lines visible.
2. Half-hourly load data for a normal week and for your lowest-load week of the year, from the HT meter or a logger.
3. Contract demand, supply voltage, Udyam registration and activity code, so PGVCL can say which banking charge applies.
4. A roof drawing with sheet type, truss spacing, stacks, vents, roof lights and gas lines marked.
5. A written proposal that states the banking charge, the avoided energy rate by time band, the dust and heat allowance, the cleaning plan, and what it assumes for FPPAS and for stoppage days.

## Is rooftop solar worth it for a ceramic unit?

For a 24 by 7 ceramic unit on HTP-1, rooftop solar will find a use for every unit, and the roof, not the load, sets the size. The time-band value of a solar unit, the banking charge and the other per-unit lines on your bill decide the payback, not the yield. Solar leaves the gas or propane bill untouched, so judge it against the electricity line only. If your roof is small, compare an off-site group captive share under the 2026 rules, and make any developer show the Gujarat charge stack in writing.
