The UAE is four solar markets wearing one flag, and a design tool that treats it as one country will get the economics wrong in at least three of them. DEWA credits exports at the consumer's own slab rate with indefinite rollover and a 1 MW per plot cap. ADDC and AADC in Abu Dhabi offer kWh offset only with no cash payment, at 5 MW per premises, against a tariff that varies by a factor of four between subsidised nationals and expatriates. EtihadWE in the Northern Emirates expires credits at year-end. Sharjah has no broadly available programme at all. This guide compares the platforms UAE installers should shortlist, judged on whether they can model the specific utility rather than a generic net metering assumption.

> **What most UAE quotes get wrong.** They apply one export assumption emirate-wide. In Dubai, where credits roll over indefinitely at the consumer's slab rate, a slightly oversized array is defensible. Under EtihadWE, where credits expire annually, the same oversizing throws away energy every December. Same hardware, opposite recommendation, one border apart.

The Abu Dhabi tariff structure deserves separate attention because it is the most commonly mishandled. Subsidised UAE national rates at 6.7 to 7.5 fils per kWh mean avoided import is worth roughly a quarter of what it is worth to an expatriate household at 26.8 to 30.5 fils. A design tool that models one residential tariff for the emirate will produce a payback figure that is wrong by a factor of four for one of those two customers. ## Approval, Equipment And Contractor Categories

**Dubai.** Approval runs in four stages: NOC via the Hab Reeh platform as a digital self-assessment, DEWA design approval, installation by an approved contractor, then inspection and bidirectional meter activation. Contractors fall into three categories by system size, being A up to 50 kW, B from 50 to 150 kW, and C above 150 kW. The contractor must be DEWA-certified and is responsible for submitting the application. Equipment must appear on DEWA's approved list. See SurgePV's [DEWA Shams Dubai guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/uae/guides/dewa-shams-dubai).

**Abu Dhabi.** Five stages: RSB approval, design submission to ADDC or AADC, signing the PV Connection Agreement, installation and inspection, then utility installation of the bidirectional meter. Both the design consultant and the installation contractor must appear on the relevant utility's approved list, and the lists are utility-specific rather than shared. The Abu Dhabi Electricity Wiring Regulations 2020 are mandatory for all work.

**Everywhere.** ECAS certification from the Ministry of Industry and Advanced Technology is required on all solar equipment sold in the UAE, covered in SurgePV's [ECAS certification guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/uae/guides/ecas-certification). The mandatory IEC set is 61215 and 61730 for panels, 62109-1, 62109-2 and 62116 for inverters, 61727 for the grid interface, and 60255 for protection relays.

The legal basis is Federal Decree-Law 17 of 2022 on distributed renewable energy. Separately, the Climate Law, Decree-Law 11 of 2024 effective May 2025, requires Scope 2 emissions reporting with fines from AED 50,000 to 2 million, which is increasingly what drives corporate rooftop demand here.

## What The Climate Does To The Yield Model

Two UAE-specific effects belong in any credible simulation, and generic temperate defaults handle neither well.

**Thermal derating.** Sustained high ambient temperatures push module cell temperatures well above test conditions for much of the year. A thermal model tuned for European conditions will overstate output. This is not a small correction in a Gulf summer.

**Soiling.** Dust accumulation between cleaning cycles produces losses that vary with cleaning frequency and season. A design that assumes a European soiling default will overstate annual yield, and a proposal built on it will underdeliver. The honest approach is to model the customer's actual intended cleaning schedule and show the sensitivity.

Both push toward an 8,760-hour engine with configurable soiling and a proper thermal model rather than a simplified annual estimate. Our [PV yield simulation software](/blog/pv-yield-simulation-software/) guide covers the methodology.

## Worked Example: 20 kW Villa Rooftop, Dubai Versus Abu Dhabi Expat Versus Abu Dhabi National

An illustrative calculation, not a specific customer project. Inputs: 20 kW DC, 1,750 kWh per kW specific yield, 35,000 kWh generation, 22,000 kWh consumption, 13,000 kWh exported.

| Line | Dubai, DEWA | Abu Dhabi, expat | Abu Dhabi, UAE national |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Import tariff | 38 fils per kWh | 28 fils per kWh | 7 fils per kWh |
| Avoided import value | AED 8,360 | AED 6,160 | AED 1,540 |
| Export credited at | Own slab, 38 fils | kWh offset, 28 fils | kWh offset, 7 fils |
| Export value | AED 4,940 | AED 3,640 | AED 910 |
| Year 1 benefit | AED 13,300 | AED 9,800 | AED 2,450 |

The same villa rooftop returns five and a half times more in Dubai than for a subsidised national household in Abu Dhabi. That is not a modelling nuance, it is the difference between a four year payback and a twenty year one, and it is invisible to any tool carrying a single national tariff. It is also why UAE national residential is generally not a solar market on economics alone, and why the honest recommendation there is often a smaller system justified on sustainability reporting rather than payback.
