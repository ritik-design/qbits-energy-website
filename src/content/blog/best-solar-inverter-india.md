## How we compared

This is a document comparison, not a lab test. We did not install, load or time any inverter, and nothing here is a measured performance result.

For each brand we picked **one exact current model** from the brand's own published datasheet, brochure or product page, and recorded the same fields: phase, rated AC output, MPPT count and voltage window, maximum DC input voltage and current per MPPT where published, maximum efficiency, enclosure rating and monitoring route. Selection rules:

This is not an exhaustive market list. Other makers publish comparable models, and leaving a brand out does not mean it failed any test. ## What makes an inverter suit Indian conditions

Eight factors matter more than a headline efficiency number.

**Grid voltage window.** Distribution voltage varies by feeder and time of day. An inverter's published AC operating range and protection settings decide when it disconnects. The range differs by model, even within one brand, so read it per datasheet and compare it with a measurement at your meter. See the weak-grid inverter comparison.

**Temperature and derating.** Terrace and wall-mounted units see high ambient heat in many regions. Check the published operating range and where derating starts. Shade and airflow around the unit matter as much as the rating; see inverter overheating.

**Ingress rating.** IP65 and IP66 are both dust-tight. IP66 is tested against stronger water jets. Neither covers heat, condensation or a bad cable gland. For coastal sites read the coastal inverter guide and the IP65 versus IP66 guide.

**MPPT layout.** Count the roof planes, then match inputs and strings. Voltage window and current per input matter more than the headline count. See MPPT explained and [dual versus single MPPT](/blog/dual-mppt-vs-single-mppt/).

**Monitoring.** A Wi-Fi dongle or app that is not set up, or not owned by you, does not monitor anything. Test it at handover. See the monitoring guide.

**Surge and earthing.** Check whether surge protection is built in or must be added, and what earthing the installer will do. See choosing inverters with surge protection.

**Written warranty.** Ask for the document for the exact model. Years alone say nothing about the remedy, the issuing entity or who pays for labour and freight. See the [warranty guide](/blog/solar-inverter-warranty/).

**ALMM and compliance.** MNRE's [Approved List of Models and Manufacturers](https://mnre.gov.in/en/approved-list-of-models-and-manufacturers-almm/) publishes List-I for solar PV modules and List-II for solar PV cells. It is not an inverter approval list. For an inverter, ask for the applicable BIS registration and test reports for the exact model, and check the scheme and DISCOM requirements separately. See solar inverter certifications.

## Worked example: like-for-like 5 kW single-phase on-grid

Nine entries share the same class: 5 kW and single phase. Eight of them state two MPPTs. Reading across them shows what a datasheet comparison can and cannot support.

**Two roof planes.** Eight of the nine single-phase entries publish two independent MPPTs. That suits a roof where the installer puts one orientation on each tracker. Dual MPPT does not fix shade inside a string: a water tank shading three modules can still pull that string down. Ask for each string's module count, orientation, open-circuit voltage and operating current.

**Current headroom.** The published per-input current limit runs from 13.5 A to 22 A across entries. Modern high-power modules can have operating currents near or above the lower figures, so compare the proposed module's current and short-circuit current against each limit and the design margin. A higher number does not prove more yield.

**Voltage window.** Sungrow publishes a 40 V lower bound, Growatt, Havells, Microtek, Qbits and Waaree 80 V, Solis 90 V, Fronius a 230–530 V MPP range. A lower bound gives layout freedom for short strings, but the string must also stay below the maximum DC voltage on the coldest morning. A normal 5 kW array may work comfortably in any of them.

**Enclosure.** Fronius, Qbits and Solis publish IP66; the others IP65. That is a laboratory ingress class, not field reliability.

**Efficiency.** The published maximum figures run from 97.4% to 98.4%. These are peak operating points. Read the [peak versus weighted efficiency note](/blog/peak-vs-weighted-inverter-efficiency/) before using a decimal difference to choose. Use efficiency as a late tie-breaker after the array, phase, MPPT and current checks pass.

If your quote specifies 6 kW, use the [exact 6 kW on-grid comparison](/blog/best-six-kw-solar-inverter-india/); different families apply. For other sizes see 3 kW, [5 kW](/blog/best-5kw-solar-inverter-india-2026/) and 10 kW.

### What this table cannot tell you

Official datasheets are strong evidence for model specifications and weak evidence for customer outcomes. ## Buyer-profile recommendations: what to compare, not who wins

**Budget-minded homeowner with a single-phase, grid-only home.** Start from the exact 5 kW or smaller single-phase on-grid models in the table. Equalise the scope across quotes: inverter model, protection, monitoring, installation and commissioning. A low inverter-only line can become the higher project cost once missing items are added. Ask for dated written quotes.

**Premium buyer.** If you want the option of storage or a backup socket, a battery-ready architecture such as the Fronius entry may be worth comparing. Ask for the India-market datasheet and a named service route, and check how the battery would be warranted.

**Power-cut zone.** The design should be hybrid, not on-grid. Compare supported batteries, backup output and transfer behaviour, then see the [Qbits hybrid range](/hybrid-inverter/) and the [power-cut backup guide](/blog/solar-inverter-power-cut-backup/). Backup is a system design with a battery, not just an inverter label.

**Three-phase home or small commercial site.** Compare 10 kW three-phase models on DC voltage window, per-MPPT current, protection and the service plan, and check the phase and capacity rules your DISCOM sets. See single-phase versus three-phase.

**EPC installer.** Standardise on documents: one datasheet per model, one warranty template and one named service route per district. See the [inverter procurement guide](/blog/inverter-procurement-india/) and the warranty and service checklist for bulk buyers.

## Common mistakes buyers make

- **Comparing a hybrid and an on-grid model as if they did the same job.**
- **Reading peak efficiency as annual yield.** It is one operating point.
- **Accepting a family brochure instead of the datasheet for the exact variant.** Brochures often carry several similar names, as the Havells and Solis entries show.
- **Choosing by warranty years alone.** Read the issuing entity, remedy and exclusions.
- **Ignoring the service route until after a fault.** Confirm a named party for your PIN code before you pay.
- **Treating "ALMM listed" as an inverter certificate.** ALMM covers modules and cells.
- **Skipping the string calculation.** A model can be good and the string design wrong.
- **Mixing revisions.** Several entries above carry notes about versions; always match the datasheet to the unit on the quote.

## Indian and international brands: what to ask for

The Indian-versus-international question is mostly about documents and service, not nationality. Whichever you consider, ask for four things. First, the legal entity that issues the warranty and sells the unit in India. Second, the India-market model code and datasheet. Third, a named service party for your PIN code and the spares route. Fourth, the applicable BIS registration and, for grid connection, the DISCOM's accepted documents. An international brand with an India subsidiary and a clear service desk can be a sound choice. An Indian brand that cannot show the exact model's documents is not. For a deeper read, see Indian versus international solar inverters and the Made in India inverter guide.

## Essential versus nice-to-have checklist

**Essential for every installation**

- Exact model code and a current datasheet for that variant
- Phase and AC rating that match the connection and DISCOM limits
- String calculation using the chosen module, within voltage and current limits
- Anti-islanding and grid-protection functions that meet the DISCOM's requirements
- Applicable BIS registration for the model
- Written warranty with issuing entity, remedy and exclusions
- Named service party for your PIN code

**Important, depending on the roof and budget**

- Two MPPTs when the roof has two planes
- IP66 for exposed walls, heavy monsoon or coastal air
- Monitoring with fault alerts and an account in your name
- Export-limit or zero-export function when the DISCOM requires it
- Built-in or installer-supplied surge protection and sound earthing

**Nice to have**

- Arc-fault detection (an option on several models above)
- Remote firmware updates
- A battery-ready path if outages may become a problem

## How to choose from here

1. Decide whether outage backup is required.
2. Confirm the connection phase and proposed AC rating.
3. Get the exact module and a string layout.
4. Compare two or three exact models using the same fields as the table.
5. Ask for current compliance, warranty and service documents for each.
6. Compare complete dated quotes, not inverter-only prices.

If your home needs grid-connected generation without a battery, review the [Qbits on-grid range](/on-grid-inverter/) by phase and capacity, and take the exact datasheet, written warranty and a named local service route into the final comparison. Use the [string sizing calculator](/string-sizing-calculator/) to cross-check a proposed string against an inverter's published limits. The best choice is the one that survives that check, even when it is not the first brand on a list.
