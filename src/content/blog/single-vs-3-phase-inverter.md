> **Quick answers**
>
> - Phase choice follows your sanctioned connection, not your rooftop capacity.
> - A three-phase inverter cannot work on a single-phase service. There are no phases to synchronise with.
> - A single-phase inverter on a three-phase connection is possible, but phase-wise netting may cost you export credit.
> - Capacity thresholds that force three phase are set by DISCOM, vary by state, and change by order.
> - Phase count does not by itself make an inverter more efficient.
> - Settle the connection question before the equipment question.

**Short version.** Read your sanctioned connection from the bill and the main breaker, confirm your DISCOM's current capacity threshold and netting method, then match the inverter to that. Choosing phase from panel kW alone is the common and expensive mistake.

## What phase actually means here

A single-phase supply delivers power on one live conductor and a neutral. A three-phase supply delivers it on three live conductors carrying alternating voltages offset from one another, usually with a neutral.

For solar the practical consequence is synchronisation. A grid-tied inverter matches its output to the incoming supply. A three-phase inverter has three output legs and needs three incoming phases to lock onto. Present it with a single-phase service and there is simply nothing for two of its legs to connect to.

The reverse direction is physically possible. A single-phase inverter can be connected to one phase of a three-phase service. Whether that is a good idea depends on how your DISCOM settles net metering, which is the part most quotes leave out.

## How to check what you have

Do this before you discuss equipment with anyone.

1. **Read the bill.** It normally states the sanctioned load and whether the connection is single or three phase. Sanctioned load is the number that matters for DISCOM thresholds, not your typical consumption.
2. **Look at the main incoming breaker.** One wide breaker fed by a single live conductor plus neutral indicates single phase. Three mechanically linked poles fed by three live conductors indicates three phase.
3. **Count the meter terminals** if accessible, or note the meter model. Three-phase meters are visibly larger and have more terminals.
4. **Confirm with the DISCOM or your installer.** Treat steps one to three as indicative. The sanctioned connection on record is what governs the application.

If you are planning to add significant load such as multiple air conditioners, a lift, a workshop or an EV charger, raise it now. A connection upgrade decided later can mean revisiting the inverter choice.

## Phase-wise netting, the expensive detail

This is the point that most often surprises owners of three-phase homes after commissioning.

Some DISCOMs settle net metering on the total across all three phases. Others settle it phase by phase. Where settlement is phase-wise, a single-phase inverter connected to one phase exports only on that phase, while your loads on the other two phases continue to import.

The result is that you can be billed for imports on two phases while the export credit on the third is settled separately, sometimes at a less favourable rate or capped. The meter is behaving correctly. The design simply did not match the settlement method.

Where a DISCOM nets phase-wise, matching a three-phase inverter to a three-phase connection removes the issue because generation is distributed across all three phases.

Ask two specific questions before signing: does my DISCOM settle net metering per phase or on the aggregate, and if per phase, how will this design be credited? Get the answer in writing. The [net metering application guide](/blog/how-to-apply-net-metering-india/) covers what to confirm during the application.

## When three phase is required rather than preferred

Several DISCOMs set a rooftop capacity or sanctioned load threshold above which a three-phase connection becomes mandatory. The threshold is not uniform across India and it changes by order, so quoting a common figure would be misleading.

What is consistent is the process. The DISCOM assesses technical feasibility against your sanctioned connection, and a design that exceeds the permitted single-phase capacity will not be approved as submitted. Discovering that at the feasibility stage costs you weeks. Discovering it after equipment is delivered costs considerably more.

Beyond the regulatory threshold, three phase is usually the practical choice where the building already runs three-phase equipment, where loads would otherwise be badly unbalanced, or where future expansion is likely.

## What goes wrong when the phase is wrong

The cost of a mismatch is rarely just the inverter. It usually lands in three places.

**The application stalls.** DISCOM technical feasibility assesses the proposed design against your sanctioned connection. A design that exceeds the permitted single-phase capacity, or proposes a three-phase inverter on a single-phase service, comes back for revision. That is weeks, not days, and it happens before any equipment is useful.

**The equipment has to change.** An inverter bought for the wrong connection type cannot be reconfigured. A single-phase unit does not become three-phase through a settings change or a firmware update. Whoever bought it absorbs the loss, and who that is depends on how the contract allocated responsibility for the connection check.

**The billing disappoints quietly.** This is the one that surfaces months later. The system generates as designed, the meter records as designed, and the credit is smaller than the customer expected because settlement is phase-wise and nobody raised it. There is no fault to repair, which makes it harder to resolve than a broken component.

On a three-phase service there is a further design consideration even when the inverter is correctly matched. Household loads are rarely spread evenly across phases. Where the imbalance is significant, discuss it with the installer during design, because balancing loads across phases is cheaper at the distribution board stage than after commissioning.
