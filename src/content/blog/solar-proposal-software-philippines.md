Net metering under ERC Resolution No. 09, Series of 2013 credits exported energy at the blended generation rate, which for a Meralco customer runs roughly PHP 5 to 6 per kWh. Imported energy costs the full retail tariff, including transmission, distribution, system loss and universal charges. Any tool that nets the two at the same rate, which is the default behaviour in most US and European platforms, overstates the return on every system that exports. ## What A Philippine Proposal Has To Get Right

**Export priced at the BGC.** This is the single setting that decides whether the proposal is accurate. The blended generation rate covers generation charges only. Everything else on the bill is still payable on imported energy. SurgePV's [ERC net metering guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/philippines/guides/erc-net-metering-rules) covers the methodology.

**Year-end forfeiture stated.** Credits roll monthly but can be forfeited at annual settlement. A proposal showing accumulated credits as banked value, without noting the settlement risk, is presenting something the customer may not receive.

**The right capacity assumption.** With the non-residential cap now at 1 MW rather than 100 kW, C&I projects that previously needed splitting can be quoted whole. Software still enforcing the old ceiling will misprice them.

**Realistic approval timelines.** 10 working days for the DU decision on a complete application, 3 working days for the LGU permit and CFEI after inspection. Those are mandated and worth quoting precisely, because they are faster than most customers expect. SurgePV's [DOE 10-day mandate guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/philippines/guides/doe-10-day-mandate) covers the process.

**The CoC and who signs it.** A licensed RME or PEE signs the Certificate of Compliance, covered in SurgePV's [Certificate of Compliance guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/philippines/guides/certificate-of-compliance).

## Why Load Profile Beats System Size In This Market

> **What most Philippine quotes get wrong.** They size the array to the roof and present net metering as though export were worth what import costs. In a BGC market the array should be sized to the daytime load, because self-consumed energy is worth roughly double exported energy.

The commercial implication favours a specific customer type. A factory, a mall, a cold store, or an office running its heaviest load during daylight hours consumes most of what it generates, and every one of those kWh avoids the full retail tariff. A warehouse with low daytime demand exports the majority of its output at half the value. Two identical arrays, materially different returns.

That makes the load profile the first question in a Philippine sales conversation rather than the roof area. It also means the proposal should demonstrate the match rather than assert it, which requires hourly modelling. An illustrative calculation, not a specific customer project. Inputs: 50 kW DC, 1,400 kWh per kW specific yield, 70,000 kWh generation, retail import at PHP 12 per kWh, export at PHP 5.50 per kWh, 70% self-consumption.

| Line | Priced at retail (wrong) | Priced at BGC (correct) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Self-consumed | 49,000 kWh | 49,000 kWh |
| Exported | 21,000 kWh | 21,000 kWh |
| Value of self-consumption at PHP 12 | PHP 588,000 | PHP 588,000 |
| Value of export | PHP 252,000 at PHP 12 | PHP 115,500 at PHP 5.50 |
| Year 1 benefit presented | PHP 840,000 | PHP 703,500 |

The retail-priced version overstates year one benefit by PHP 136,500, roughly 19%, on a system with fairly high self-consumption. On a warehouse exporting 65% of output the same error would be several times larger. The customer does not discover it at signing. They discover it on the bills, and the gap compounds every year of the asset's life.
