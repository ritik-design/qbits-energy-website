## Establish The Tariff Tier Before Anything Else

> **What most Saudi quotes get wrong.** They lead with system size and annual generation, then apply an assumed average electricity price to the whole output. Under a tiered tariff with a 5 halala export floor, that method can be wrong in both directions at once: overstating the value of exported energy by a factor of several, and misstating the value of displaced energy depending on which tier the customer occupies.

The correct sequence inverts the usual one.

| Step | Question | Why it comes first |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Which tariff category and tier is the customer in? | Sets the value of every displaced kilowatt-hour |
| 2 | What does the hourly consumption profile look like? | Determines how much can be displaced at all |
| 3 | What system size maximises overlap with that profile? | The array follows from the load, not the roof |
| 4 | What does the residual export earn? | A small line at 5 halalas |

Only step three involves the roof. This is uncomfortable for a sales process built around a site survey, but it produces proposals that survive contact with the customer's actual bills.

The practical benefit is that it also identifies the projects worth pursuing. A commercial facility on an upper tariff tier with strong daytime load is an excellent prospect. A facility with modest daytime consumption is a poor one regardless of how much roof it has, and finding that out during qualification is considerably cheaper than finding it out after a survey.

## A Large Export Line Is A Warning Sign

Because exports earn around 5 halalas, a well-designed Saudi system produces little of them by construction. It follows that a proposal showing substantial export revenue is describing one of two things: a system sized well past the facility's daytime load, or a calculation using the wrong credit rate.

This is worth understanding as a competitive tool as well as a self-check. When a Saudi buyer is comparing quotes and one of them shows healthy export income, that document is either technically wrong or proposing to sell them capacity they do not need. Being able to explain the 5 halala rate and what it implies gives your proposal a way to be evaluated that a bigger headline number cannot survive.

The corollary is that your own export line should be present and small. Removing it looks evasive. Showing "residual export, 5 halalas per kWh, modest annual value" makes the argument for you.

## Soiling Assumptions Decide Whether The Yield Is Real

Measured Saudi conditions put daily dust-driven efficiency loss in the region of 2 to 5 percent, with a single sandstorm observed cutting module output around 20 percent, and a module left uncleaned for six months in Dhahran losing more than half its power.

A proposal carrying default European soiling assumptions will therefore overstate delivered yield substantially, and that overstatement compounds every year of the projection.

Three things make the yield figure defensible:

**State the assumed cleaning frequency.** The yield number depends on it entirely, so presenting one without the other is presenting half a claim.

**Show sensitivity.** What the system delivers at monthly cleaning versus quarterly is information the customer needs and a competitor probably has not provided.

**Quote the maintenance alongside.** Cleaning is a real recurring cost driven by conditions that will not improve. A proposal that omits it is not cheaper, it is incomplete, and the customer discovers the gap in year one.

There is a design interaction worth mentioning where relevant. Modules that spend hours a day in parapet or inter-row shadow recover less from a wash than clear ones, so a cleaning schedule informed by the shading map delivers more recovered energy per riyal than a uniform one.

## What Else Belongs In The Document

**Portfolio headroom under the 5 MW area cap.** For a multi-site customer, confirm the aggregate allocation in each distribution area rather than assuming it. A proposal promising connection across several sites is making a commitment that depends on someone else's queue.

**SASO conformity and Saudi Building Code.** Naming them signals that equipment selection and structural design have been considered rather than assumed, and it distinguishes a planned installation from a price.

**The framework's scope.** Systems from 1 kW to 2 MW fall under the Small-Scale Solar PV framework. Stating that the project sits within it, and that net billing applies, avoids a customer arriving with net metering expectations from another market.

An illustrative calculation, not a specific customer project. Inputs: a commercial facility on an upper tariff tier near 32 halalas, 400 kWp array, strong daytime load, export credited at 5 halalas.

| Line | Quoted on averaged assumptions | Quoted properly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Annual generation shown | Optimistic, European soiling | Lower, Saudi soiling with stated cleaning |
| Value applied to output | One average price across all kWh | Tier price on displaced, 5 halalas on export |
| Export revenue shown | Overstated several times | Small and stated |
| Maintenance | Omitted | Quoted alongside |
| Payback | Flattering | Longer and defensible |
| Survives the first year? | No | Yes |

The right-hand column produces a worse-looking headline and a proposal that still holds twelve months later. That is a genuine commercial difficulty in a competitive tender, and there is only one workable answer to it: make the method visible.

A proposal that shows its soiling assumption, its cleaning regime, its tariff tier and its export rate is inviting comparison on inputs rather than on the final number. Against a rival quoting a better payback with none of those disclosed, that is a strong position with any buyer sophisticated enough to ask where the numbers came from, and Saudi commercial and industrial buyers generally are.
