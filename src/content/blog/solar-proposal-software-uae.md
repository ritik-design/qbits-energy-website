It is applying one export assumption and one tariff across a country that has four utility regimes and a residential tariff spread of roughly four to one. A villa in Dubai credited at the consumer's own slab rate under DEWA, an expatriate household in Abu Dhabi at 28 fils, and a subsidised UAE national household at 7 fils are three completely different investment cases on identical hardware. Add EtihadWE credits that expire every December while Dubai and Abu Dhabi roll over indefinitely, and a template built for one emirate will mislead in the others. ## Tariff Category Is The First Question, Not The Roof

> **What most UAE quotes get wrong.** They quote a payback without establishing the customer's tariff category. In Abu Dhabi that single omission can produce a figure wrong by a factor of four, because a subsidised UAE national pays roughly a quarter of what an expatriate pays for the same imported kWh, and avoided import is where the return comes from.

The practical consequence is uncomfortable but worth stating to customers directly: solar for a subsidised UAE national household in Abu Dhabi does not pay back on economics alone within any reasonable horizon. The honest recommendation there is either a smaller system justified on sustainability grounds, or a frank conversation that the numbers do not support the investment at current subsidised tariffs.

For expatriate households and commercial buyers the picture reverses entirely. At 26.8 to 30.5 fils per kWh, avoided import carries real value, and Abu Dhabi's 5 MW per premises ceiling gives commercial buyers far more headroom than Dubai's 1 MW per plot.

Dubai sits between the two on tariff and above both on credit quality. Crediting exports at the consumer's own slab means a high-consumption villa earns upper-slab value on every exported unit, which is unusually generous and rewards sizing to the roof rather than strictly to consumption. SurgePV's [DEWA Shams Dubai guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/uae/guides/dewa-shams-dubai) and [ADDC guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/uae/guides/addc-solar) set out both mechanisms.

## Credit Expiry Changes The Sizing Recommendation

The Northern Emirates are the exception that catches out anyone working from a Dubai template. Under EtihadWE, credits are calendar-year and expire at year-end. There is no banking across years.

That inverts the sizing logic. In Dubai, where credits roll indefinitely, a modest oversize accumulates value that will eventually be used. Under EtihadWE, the same oversize generates credits in summer that are written off in December. The correct EtihadWE design sits closer to annual consumption, and the proposal should say so rather than presenting surplus as banked value.

Availability is a second EtihadWE-specific risk. The scheme launched on 17 September 2024 with an aggregate cap allocated first-come first-served, so a proposal promising connection should be written against confirmed availability rather than an assumption. See SurgePV's [EtihadWE guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/uae/guides/fewa-solar).

Sharjah remains the gap. SEWA has no formal distributed solar programme published as of early 2026, so quoting a grid-connected export system there means confirming the current pathway with the utility directly rather than assuming one exists.

## What Else Belongs In The Document

**Zero-rated VAT, stated.** Solar generation equipment is zero-rated for UAE VAT at supply and installation in all seven emirates. Customers frequently assume 5% applies, so making the position explicit is worth a line.

**ECAS and the approved lists.** All solar equipment sold in the UAE requires ECAS certification from the Ministry of Industry and Advanced Technology, covered in SurgePV's [ECAS guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/uae/guides/ecas-certification). In Dubai the equipment must also be on DEWA's approved list, and in Abu Dhabi both the design consultant and the installation contractor must appear on the relevant utility's list. Specifying off-list equipment produces a rejection rather than a query.

**Scope 2 reporting.** The Climate Law, Decree-Law 11 of 2024 effective May 2025, requires Scope 2 emissions reporting with fines from AED 50,000 to 2 million. For corporate buyers this is often the actual driver, and quantifying the avoided emissions alongside the financial return addresses the real decision.

**Realistic soiling.** A Gulf proposal that models European soiling assumptions will overstate delivered yield. Stating the assumed cleaning frequency and showing the sensitivity is more credible than a single optimistic number.

## Worked Example: 300 kW Commercial Rooftop, Dubai Versus Northern Emirates

An illustrative calculation, not a specific customer project. Inputs: 300 kW DC, 1,750 kWh per kW specific yield, 525,000 kWh generation, 380,000 kWh self-consumed, 145,000 kWh exported, commercial tariff at 44 fils per kWh Dubai and comparable in the Northern Emirates.

| Line | Dubai, DEWA | Northern Emirates, EtihadWE |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Generation | 525,000 kWh | 525,000 kWh |
| Self-consumed | 380,000 kWh | 380,000 kWh |
| Avoided import at 44 fils | AED 167,200 | AED 167,200 |
| Exported | 145,000 kWh | 145,000 kWh |
| Credits realised | 145,000 kWh, rolls indefinitely | ~95,000 kWh, remainder expires |
| Export value realised | AED 63,800 | AED 41,800 |
| Year 1 benefit | AED 231,000 | AED 209,000 |

The gap is AED 22,000 a year, entirely from credits expiring unused at the calendar year end. On this profile the correct Northern Emirates response is either a slightly smaller array or load shifting to raise self-consumption, and either recommendation requires knowing that credits expire. A proposal copied from a Dubai template shows AED 231,000 in both columns and is wrong by roughly 10% in one of them, every year, for the life of the system.
