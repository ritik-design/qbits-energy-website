  - q: "What does CAPEX mean in solar?"
    a: "CAPEX (Capital Expenditure) in solar refers to the model where the buyer purchases and owns the solar system outright. The buyer pays the full installation cost upfront - or finances it through a bank loan - and owns the panels, inverter, and all equipment. All electricity generated is used or exported at net metering rates. The system depreciates on the buyer's books and delivers returns as reduced electricity bills over 20–25 years. CAPEX is the dominant model for residential buyers and the preferred model for C&I buyers with long-tenure buildings."
  - q: "What does OPEX mean in solar, and how is it different from RESCO?"
    a: "OPEX (Operational Expenditure) in solar describes models where the buyer pays for solar electricity as an operational cost - either via a PPA tariff per unit (RESCO model) or via a fixed annual O&M fee. Under OPEX, the solar developer or RESCO owns the system; the buyer does not. RESCO is a specific type of OPEX model where payment is per unit generated. In OPEX, costs appear on the P&L as operational expenses rather than on the balance sheet as assets, which suits organisations that prefer to minimise balance sheet capex."
  - q: "What is a typical IRR for a C&I solar CAPEX investment in India?"
    a: "A C&I solar CAPEX investment in India - factory, warehouse, or commercial building - typically delivers an IRR of 18–25% when financed from own funds, at a grid tariff of ₹8–12 per unit. The IRR improves further when accelerated depreciation (40% in Year 1 under the Income Tax Act) is applied, reducing the effective capex by the tax benefit. Loan-financed CAPEX at 8–10% reduces the IRR somewhat, but the spread over cost of capital is still strongly positive."
  - q: "Can I switch from OPEX to CAPEX mid-contract?"
    a: "Switching from OPEX to CAPEX mid-contract depends on the terms of your PPA. Some RESCO agreements include a buyout clause - the premises owner can purchase the system at a predetermined value after a certain number of years. Others do not allow buyout at all. If you intend to eventually own the system, negotiate the buyout option and pricing at the time of signing, not after the system is installed."
  - q: "Does accelerated depreciation apply to RESCO/OPEX solar?"
    a: "No. Accelerated depreciation (40% in Year 1 under Section 32 of the Income Tax Act) applies only to the buyer who owns the asset. Under OPEX or RESCO, the solar developer owns the system and claims the depreciation benefit on their books - not the premises owner. The premises owner's tax benefit under OPEX is limited to deducting the PPA payment as an operating expense. This depreciation asymmetry is one reason CAPEX often outperforms OPEX on a post-tax basis for profit-making companies."
  - q: "What tenure of lease or occupancy makes CAPEX worth it?"
    a: "As a general rule, CAPEX makes financial sense when the remaining lease or occupancy tenure is at least 7 years, preferably 10+. The payback period for a well-designed C&I solar system is typically 4–6 years. If you vacate the premises before payback, you have invested capex without recovering it. OPEX/RESCO is structurally better for tenants with leases under 5 years, since the PPA contract can be aligned to the lease term."

Two factory owners. One owns a 10,000 sq ft industrial shed in Pune. The other rents a similar facility in Surat on a three-year rolling lease. Both are paying ₹10 per unit for electricity. Both have 150 kW of usable rooftop area. Both want to reduce energy costs.

For the Pune owner: CAPEX, buy the solar system outright, own the asset, claim accelerated depreciation, earn an IRR of 20%+.

For the Surat renter: OPEX, let a RESCO install for free, pay ₹5 per unit, save 50% on electricity from day one, walk away at lease end.

The answer changes entirely based on ownership, tenure, and capital availability. This guide maps the decision matrix precisely.

> **Direct answer.** C&I solar CAPEX delivers an IRR of 18–25% for building owners with 10+ year tenure and access to capital; OPEX/RESCO delivers 20–35% lower electricity bills with zero upfront cost for tenants or capital-constrained buyers. Apply the 4-Question CAPEX vs OPEX Test in this guide to identify your optimal model in under three minutes.

The guide covers CAPEX vs OPEX across four dimensions: financial returns, balance sheet treatment, risk allocation, and operational implications, with a decision matrix and worked IRR examples.

## The Core Difference: Ownership vs Access

[CAPEX](/glossary/capex/) and [OPEX](/glossary/opex/) describe not just payment timing but the fundamental nature of the transaction:

**CAPEX Solar:**
- You buy and own the system.
- Asset appears on your balance sheet.
- You claim depreciation (40% in Year 1).
- You bear performance risk (if the inverter fails, you replace it).
- You capture the full electricity saving as financial return.
- Payback: 4–6 years. System life: 25 years. IRR: 18–25%.

**OPEX Solar (RESCO/PPA):**
- The solar developer owns the system.
- No asset on your balance sheet.
- You pay a PPA tariff per unit generated.
- The RESCO bears performance and maintenance risk.
- You capture the spread between grid tariff and PPA tariff as savings.
- Savings from Day 1: 20–50% reduction on the units generated.

Neither is universally superior. The right choice is determined by four questions.

## The 4-Question CAPEX vs OPEX Test

### The 4-Question CAPEX vs OPEX Test

Answer these four questions in order. The result points to your optimal model.

1. **Do you own the building or land where solar will be installed?**
   - Yes → CAPEX is viable. Proceed to question 2.
   - No (rented premises) → OPEX/RESCO is the primary option. Align contract term to lease term.

2. **Is your remaining tenancy or ownership horizon ≥ 7 years?**
   - Yes → CAPEX payback will occur within tenure. Proceed to question 3.
   - No (<7 years) → OPEX avoids stranded capex risk. Choose RESCO with exit clause aligned to lease.

3. **Is capital available at a cost below 12% per annum?**
   - Yes (own funds, MSME loan at 8–10%, IREDA at 7%) → CAPEX IRR (18–25%) exceeds cost of capital by a strong margin. CAPEX wins.
   - No (only expensive debt at 14%+, or capital fully committed to core business) → OPEX removes capital constraint. RESCO may be better even if you own the building.

4. **Does your organisation's policy permit balance sheet capital for non-core assets?**
   - Yes → CAPEX is straightforward.
   - No (policy requires OPEX treatment, off-balance-sheet financing) → OPEX/RESCO delivers the same electricity saving without capex line item on the P&L.

If you answered Yes to all four questions: **CAPEX is your answer.** The financial case is compelling.

If you answered No to question 1 or 2: **OPEX is your answer.** The structural constraints make CAPEX unsuitable.

Mixed answers (Yes to 1 and 2, No to 3 or 4): **Evaluate hybrid CAPEX with MSME/IREDA financing.**

- System size: 150 kW

## When CAPEX Wins

CAPEX is the superior model when:

- **Building ownership**: You own the premises and plan to occupy or derive rental income from it for 10+ years.
- **Capital available**: Own funds or low-cost institutional debt (MSME, IREDA, SBI at 7–10%) is accessible.
- **Profitable company**: Tax benefit from accelerated depreciation (Section 32, Income Tax Act) directly reduces effective capex. A company paying 30% corporate tax on ₹97.5 Lakh capex saves ~₹12 Lakh in Year 1.
- **Long tenure**: Residual lease or ownership exceeds payback period comfortably.
- **Want asset ownership**: The solar system adds to the property's value and the balance sheet at depreciated cost.

**Pros of CAPEX**
- ✓ Highest total financial return (IRR 18–25%)
- ✓ Depreciation benefit (40% Year 1) for companies
- ✓ Full ownership, asset on balance sheet
- ✓ No RESCO counterparty risk over 20 years
- ✓ Freedom to modify, expand, or refinance

**Cons of CAPEX**
- ✗ Requires significant upfront capital or debt
- ✗ Buyer bears performance and maintenance risk
- ✗ Unsuitable for rented or short-tenure premises
- ✗ Management attention required for O&M

## When OPEX/RESCO Wins

OPEX is the superior model when:

- **Rented premises**: The buyer does not own the building and cannot install a permanent asset.
- **Capital constrained**: Core business requires capital; non-core investments are policy-restricted.
- **Short lease**: Remaining lease under 5 years makes payback before exit impossible.
- **Non-profit or government institution**: Cannot claim depreciation benefit; balance sheet capex adds complexity.
- **Risk aversion**: Buyer prefers fixed, predictable energy costs without performance variability.

| Decision Dimension | CAPEX | OPEX / RESCO |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Upfront cost | High | Zero |
| Financial return | IRR 18–25% | 20–50% bill reduction |
| Balance sheet | Asset added | No impact |
| Performance risk | Buyer | RESCO |
| Suitable for | Building owner | Tenant / capital-constrained |
| Tax benefit | Yes (depreciation) | Limited (PPA as opex expense) |
| Exit flexibility | High (can sell building) | Depends on contract |

## Common Mistakes in CAPEX vs OPEX Decision-Making

- **Choosing OPEX because it feels cheaper**: zero upfront cost is psychologically attractive but the 25-year total saving under CAPEX (₹5.5 crore vs ₹2.5 crore in the worked example) is dramatically higher.
- **Underestimating OPEX escalation**: a 3% annual PPA escalation means the Year 20 tariff is 1.8× the Year 1 tariff. If grid tariffs do not increase proportionally, the saving narrows or disappears.
- **Ignoring the depreciation benefit in CAPEX**: many C&I buyers calculate CAPEX payback without factoring in Year 1 depreciation. This underestimates CAPEX attractiveness by ₹10–15 Lakh on a typical 100 kW system.
- **Signing OPEX without an exit clause**: if the business closes or relocates before contract expiry, a poorly worded RESCO contract may demand 3+ years of PPA payments as penalty.
