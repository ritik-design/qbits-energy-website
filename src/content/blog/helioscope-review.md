## What HelioScope Is and Who It Was Built For

HelioScope is a cloud design and yield simulation platform. You draw or import the site, place arrays across roof planes or ground, assign modules and inverters from a large component database, wire the strings, and run an hourly production simulation that accounts for shading, temperature, soiling, and inverter behaviour. Out the other end you get a production estimate, a loss breakdown, and design documentation.

The user it was designed for is an engineer, not a salesperson. Everything in the interface assumes you know what a string sizing constraint is and why it matters. There is no wizard trying to guess your intent, and no homeowner-facing view. That is a deliberate choice and it is why engineers like it.

The centre of gravity is commercial and industrial rooftop. Large roofs with parapets, mechanical units, multiple orientations, and awkward setbacks are exactly the geometry HelioScope handles well. It will do residential, and it will do modest ground mount, but the tool clearly grew up on C&I work.

## What HelioScope Genuinely Does Well

These are real strengths. We have run HelioScope alongside PVsyst, Aurora, and SurgePV on live commercial projects, and the following held up every time.

**C&I rooftop layout speed.** This is the strongest thing about the product. A 400 kW roof with three orientations, a parapet setback, and a dozen rooftop units is fiddly work in most tools. HelioScope handles it quickly, and re-laying an array after the client moves a mechanical unit does not mean starting over. For a team shipping several commercial designs a week, that speed compounds.

**Layouts that survive review.** Speed is worthless if the output is loose. HelioScope's array placement, row spacing, and string configuration produce results that an independent reviewer signs off without a fight. Engineers trust it because they have checked it and it kept being right.

**Component handling and single-line output.** The module and inverter database is deep, string configuration respects real electrical constraints, and the design package output is usable rather than decorative. That matters when your deliverable is a set of documents an electrical contractor will build from.

**Shading that people accept.** The hourly shading model is granular enough for serious C&I work, and the loss waterfall it produces is legible. When a finance reviewer or an owner's engineer questions a production number, the loss breakdown usually ends the conversation. A number nobody argues with saves a week per deal.

**Browser-based, no machine lock.** You open it on any machine with a browser. There is no Windows-only install, no licence dongle, no VM for the Mac users on your team, and no fight over which workstation has the seat. Anyone who has run PVsyst on a Mac through a virtual machine will understand why this is on a strengths list.

**Well regarded by engineers.** Reputation is not a technical feature, but it has commercial value. Sending a HelioScope report to a reviewer who has seen a hundred of them removes friction. Pretending that counts for nothing would be dishonest.

## Where HelioScope Stops Short

**Not positioned as a customer-document tool.** This is the defining limitation, and it is a scope decision rather than a quality failure. HelioScope is built to produce engineering output rather than the thing a customer signs: a branded document with pricing, financing options, savings projections, and a signature block. Teams solve this with a separate proposal tool, a CRM, or a designer rebuilding numbers in a template. Every one of those solutions introduces a handoff, and every handoff is a place where the design and the document drift apart.

**Not positioned as a tariff or incentive modelling tool.** Related, and just as consequential. HelioScope tells you how many kilowatt hours the array will make. Valuing those kilowatt hours under a specific utility tariff, a time-of-use structure, a feed-in tariff, or a subsidy scheme is not what the product is built around, and teams should confirm the current scope with the vendor. In most stacks that means the financial half of every proposal is built somewhere else, usually a spreadsheet that one person maintains and nobody else fully understands.

**Thinner on residential than Aurora.** For a 7 kW roof, the work is remote roof capture, a fast layout, a utility bill calculation, and a presentation a homeowner understands. Aurora has invested heavily in exactly that chain. HelioScope's flexibility on complex geometry is not what a residential job needs, and its lack of a customer-facing view is felt more sharply at the kitchen table than in an engineering review. Our [Aurora Solar review](/blog/aurora-solar-review/) covers that side in detail.

**Bankable reporting weaker than PVsyst in practice.** HelioScope's simulation is credible and widely accepted. The gap is contractual rather than technical. Some project finance agreements name PVsyst output specifically, and when they do, the question is not which engine is better modelled, it is which report the contract accepts. The [HelioScope and PVsyst comparison](/blog/helioscope-vs-pvsyst/) works through when a substitution is possible and when it is not.

| Dimension | HelioScope strength | HelioScope limitation |

| Single-line output | Usable in a real design package | Not a full construction document set |

> **What most buyers get wrong.** They evaluate HelioScope on layout quality, which it passes easily, and never ask who produces the customer document. That second question decides whether the tool is a bargain or half a purchase. Price the whole workflow, from address to signed contract, before you compare seat costs.

## Who Should Buy HelioScope

Buy it if most of these describe you.

- Your work is majority commercial and industrial rooftop, with complex geometry rather than simple planes.
- Your team is engineering-led, and someone else in the business produces the customer document.
- You already run a proposal tool or CRM your sales team likes and will not give up.
- Your financial modelling lives in a spreadsheet that someone competent owns and keeps current.
- Nobody in your financing chain has named a specific report format by contract.
- You have mixed operating systems on the team and a desktop-only tool would be a genuine problem.

At that profile HelioScope is a good buy and this review will not talk you out of it. An engineering team that ships C&I layouts fast, with a separate function owning the sale, is exactly the customer the product was scoped for. Arguing otherwise would be a sales pitch.

## Who Should Not Buy HelioScope

- **Residential volume installers.** The tool's strengths do not pay off on simple roofs, and sourcing the customer-facing document elsewhere is felt on every single deal rather than occasionally.
- **Anyone needing design and proposal in one place.** If your bottleneck is turning designs into documents, buying a design-only tool solves the half of the problem you did not have.
- **Small teams where one person does everything.** When the designer is also the salesperson, every handoff is a context switch for the same human. Two tools is two logins, two data sets, and two chances to send the wrong number.
- **Teams whose financial model keeps drifting.** If your production estimate and your proposal savings figure disagree because they live in different systems, that is a structural problem no amount of engineering accuracy fixes.
- **Teams under a lender mandate for a named report format.** Check the contract before the software choice. If PVsyst is named, that decision is already made for you.

## Verdict

HelioScope is a good product doing exactly what it set out to do. The C&I layouts are fast and accurate, the shading holds up under review, the component handling is serious, and running in a browser removes a class of problem that desktop tools still inflict on their users. If you are an engineering-led commercial team, it will not be the thing slowing you down.

The limitation is scope, not quality, and it is worth being precise about that distinction. HelioScope never promised a proposal builder, a tariff library, or a customer-facing document, and criticising it for not having them is only fair in the context of what a buyer actually needs to close a sale. What the buyer needs is design plus document. HelioScope supplies one half at a high standard, and expects you to source the other half elsewhere.

So the verdict is this. HelioScope is a strong component of a stack rather than a complete stack. If the rest of your stack already exists and works, that is a fine thing to be, and you should buy it without hesitation. If it does not, you are about to buy two tools, maintain two sets of numbers, and pay in coordination time for the rest of the contract. That is the case where SurgePV wins, not because HelioScope designs badly, but because one record producing both the design and the document removes a problem HelioScope was never trying to solve.

Three things to do next:

- Ask who in your business produces the customer document today, and what it costs in time and licence fees. That answer decides this purchase more than any feature list.
- Get a written HelioScope quote for your real seat count, because the public bands are wide and your number may sit anywhere inside them.
- Rebuild three recent commercial projects in a competing tool and compare the finished customer proposal, not the layout screenshot.
