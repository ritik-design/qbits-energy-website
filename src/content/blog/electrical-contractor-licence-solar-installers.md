**Central law says electrical installation work on a customer's premises must be done by a contractor licensed by the State Government. A certificate-of-competency holder must supervise it, and a permit holder must do it. The licence itself comes from your state.** The rule is regulation 31(1) of the Central Electricity Authority (CEA) (Measures relating to Safety and Electric Supply) Regulations, 2023. Regulation 31(2) adds that work done in breach may not be energised or connected to a supplier's works.

The regulation does not mention solar. We read eight state licensing texts for this guide. None names rooftop solar PV as inside or outside any licence class. So we do not say whether a licence is required for your solar work in any state. We show what each text says and mark the states we could not read. We also give you the questions to put to your board in writing.

Treat this as general information read on **30 October 2026**, not legal advice. Fees, forms and rules change, and the official text controls.

> **Short version**
> - Three layers apply at once. A licensed **contractor** (the firm) hires a **supervisor** with a certificate of competency and a **wireman** with a permit.
> - Every state text we read ties supervisors and wiremen to one employer at a time. You cannot borrow one electrician's certificate for several firms.
> - Selling solar (GST, Udyam), PM Surya Ghar vendor registration and a distribution company's (DISCOM's) own vendor list are separate from the contractor licence. None of them replaces it.
> - Eight states are read from primary text below. Eight more are marked **not verified**, and you should not rely on this guide for them.
> - Seven written questions to send to every state board are listed in the section "Seven questions to put to the licensing board in writing".

## What does central law say about an electrical contractor licence for solar?

Regulation 31 of the CEA's 2023 safety regulations sets the three-layer rule. We read the regulations from the gazette notification of 8 June 2023 (No. CEA-PS-16/1/2021-CEI Division), made under section 177(2)(b) read with section 53 of the Electricity Act, 2003. We read the English text in an archived copy of that gazette PDF. On 31 October 2026 we re-read regulations 2(1), 31 and 33 in the gazette file listed on CEA's current site, which opened with the Hindi text, and the substance matched. A CEA amendment of 27 March 2026, in force from 1 April 2027, adds definitions and battery energy storage rules (a new Chapter XA) and does not touch regulation 31.

| Provision | What it says (paraphrased) | Why it matters to an installer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Regulation 31(1) | No electrical installation work, including additions, alterations and repairs, may be carried out on a consumer's premises for the purpose of supply to that consumer, except by an electrical contractor licensed by the State Government, under the direct supervision of a person holding a certificate of competency, and by a person holding a permit issued or recognised by the State Government. Small replacements such as lamps, fans, fuses and switches up to 250 V, which do not change capacity or character, are carved out | The three layers: firm licence, supervisor, wireman |
| Regulation 31(1), first proviso | The Central Government (for its own works and for mines, oil-fields and railways) or the State Government (in other cases) may notify an exemption for specified work or classes of consumer | A state could have exempted something. Ask whether it has |
| Regulation 31(1), second proviso | For work on behalf of the Central Government, and for mines, oil-fields and railways, a contractor licensed by one state need not take a licence from another | It does not cover private rooftop jobs, so your home-state licence does not travel on its own |
| Regulation 31(2) | Work done in breach may not be energised or connected to a supplier's works | The practical penalty is a plant that cannot be switched on |
| Regulation 33(1) | Before supply starts, the supplier tests the installation or accepts test results signed by the licensed electrical contractor | A DISCOM can rely on your licensed contractor's signature |
| Regulation 2(1)(zj) | "Installation" means any composite electrical unit used for generating, transforming, transmitting, converting, distributing or utilising electricity | The definition includes generating units |

**Our reading, not a ruling.** Regulation 31(1) covers work "for the purpose of supply" to the consumer, and "installation" includes generating units. On that wording a rooftop plant's wiring looks within reach of the rule. But the text never says "solar", and what binds you is how your state board reads it. The written questions later in this guide matter more than any summary, including ours.

Two more points from the same regulations are easy to confuse with the licence.

- A **Chartered Electrical Safety Engineer** (regulation 6) is authorised by the state to help owners self-certify installations. That is an owner-side role, not a contractor licence.
- Inspector approval of a plant (regulations 34 and 45) is a project question, covered in our [guide to CEIG inspection](/blog/ceig-inspection-solar-plant-inverter-checks/). CEIG stands for Chief Electrical Inspector to Government. The contractor licence is a question about your firm.

Three patterns show up in the primary text of the states we read.

**Supervisors and wiremen belong to one firm.** Tamil Nadu's form carries a declaration from each supervisor or wireman. It says he has not obtained a contractor licence on his certificate and is not working under any other contractor. Karnataka's text says a permit holder seeking a licence may not endorse his permit to anyone else. Kerala and Haryana say staff are employed "solely" for the contractor's works. Odisha asks for affidavits of sole employment. Rajasthan grants no licence to a person employed by a licensed contractor unless he or she undertakes to leave that employer and work independently. Andhra Pradesh's regulation says the same of a competency-certificate holder in another contractor's employ.

**The smallest licence classes are person-sized.** Kerala's Grade C and Rajasthan's Class D are written for an individual who already holds a wireman or supervisor permit. For a one-person business doing low-voltage work, ask about those classes in those states.

**Your home licence does not travel.** Tamil Nadu's form asks an applicant from outside the state to have a branch office in Tamil Nadu. Karnataka's text sets extra conditions for outside applicants. Haryana allows a temporary arrangement of up to six months, applied for online, for a person holding a licence from any state. Rajasthan's schedule provides a temporary work permit for contractors of other states, for an equivalent class and a particular work order. As noted above, regulation 31's cross-state proviso covers Central Government works, not private jobs.

A new firm should read one Haryana condition twice. The rule asks for tax returns and turnover or balance sheets for the last three years. It sets a minimum turnover of Rs 20 lakh in one year for licences up to 650 V. The text does not say what a newly formed firm should do, so ask the Chief Electrical Inspector's office before you form the entity.

## Seven questions to put to the licensing board in writing

Send the same email to every board where you plan to work. Ask for a reply on letterhead or with a reference number, and keep it with your firm's records.

1. Does the board treat installing a grid-connected rooftop solar plant (DC wiring, inverter, and AC wiring up to the meter) as "electrical installation work" under regulation 31(1) of the CEA 2023 regulations?
2. If yes, which contractor class or grade covers a low-tension plant of the size we install, and what does the licence say about capacity?
3. Which supervisor certificates and wireman permits must we employ, and how many, for that class?
4. Has the state issued any notification under the first proviso of regulation 31(1) exempting any class of work or consumer?
5. Who must sign the completion and test report that the DISCOM or inspector asks for, under regulation 33?
6. Do you recognise a licence or permit issued by another state, and on what terms?
7. Which version of the rules does the board apply today, and where is the current application form?

If the answer to question 1 is unclear, ask for a written clarification. A verbal "everyone does it" is not a document you can show a DISCOM or an inspector.

## Documents, time limits and what can sink an application

We give no step-by-step timeline, because most texts set none. Where a text states a period, it is below.

| State | Period stated in the text | Where it appears |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tamil Nadu | The Board's service standard on its page: contractor licence within one month, certificates within 30 days | Services page, read 30 Oct 2026. A service charter, not a legal deadline |
| Rajasthan | Within 10 days of a complete application for Class A, 7 days for Classes B, C and D, 5 days for permits. If the office does not report a deficiency within three days, the application is treated as complete | Rule 20 |
| Haryana | A licence is first issued for one year and may remain valid for up to five. Rejection for a discrepancy must be communicated within seven days | Rules 13 and 14 |
| Karnataka | A licence is first granted for a maximum of one year, then renewable for five years | Rules 10 and 13 |
| Odisha | Contractor licence valid for three years, renewable. Supervisor certificate three years. Permits five years | Regulation 28 |

**Documents that recur across the texts.** Every text we read asks for some version of these, so use the board's own form as the checklist.

- Proof of the firm's identity, address and a business premises in the state (Haryana asks for that state's GST registration; Rajasthan and Kerala ask for an office in the state).
- Consent or declaration letters from each supervisor and wireman, with their original certificates or permits (Tamil Nadu asks for the originals, Karnataka for copies with the consent letters, Odisha for willingness letters and affidavits).
- Test instruments, with calibration or test reports. Tamil Nadu asks for reports from a government laboratory dated within three months before the application. Haryana asks for tests by an accredited laboratory.
- Bank solvency or financial evidence, in the form and amount the class sets. Tamil Nadu's ESA form asks for Rs 1 lakh for at least three years. We list no amounts for other classes, because the texts differ and some copies are inconsistent.
- Signed specimen signatures and the prescribed fee. Tamil Nadu's form sets Rs 50,000 for the ESA licence and Rs 2,500 for a supervisor certificate, both from 1 January 2024. Fees for the other states sit in schedules we did not read.

**What the texts say can sink an application.** These are grounds written into the rules. They are not a count of real rejections, for which we have no source.

- An incomplete application: Karnataka's text says an application that drew an objection is liable for rejection, with the fee forfeited, if it is not resubmitted in time or is resubmitted incomplete.
- A late renewal: Karnataka's text makes renewal applications after the due date liable for rejection.
- A discrepancy in the application or documents: Haryana's rule lets the Competent Authority reject on one.
- Staff on more than one payroll: see the one-firm pattern above.

## What should you do before taking your first solar job?

1. Name the state and DISCOM for your first ten jobs. Each state is a separate licensing question.
2. Email the licensing board the seven questions above and file the reply.
3. Confirm that each supervisor and wireman you rely on is employed only by you, and that the certificate or permit is valid.
4. Keep the supervisor's name and certificate number on every completion report you sign.
5. Treat GST, Udyam, PM Surya Ghar registration and any DISCOM listing as separate files with their own renewals.
6. Do not start a job on the strength of a consultant's summary, including this one. The board's written answer is the evidence you can show.
