Malaysian solar design begins with two numbers that have nothing to do with the roof. Domestic NEM 3.0 installations are capped at 12 kWp. Non-domestic installations are capped at 75% of Maximum Demand, which means a large warehouse roof on a low-MD premises simply cannot be filled, regardless of available area. On top of that sits a quota: NEM 3.0 is administered by SEDA in finite periodic rounds, and a system installed without quota approval will not receive TNB's bi-directional meter and therefore cannot legally export. Design software built for markets where you size to the roof and apply later gets Malaysian projects wrong at the first step. This guide compares the platforms Malaysian installers should shortlist.

## Start From Maximum Demand, Not Roof Area

> **What most Malaysian commercial quotes get wrong.** They size to available roof and discover the 75% of Maximum Demand ceiling at application. A 500 kWp roof on a premises with 400 kW MD can only carry 300 kWp under NEM 3.0. Designing the 500 and cutting it back later wastes the survey and usually the customer's patience.

The correct sequence on any Malaysian non-domestic project is to obtain the MD figure from the TNB bill first, compute 75% of it, and treat that as the hard ceiling before any layout work begins. Roof area then determines whether that ceiling is achievable, not the other way around.

Domestic is simpler at a flat 12 kWp, but the cap still binds on larger houses, which means the design conversation shifts to which loads are covered and whether self-consumption can be raised through scheduling. Because NEM 3.0 offsets at 1:1 against the tariff, self-consumption and export are worth the same, which is unusual and considerably more forgiving than the blended-generation-rate schemes used in the Philippines or the supplier-set rates in Europe.

That 1:1 equivalence has a design consequence worth stating plainly: under NEM 3.0 there is much less reason to chase self-consumption than in most markets, so orientation and battery arguments that work elsewhere are weaker here. The binding constraints are the caps and the quota, not the timing of generation.

## Quota, Licensing And The PE Stamp

**SEDA quota.** Applications go through nem.seda.gov.my and require the TNB account number, system capacity, installation address, applicant NRIC or company registration number, and a SEDA-registered contractor ID. Rounds are periodic and finite, and monitoring seda.gov.my for announcements is part of the job. Installing ahead of approval means no bi-directional meter.

**Licensing.** The contractor needs an Electrical Contractor Licence and a Wireman licence from the Energy Commission, plus SEDA Registered Solar PV Contractor status. Three separate credentials, all required.

**The PE stamp.** All grid-connected installations require a single line diagram stamped by a professional engineer registered with the Board of Engineers Malaysia, who carries responsibility for system compliance. That makes SLD quality a gating item rather than a documentation afterthought, and it is a strong argument for design software that auto-generates the SLD from the design rather than leaving it to be drawn separately in CAD. Our [solar SLD software](/blog/solar-sld-software/) guide covers why auto-generated diagrams cannot contradict the string configuration in the way hand-drawn ones can.

**Technical.** Inverters must disconnect automatically within 2 seconds of loss of TNB supply and comply with MS IEC 62109.

**East Malaysia.** Sarawak runs independently under Sarawak Energy Berhad with separate portals and potentially different caps and rates. Sabah and Labuan use SESB's distinct scheme. Peninsular assumptions do not transfer.

Typical timeline from SEDA application to first billing cycle is 4 to 6 months, which belongs in the customer's expectations from the first conversation.

## Worked Example: 500 kWp Roof, 400 kW Maximum Demand

An illustrative calculation showing the MD ceiling in practice. Inputs: factory with 500 kWp of usable roof, 400 kW Maximum Demand, 1,450 kWh per kWp specific yield, tariff at RM 0.50 per kWh, installed cost RM 2.20 per Wp.

| Line | Designed to roof (not permissible) | Designed to 75% of MD |
| --- | --- | --- |
| System size | 500 kWp | 300 kWp |
| Permissible under NEM 3.0 | No | Yes |
| Annual generation | 725,000 kWh | 435,000 kWh |
| Value at RM 0.50 (1:1 offset) | RM 362,500 | RM 217,500 |
| Installed cost | RM 1,100,000 | RM 660,000 |
| Simple payback | 3.0 years | 3.0 years |
| Outcome | Application rejected | Approved |

The payback is identical, which is the point. Under a 1:1 offset scheme, scaling the system does not change the payback ratio, so there is no economic penalty in designing to the cap. There is a large practical penalty in designing above it: a rejected application, a re-survey, and a delay against a 4 to 6 month timeline that was already the long pole. The only reason the 500 kWp design exists is that the tool did not know about the ceiling.
