**A franchise licenses you a brand and a way of working, and you pay for that licence. A dealership sells you goods, and you resell them at your own risk.** In Indian solar the two words are used loosely, so the brochure label tells you little. Your risk depends on four facts: who owns the stock, who holds the customer contract, whose name is on the invoice and who sets the price. Those facts sit in the agreement, not in the title.

This guide shows you how to read any offer by those four facts. It then sets out what Indian law says, gives an illustrative cost comparison, and ends with a list to run before you sign. It names no brand's fees and states nothing about Qbits terms.

The information is general and current to **30 October 2026**. We re-opened the statutes quoted below on 31 October 2026 to check the quoted wording. It is not legal, tax or financial advice, so a lawyer and a chartered accountant should read the actual draft.

> **Short version**
> - Ask four questions of any offer. Who owns the stock? Who holds the customer contract? Whose name is on the invoice? Who sets the resale price?
> - There is no dedicated franchise statute in India that we could find. A franchise is a bundle of ordinary law: contract, trade mark licensing, competition rules and tax.
> - A franchise fee is money you pay for rights, so it is not recoverable like stock can be. Check what you get for it in writing.
> - A "franchise" that is only a resale agreement with a fee attached is a dealership you have paid extra to join.

## The four facts that sort every offer

Answer each line below from the draft agreement, not from the sales call.

| Question | If the answer is "you" | If the answer is "the brand" |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who owns the stock? | You bought it. It is your cash and your obsolescence risk. | You hold it for the brand, or it ships against each order. Your cash risk is lower. |
| Who holds the customer contract? | You sell in your own name and carry the customer's complaints and credit risk. | The brand contracts with the customer and you act for it. |
| Whose name is on the customer invoice? | You invoice the customer. You are a seller in your own right. | The brand invoices. You earn a commission or a service fee. |
| Who sets the resale price? | You do. This is what resale means. | The brand does. Check whether the clause is legal (see the competition section below). |

Mostly "you" is a dealership or distributorship, whatever it is called. Mostly "the brand" is an agency or a commission arrangement. A franchise sits across both. You often buy and invoice in your own name, but you also take a licence to use the brand and follow its operating system.

## What Indian law says, and where it says nothing

**We did not find an Indian statute that defines or regulates franchising.** Law-firm guides we read say the same. We could not open a government source that states it, and a missing law is hard to prove from a primary document. The table below lists the laws that apply to the pieces of a franchise. It is not a franchise code. Ask your lawyer this question directly.

| Legal point | Primary source | What the text says | Why it matters to you |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Contract | [Indian Contract Act, 1872](https://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/2187/2/A187209.pdf), sections 27 and 74 | Section 27: an agreement that restrains anyone from a lawful profession, trade or business is void to that extent, with a saving for the sale of goodwill. Section 74: where a sum is named for breach, the injured party gets reasonable compensation not exceeding that sum, whether or not actual loss is proved. | A post-exit non-compete may not hold as written. A forfeiture or penalty clause is tested against section 74. Ask a lawyer how both apply to your draft. |
| Agency | Same Act, section 182 | An agent is a person employed to do any act for another, or to represent another in dealings with third persons. | If you act for the brand in front of customers, you may be an agent, whatever the contract title says. |
| Brand licence | [Trade Marks Act, 1999](https://ipindia.gov.in/tm-act-1999), sections 2(1)(r), 48 and 49 | "Permitted use" covers a registered user, and also use by another person by consent of the registered proprietor in a written agreement. A person may be registered as a registered user, applying jointly with the proprietor. | You should hold a written licence naming the mark, the goods and the conditions. Registration as a user is not compulsory under the text we read. |
| Competition | [Competition Act, 2002](https://www.cci.gov.in/images/legalframeworkact/en/the-competition-act-20021652103427.pdf), section 3(4), as amended by the [Competition (Amendment) Act, 2023](https://www.cci.gov.in/images/legalframeworkact/en/the-competition-amendment-act-20231681363446.pdf) | Tie-in, exclusive dealing, exclusive distribution, refusal to deal and resale price maintenance are caught only if they cause or are likely to cause an appreciable adverse effect on competition in India. | An exclusive territory or a mandated resale price is not automatically unlawful, and not automatically safe. |
| Tax | [CGST Act, 2017](https://cbic-gst.gov.in/pdf/CGST-Act-Updated-30092020.pdf), section 2(5) and Schedule I | An agent is a person who carries on the business of supply or receipt of goods or services on behalf of another. Supply of goods by a principal to an agent who undertakes to supply them on the principal's behalf is a supply even without consideration (Schedule I, item 3). The same wording appears in CBIC's consolidated text of 1 January 2022 and in an ICAI compilation of November 2025. | Whether you are a buyer-reseller or an agent changes the invoice flow, and so your tax records. |

Four details deserve a closer read.

**Section 27 is about restraint.** The text voids restraints on a lawful trade "to that extent". It does not say what a court will do with a particular franchise clause. We give no view on in-term exclusivity or on how far a post-exit restriction can run. That is a question for your lawyer, with your draft in hand.

**The Trade Marks Act speaks of permitted use, not of "franchising".** The permitted-use definition has two limbs. One covers a registered user. The other covers a person who uses the mark with the proprietor's consent, under a written agreement, for goods or services connected with that person in the course of trade. In practice, without a signed written licence your right to display the brand is weak and the brand's right to stop you is strong.

**The Competition Act has two sides.** The 2023 amendment reworded section 3(4). It changed "exclusive supply" to "exclusive dealing" and added a proviso that the sub-section does not apply to an agreement between an enterprise and an end consumer. Resale price maintenance now covers "any direct or indirect restriction" on the resale price, with the original saving where the agreement clearly allows lower prices. Section 3(5)(i)(c) separately preserves the right to impose reasonable conditions necessary to protect rights under the Trade Marks Act. The Ministry of Corporate Affairs notification S.O. 2228(E) of 18 May 2023, which we opened on 31 October 2026, brought sections 1 to 5 of the 2023 Act into force from that date. Section 4 of the 2023 Act is the one that amends section 3. We did not check the commencement of the Act's other provisions, so confirm the current text.

**The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 also applies to the sale to the end customer.** We have not cited a section here. Under any structure, ask who answers the customer when something goes wrong.

## Five models, compared by what each one holds

These are the usual structures. A real agreement can mix them, so test your own draft against the four facts above.

| Model | Stock | Customer contract and invoice | Brand rights | Money flows to the brand | Main risk for you |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Franchise | Often yours, sometimes shipped per order | Often yours, under a prescribed process and brand standards | Licence to use the mark and system | Fees, often an entry fee plus a recurring charge, set by the contract | Paying for rights you cannot enforce or use |
| Dealership | Yours | Yours | Usually only a right to say "authorised" | Goods bought at an agreed price | Stock you cannot sell and credit you cannot collect |
| Distributor | Yours, in bulk | Yours, selling to dealers and installers | Right to supply the network | Goods bought at an agreed price | Large stock, large receivables, channel conflict |
| Authorised installer or channel partner | Usually the brand's or the project's | Often the customer's contract is with you for installation | A listing or approval | Sometimes none, sometimes a fee for training or approval | Doing the site work without owning the product sale |
| Reseller agent or referral partner | None | The brand or the installer contracts with the customer | None or minimal | Commission to you | No control over the sale, and commission disputes |

Two of these tend to cause confusion in solar.

**"Authorised" is a statement about permission, not about ownership.** An authorised installer or dealer has been allowed to sell or fit a brand's products. That says nothing about territory, exclusivity or a right to return stock. Our guide to [dealer vs distributor vs EPC](/blog/dealer-vs-distributor-vs-epc/) shows how the roles overlap and why the signed scope must settle them.

**A franchise that sells you goods is also a dealership.** If you must buy product from the franchisor and resell it, the stock, credit and return questions of a dealership apply on top of the brand-licence questions. A [clause checklist for the dealer agreement](/blog/solar-dealer-agreement-checklist/) covers those.

## What does a solar franchise fee actually buy?

Solar franchise offers in India usually describe an entry fee, sometimes a security deposit, and sometimes a recurring royalty or marketing charge. We could not find a dated primary source that publishes typical amounts. The franchise pages we saw were written by companies selling franchises, so we give no ranges.

Ask the franchisor to separate the payment into items, with a written reason for each:

| Fee item | What to ask |
| --- | --- |
| Entry or licence fee | What rights does it buy, for how long, and is any part refundable if the franchisor fails to deliver? |
| Security deposit | Under what conditions is it returned, and in how many days? |
| Training | How many people, how many days, and is the certificate recognised outside the brand? |
| Leads | Are they promised in the contract, or only described in the sales pitch? |
| Recurring royalty or marketing charge | On what base is it charged, and can it change by notice? |
| Mandatory purchases | Are you required to buy stock, tools, signage or software from the brand or a named vendor? |

If the contract says nothing about leads, you have bought a licence, not a lead stream.

## Franchise vs dealership economics: an illustrative comparison

**All numbers below are assumed for illustration. They are not market data and not any brand's terms.**

Take a dealer and a franchisee in the same local market, each with fixed costs of Rs 60,000 a month.

| Line | Dealership (assumed) | Franchise (assumed) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly sales, ex-GST | Rs 10,00,000 | Rs 10,00,000 |
| Gross margin | 10% = Rs 1,00,000 | 12% = Rs 1,20,000 |
| Recurring royalty | none | 3% of sales = Rs 30,000 |
| Entry fee spread over 24 months | none | Rs 4,80,000 / 24 = Rs 20,000 |
| Fixed costs | Rs 60,000 | Rs 60,000 |
| **Monthly result before tax** | **Rs 40,000** | **Rs 10,000** |

On these assumptions the franchise earns 9% (12% less 3%) against the dealer's 10%, and it also carries the entry fee. To match the dealer's Rs 40,000, the franchisee needs sales of about Rs 13,33,333 a month. At that level, 9% is Rs 1,20,000, less Rs 80,000 of fixed costs and fee, which leaves Rs 40,000. That is about one third more volume. **Ask the franchisor, in writing, how much extra volume its leads, pricing and brand will bring.**

Cash at risk before the first sale also differs (assumed). The dealer buys Rs 6,00,000 of opening stock. The franchisee pays the Rs 4,80,000 entry fee, a Rs 1,00,000 refundable deposit and Rs 3,00,000 of stock, Rs 8,80,000 in all. If either business closes, unsold stock can still be sold or returned if the agreement allows it. A fee already spent cannot. ## Which model fits which starting point

| If you are | Look first at | Why | What to watch |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Starting with little capital and no technical background | Referral or reseller agent, or training first | Almost no stock or fee at risk | Get commission terms and payment dates in writing |
| An electrician or installer with a crew | Authorised installer or channel partner | Your skill is the asset, and stock is the brand's concern | You do the site work, so check who bears warranty claims and what [electrical licensing](/blog/electrical-contractor-licence-solar-installers/) your state expects  |
| A shop owner with walk-in customers | Dealership | You already have the premises and the customers | Stock and customer credit, not the headline margin |
| An EPC with project contacts | Distributor or dealer for components, not a retail franchise | You buy for projects, and a retail brand fee may not help | Whether resale is even allowed and at what price |
| A multi-brand trader | Distributorship or a second dealership | You already carry stock and receivables | Channel conflict with brands you already sell |
| Someone who values a recognised brand and a ready process | Franchise | You may get training and a system | The fee, the leads promise and the exit |

None of these rows is advice for you. They show where each structure usually fits.

## Does a solar franchise or dealership give you an exclusive territory?

Not unless the contract names one. The Competition Act text treats an agreement that allocates an area or market for selling goods as exclusive distribution. That is not unlawful in itself, because section 3(4) turns on an appreciable adverse effect on competition. For a small local dealer or franchisee the practical point runs the other way: **a territory the contract does not name is a territory you do not have.** Check whether the grant is exclusive, sole or non-exclusive, whether online sales and projects are carved out, and who owns a lead that arrives from outside your area. The dealer agreement checklist has the full set of territory questions.

## Invoice and tax flow: three common patterns

| Pattern | Who invoices the customer | Who invoices whom | Points to check with your accountant |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Resale (dealer or distributor) | You | Brand invoices you, you invoice the customer | Your own GST registration and the rate on each product |
| Agency or commission | The brand | You invoice the brand for your commission or fee | Whether you are an "agent" under the CGST Act, and the position on any stock you hold for the brand |
| Franchise | Usually you, sometimes the brand | Brand invoices you for goods, and separately for fees or royalty | Treatment of each fee as its own item |

The CGST text quoted earlier makes the agency point sharply. Where a principal supplies goods to an agent who will supply them on the principal's behalf, the supply counts even with no payment. Do not assume that stock held "on consignment" is outside tax. We state no GST rate for franchise or royalty fees, because we did not verify one. ## Exit and stock return: where the models differ most

| Model | What usually stays with you on exit | Ask for in writing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dealership | Unsold stock, open receivables | Return or sell-off rules, with models, condition, timing, freight and credit note |
| Distributor | Larger stock, sub-dealer commitments | Treatment of stock held for downstream customers |
| Franchise | The fee already paid, brand-removal costs, a non-compete | Refund conditions, signage and system removal duties, and the scope of any post-exit restriction |
| Agent | Unpaid commission | Cut-off for commission on deals in progress |

Never assume a buyback. A buyback talked about in negotiation binds nobody unless it is in the signed documents. If there is a non-compete, take legal advice on section 27 before you sign, not after you leave.

## Due-diligence checklist before you sign

1. **Get the structure in one sentence, in writing.** For example: "You are appointed as a [franchisee / dealer / distributor / agent] for [products] in [territory]." If that sentence cannot be written, the structure is undefined.
2. **Check who the contracting party is.** Match the company name and GST registration on the draft, the quotation and the invoice.
3. **Ask for the written brand licence.** It should name the mark, goods, territory and conditions, and say whether you will be registered as a user.
4. **Search the mark.** Use the public search on the Trade Marks Registry to see whether the brand is registered and who the proprietor is.
5. **Split every payment.** List each fee, deposit and royalty with what it buys and whether it is refundable.
6. **Find every mandatory purchase.** Stock, tools, software, signage and training you must buy from the brand.
7. **Read the price clause.** If it fixes your resale price, ask a lawyer how the competition rules apply.
8. **Read the exit clauses together.** Termination, notice, stock return, deposit refund, non-compete and dispute process.
9. **Talk to existing partners.** Ask three, chosen by you rather than by the brand, what they paid, what they received and how exit went.
10. **Have a lawyer and an accountant read the actual draft.** Share this guide with them as a starting list, not as a substitute.
