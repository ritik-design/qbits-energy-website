**Solar financing for installers means getting the paperwork right, not lending, collecting fees or touching the loan money.** The customer is the borrower. A bank or finance company is the lender. Your job is a registered-vendor profile, an honest quotation and invoice, a site feasibility report and a vendor-customer agreement. The lender pays you under its own rules, and the subsidy goes to the customer's loan account, not to you.

This guide is for a small installer who gets asked "can I pay in instalments?" at almost every site visit. It reads the scheme loan terms from SBI's own page and the guidelines of the Ministry of New and Renewable Energy (MNRE). It reads what the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) says about agents who bring customers to lenders. It then works through an EMI example, shows what the loan does to your cash flow, and ends with a disclosure checklist you can hand to the customer.

This is general information as of **31 October 2026**, not legal, tax or credit advice. The sources below were read on 30 October 2026 and opened again on 31 October 2026. Every rate below is a snapshot that moves with the bank's benchmark, so quote the lender's current page, not this one. The guide states nothing about any brand's finance offer, including Qbits.

> **Short version**
> - Under PM Surya Ghar, the customer applies for the loan and the lender decides. You supply a quotation or invoice, a feasibility report and the vendor-customer agreement, and you must be a registered vendor on the national portal.
> - SBI's page shows, as of 15 October 2026, a floating 6.00% for loans up to Rs 2 lakh and 8.15% above that, up to Rs 6 lakh. The tenure is up to 120 months including a 6-month moratorium, and the customer pays a 10% minimum margin.
> - The RBI Digital Lending Directions, 2025 say the lender pays a lending service provider (LSP), not the borrower. No third party may sit in the flow of loan funds.
> - The central subsidy is credited to the customer's loan account after the power distribution company (DISCOM) inspects. Never promise it as an advance or count it as your receipt.
> - Show the customer the total interest, the floating-rate warning and who gets the money, in writing, before they sign.

## Solar financing for installers: what you do and what stays with the bank

The MNRE guidelines for the residential subsidy are an Office Memorandum dated 7 June 2024. Clause 6 sets out the chain of roles. The consumer applies on the national portal, chooses a vendor registered there, and agrees system design and financial terms with that vendor. The consumer may opt for financing through the portal, and the loan products of banks and financial institutions are meant to be listed there.

From that text, and from the SBI page below, the split of work looks like this.

| Task | Installer | Customer | Lender |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Register on the national portal as a vendor | Yes (see our [vendor registration guide](/blog/pm-surya-ghar-vendor-registration-installers/)) | No | No |
| Apply for the loan | Helps with documents | Applies in own name | Decides |
| Quotation, invoice, site feasibility report, vendor-customer agreement | Prepares | Signs | Reads them before it releases money |
| Pay the margin (the customer's share) | Receives | Pays | Sets the minimum |
| Release the loan amount | Receives it against the invoice | None | Pays, per its rules |
| Install, commission, support for five years | Yes | Gives access and pays any agreed extras | None |
| Claim the central subsidy | Helps upload documents | Claims on the portal | Receives it into the loan account |

Clause 7 of the same guidelines says registered vendors must give repairs and maintenance free for five years from commissioning. They must also rectify any deficiency that stops the subsidy being paid. A loan does not change that duty. It does mean a defect can hold up money the bank has already agreed to release, so a clean commissioning matters more with finance than with cash.

If you are also working out how to win these customers in the first place, our guide to [how solar installers get customers](/blog/how-solar-installers-get-customers/) covers the channels.

## The scheme loan, term by term

The headline facts first, each with its source and date.

| Term | What the source says | Source and date read |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who it is for | Individual applicants with rooftop rights, sufficient roof area as MNRE requires, a valid electricity connection, a savings account with the bank and no prior solar subsidy | SBI Surya Ghar loan page, read 30 and 31 Oct 2026, page marked last updated 19 Oct 2026 |
| Loan ceiling | Up to Rs 2 lakh for systems up to 3 kW. Above Rs 2 lakh and up to Rs 6 lakh for systems above 3 kW and up to 10 kW | Same SBI page |
| Interest, up to Rs 2 lakh | Floating, external benchmark lending rate (EBLR) minus 2.15%, shown as 6.00% effective as of 15 Oct 2026 | Same SBI page, rate table |
| Interest, Rs 2 lakh to Rs 6 lakh | Floating, EBLR plus 0%, shown as 8.15% effective as of 15 Oct 2026 | Same SBI page, rate table |
| Tenure | Maximum 120 months including a 6-month moratorium from disbursement. No minimum period and no prepayment penalty | Same SBI page |
| Customer margin | Minimum 10% of project cost, paid upfront | Same SBI page |
| Security | "Hypothecation of Assets" | Same SBI page |
| Processing fee | Nil | Same SBI page |
| Disbursal | Directly to the vendor or EPC contractor, after the feasibility reports MNRE requires, for the loan amount plus the borrower's margin | Same SBI page |
| Application route | Register at the PM Surya Ghar portal first, then apply through the Jan Samarth portal, or apply from the bank's own site | Same SBI page |

Three cautions on these numbers.

**The rate is a snapshot.** The same SBI page still carries an older FAQ line. It gives 5.75% and 7.90% "as on" 15 December 2025, against 6.00% and 8.15% in the table dated 15 October 2026. The Press Information Bureau (PIB) release of 8 December 2025 described the scheme loan as repo rate plus 50 basis points, "i.e. 6% per annum for the present", with a 10-year tenure. An earlier PIB release of 13 March 2025 said 6.75% on loans up to Rs 2 lakh through 12 public sector banks. Different dates, different numbers, one floating product. We re-opened the SBI page and both PIB releases on 31 October 2026, and the figures and dates above were unchanged. Print no rate on a flyer without its date and the lender's name.

**Other banks differ.** Indian Bank's page for the same scheme, marked last updated 29 April 2026, gives repo plus 50 basis points up to Rs 2 lakh. It also shows a 680 credit-score floor and a margin of 10% or 20%. SBI's page has a 10% margin and no minimum score up to Rs 2 lakh. We opened Indian Bank's page directly in a browser on 31 October 2026, and it still showed last updated 29 April 2026 with the figures above. Check the bank's site before you quote it. We did not read any other lender's current page.

**The MNRE text says "envisaged".** Clause 6(r) says standardised low-interest loan products in the range of repo plus 50 basis points are "envisaged" for residential systems up to 3 kW. Similar products for larger and non-subsidised systems are to be published on the portal. The binding terms are the lender's.

PIB calls these loans collateral-free, while the SBI table lists the security as hypothecation of the financed assets. Our reading is that no other property is pledged, but the system itself is charged to the bank. Ask the branch to confirm in writing what the customer is pledging, because the customer will ask you.

## Who the borrower can be, and which documents decide it

The SBI page is written for individuals. MNRE's subsidy is for residential consumers; clause 5(d) says no central subsidy for commercial, industrial or government segments. We did not read a loan product for firms, housing societies or commercial roofs, so this guide covers the individual homeowner. For a business customer's own loan, ask the lender about its business product. The [working capital guide for dealers](/blog/working-capital-solar-dealers/) shows how lenders read cash cycles.

What the SBI page lists as documents, by loan size:

| Item | Up to Rs 2 lakh | Above Rs 2 lakh up to Rs 6 lakh |
| --- | --- | --- |
| KYC | Required | Required |
| Electricity bill | Copy; physical bill not mandatory | Copy |
| PAN | Not mandatory | Mandatory |
| Income proof | Not stipulated | Salary slip or statement, Form 16, pension slip or ITR, as the page lists |
| Credit score | No minimum prescribed; defaults or write-offs on the credit record are not considered | 650 or above; defaults or write-offs not considered |
| Insurance of the system | Not mandatory | Mandatory, paid by the customer |
| From the vendor | Quotation or invoice, site feasibility report, vendor-customer agreement | Same |

Two page quirks to clear with the branch. The page says new-to-credit customers are eligible. Its income line also reads "Minimum" Rs 3 lakh net annual income in the features table but "Max NAI is Rs 3 Lakhs" in the documents table for the larger loan. Both wordings were still on the page on 31 October 2026. Confirm which one the branch applies.

On credit scores, we have only what the lenders print. A "no minimum" for the small loan does not mean approval; the bank still decides, and a recent default can end the application. Tell the customer to ask their bank for the credit-score check before you design a Rs 6 lakh system around a loan that may not come.

## Sequencing the subsidy and the loan

The central subsidy under clause 5(h) is Rs 30,000 per kW for the first 2 kW and Rs 18,000 for the third kW. Nothing is paid beyond 3 kW, so a 3 kW system gets Rs 78,000. Special-category states have higher per-kW amounts. The subsidy is calculated on benchmark costs of Rs 50,000 per kW for the first 2 kW and Rs 45,000 for the additional kW. Those are for the subsidy sum. Clause 6(c) says the consumer is free to agree rates with any registered vendor, so the benchmark is not a price cap.

The order of events, from the guidelines and the bank pages:

1. Customer registers and applies on the national portal and gets feasibility clearance where needed.
2. Customer chooses a registered vendor and signs a vendor-customer agreement. The guidelines' model agreement says payment schedule is mutually agreed and the consumer may opt for milestone-based payment.
3. Customer applies for the loan, through the Jan Samarth portal or the bank, with your quotation, feasibility report and agreement.
4. The bank sanctions. It pays you the loan amount plus the customer's margin, against the documents, under its own schedule.
5. You install. The consumer then updates the system details on the portal, with geo-tagged photographs, and you may help with that.
6. The DISCOM physically inspects. Clause 6(k) says the subsidy is processed only after the DISCOM has physically verified the system.
7. An e-token is activated with the eligible amount. When the consumer redeems it, the subsidy goes to the loan account. If it exceeds the outstanding loan, the excess goes to the consumer's bank account (clause 6(s)).
8. The bank adjusts the loan. Whether it cuts the equated monthly instalment (EMI) or the tenure is the bank's choice.

On timing, clause 6(t) says the subsidy "will be processed within 15 days of approval by the concerned DISCOM". PIB's March 2025 release said the average was around 15 days after the redemption request. We could not confirm the current real-world time. Tell customers the guideline figure and that your control ends at the portal upload.

Note the scheme period. The guidelines say implementation runs until 31 March 2027. We opened the 7 June 2024 memorandum again on 31 October 2026, and the subsidy amounts, benchmark costs, clause 6(t) and the other clauses cited here read as quoted. A search listing shows an MNRE notice titled "Amendment in Guidelines for Implementation of PM-Surya Ghar: Muft Bijli Yojana for the component of CFA to residential consumers", dated 7 July 2025. MNRE's page for it returned "not found" when we tried, so we have not read it and do not know what it changed.

## EMI arithmetic you can show a customer

The formula for a fixed-rate-looking monthly instalment is:

**EMI = P x r x (1 + r)^n / ((1 + r)^n - 1)**

where P is the loan amount, r is the annual rate divided by 12 and by 100, and n is the number of monthly instalments. The loan here floats, so this gives the EMI at one rate on one day. When the bank's benchmark moves, the EMI or tenure moves.

**Illustrative example, not a price or a lender offer.** Assume a 3 kW system quoted at Rs 2,00,000. The customer margin is 10% (Rs 20,000) and the loan is Rs 1,80,000. Take 6.00% a year, the rate shown in SBI's table for loans up to Rs 2 lakh on 15 October 2026, over 120 months. Here r is 0.5% a month and (1.005)^120 is about 1.8194.

| Item | Working | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly EMI | 180000 x 0.005 x 1.8194 / 0.8194 | about Rs 1,998 |
| Total repaid | Rs 1,998.37 x 120 | about Rs 2,39,804 |
| Total interest | Rs 2,39,804 less Rs 1,80,000 | about Rs 59,804 |
| Quick rule at this rate and tenure | per Rs 1 lakh borrowed | about Rs 1,110 a month |

Now the subsidy. Suppose, for the arithmetic only, that the Rs 78,000 is credited to the loan after three EMIs. The outstanding balance then falls from about Rs 1,76,688 to about Rs 98,688. Two outcomes are possible, and which one happens is the bank's call.

| If the bank | Monthly EMI | Total interest over the life of the loan |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recasts the EMI over the remaining 117 months | about Rs 1,116 | about Rs 34,600 |
| Keeps the EMI at Rs 1,998 and ends the loan sooner | about Rs 1,998 | about Rs 17,600, finished in about 60 months in all |

Simplifications: the example starts instalments at once, ignores the 6-month moratorium, ignores insurance and ignores later rate changes. SBI's page does not say whether interest builds up during the moratorium, and we could not confirm it, so ask for the repayment schedule. Show the customer the last column. The subsidy cuts interest a lot if it reduces the balance early, so the customer should know which way the bank applies it.

## What the loan does to your cash flow

When the customer pays by loan, your money comes in two or three pieces from a payer who is not your customer. Indian Bank's page, which we read directly on 31 October 2026 and which is marked last updated 29 April 2026, says 70% of project cost is disbursed upfront, including the applicant's margin. The remaining 30% follows within 30 days of successful installation and verification. The SBI page we read states no split and says disbursal follows the feasibility reports. Ask each lender for its own schedule in writing.

The table below uses the 70/30 pattern with made-up costs, to show the shape. It is illustrative.

| Day (assumed) | Event | Cash in | Cash out | Running position |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | Pay suppliers on order | | Rs 1,20,000 | minus Rs 1,20,000 |
| 10 | Bank releases 70% with the customer's margin | Rs 1,40,000 | | plus Rs 20,000 |
| 20 | Balance to suppliers | | Rs 40,000 | minus Rs 20,000 |
| 25 | Install, labour and transport | | Rs 10,000 | minus Rs 30,000 |
| 55 | Bank releases the final 30%, after verification | Rs 60,000 | | plus Rs 30,000 |

Three lessons come out of that. First, the deepest hole is day 5, before the bank pays, so time your supplier payments to the sanction, not the quotation. Second, the last 30% is your whole margin on the job; if verification or the DISCOM inspection slips, your margin waits. Third, the subsidy never reaches you. Any plan that counts it as your income is wrong. So is lending the customer the subsidy amount "until it arrives": you would be funding the scheme from your own pocket.

For funding your own gap between supplier payment and bank payment, see the [working capital guide](/blog/working-capital-solar-dealers/).

## What the RBI rules say about agents who bring customers

The RBI (Digital Lending) Directions, 2025, circular RBI/2025-26/36 dated 8 May 2025, apply to the digital lending of banks, co-operative banks, non-banking finance companies (NBFCs) and all-India financial institutions. We read the text on the RBI website on 30 October 2026 and again on 31 October 2026, and the paragraph numbers below matched. They replaced the 2022 digital lending guidelines and related circulars.

The Directions define a lending service provider (LSP) as an agent of the lender that carries out one or more of its digital lending functions in customer acquisition, services incidental to underwriting and pricing, servicing, monitoring or recovery. Whether a given installer is an LSP depends on what the lender has asked you to do and what contract you signed. Ask the lender how it classifies you. We did not read the RBI's outsourcing master direction, so we say nothing on the wider outsourcing rules.

Where the Directions apply, the text says:

- **Fees.** Para 9(iv): fees and charges payable to the LSP "are paid directly by the RE", the regulated lender, "and are not charged to or collected from the borrowers separately by LSP". The SBI page also lists the processing fee as nil. So an installer should not charge a "loan processing" or "file" fee to the customer.
- **Funds.** Para 9(i) to (iii): the loan goes to the borrower's bank account, with exceptions that include disbursals for a specific end use paid directly to the end-beneficiary's account. The lender must ensure no disbursal goes to a third-party account, including an LSP's, other than as the Directions allow. Repayments go from the borrower directly to the lender, without any pass-through or pool account of a third party. The borrower-lender flow must not be controlled, directly or indirectly, by a third party. In plain terms, and this is our summary: do not take the customer's EMI. Do not hold loan money. Do not ask for the customer's net-banking login.
- **Disclosure.** Para 8(i) and (iii): the lender provides a Key Fact Statement and digitally signed documents, including the sanction letter, directly to the borrower. The customer should get the loan's key facts from the lender, not from your quotation.
- **Several lenders.** Para 6: where an LSP has agreements with more than one lender, it must show the borrower every matching offer, with names, amounts, tenor, APR and monthly repayment. It must not push one lender with dark patterns. This applies to LSPs partnering with multiple lenders. Check with your lenders how it applies to you if you refer customers to more than one.
- **Cooling-off.** Para 10: a digital loan must carry an explicit option to exit, with principal and proportionate APR, within a cooling-off period of at least one day, set by the lender's board.
- **Loss cover.** Chapter VI defines default-loss cover, written DLG, as a contract in which another entity promises to compensate the lender for defaults up to a percentage specified upfront, including implicit arrangements of that kind. A lender may enter such an arrangement only with an LSP or another regulated entity, and the LSP giving the cover must be a company under the Companies Act, 2013 (para 18). The cover is capped at 5% of the amount disbursed on the portfolio (para 23). It must be held as cash with the lender, a fixed deposit with a lien, or a bank instrument in the lender's favour (para 22). So an installer should not promise a lender or a customer that it will cover defaults. Ask the lender in writing before agreeing any such term.

We read nothing on how a lender may pay an installer a sourcing commission. That is a matter between you and the lender's agreement, which is why a written deal matters. Our [dealer agreement checklist](/blog/solar-dealer-agreement-checklist/) shows what to look for, though it is written for brand-dealer agreements, not lender agreements.

## Three partner models, by function

You will meet lenders in three shapes. Describe them by what they do, and do not assume one is better.

| Model | How it works | Installer's job | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Direct bank route through the portals | Customer applies on the national portal and Jan Samarth or at the bank. The installer is only the vendor on the invoice | Documents, invoice, installation, portal upload | Registered vendor profile and bank beneficiary account in the same name |
| Lender-appointed agent or channel partner | A bank or NBFC signs you as its agent to bring customers, under a contract | Customer acquisition and document help, and any other function the contract lists | Written scope, how and when the lender pays you, that no fee is taken from the customer, whether the lender treats you as an LSP |
| Multi-lender platform | A fintech or aggregator shows offers from several lenders and the customer picks one | Introduces the customer, or is introduced by the platform | That it shows every matching offer neutrally, per Para 6, and that the lender, not the platform, sends the Key Fact Statement |

The SBI page says its loan is available for a "Self-Sourced Journey as well as assisted journey", so the first two models can exist side by side for the same product. We read no NBFC solar loan product page, so we state no NBFC rate, tenure or margin.

## Your own working capital: the CGTMSE option

CGTMSE is a trust that runs a credit-cover scheme for micro and small enterprises. The Small Industries Development Bank of India (SIDBI) page on it, which we read on 30 and 31 October 2026, says SIDBI and the Ministry of MSME set up the trust in 2000. The cover applies to facilities that member lenders extend without collateral or third-party security, or with only part of it. It says the limit of cover was raised from Rs 2 crore to Rs 5 crore with effect from 1 April 2023. Other websites report a later change; we found no primary page that confirms one, so we quote only the SIDBI figure.

That page does not say whether an installer's trading or contracting activity qualifies, or what a bank will lend. Those depend on the lender's membership and its own credit view. Have your Udyam and GST papers ready; our [guide to GST and Udyam registration](/blog/gst-udyam-solar-inverter-dealer/) lists them.

## Pitfalls that cost installers money

| Pitfall | Why it hurts | What the sources say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inflated invoice, to cover the customer's margin | The customer borrows more than the system costs, the bank pays against your invoice, and the paperwork no longer matches reality | The bank releases loan plus margin against the quotation or invoice. The MNRE guidelines allow de-registration or penalties for non-compliance with scheme rules after notice (clause 7(e)). We found no text that names this practice |
| Vendor name on the invoice differs from the portal registration | The bank pays registered vendors; a mismatch can stall disbursal | SBI's page says disbursal is to the vendor or channel partner or EPC contractor "who are registered in the National portal". The mismatch risk is our reading, not stated |
| Promising the subsidy in advance, or lending it | You fund the gap, and it only releases after DISCOM inspection and e-token redemption | MNRE clause 6(k) and 6(p) to 6(s) |
| "Zero cost solar" or "free" in your marketing | The customer still pays the margin and the loan, and the subsidy covers part of the cost | PIB's 10 June 2022 note on the consumer authority's advertising guidelines says they define what counts as a free claim and that a disclaimer must not try to correct a claim that is itself deceptive. We read PIB's summary, not the full notified text. Separately, the RBI Directions footnote 12 says credit-card EMI conversion with interest "shall not be camouflaged as zero-interest/no-cost EMI" |
| Collecting a "loan fee" from the customer | Conflicts with the nil processing fee and with RBI's fee rule where it applies | SBI page; RBI Directions para 9(iv) |
| Handling the customer's EMI or loan money | Third-party control of fund flow is what the Directions bar | RBI Directions para 9(ii) and (iii) |
| Guessing approval | The customer cancels other options on a promise | The bank decides. The SBI page says defaults or write-offs on the credit record are not considered |
| Ignoring the five-year duty | A failed system can stop the subsidy and the customer stops paying EMI | MNRE clause 7(d) |

## A disclosure checklist to give the customer

Give this as a one-page sheet with the quotation, and keep a signed copy. Items marked "ours" are our suggestions, not requirements we found in a rule.

1. System size in kW, brand and model of panels and inverter, and the full price including GST.
2. Margin the customer pays, loan amount applied for, and who pays the bank's insurance charge where the bank requires it.
3. Name of the lender, and that the lender, not the installer, decides the loan.
4. Interest rate with its date, stated as floating, with how the rate follows the lender's benchmark.
5. EMI, tenure, any moratorium, and total interest at that rate, shown as illustrative.
6. That the lender sends the Key Fact Statement and sanction letter; the customer should read those, not only your sheet.
7. That the lender pays the loan to the installer's account against the invoice, and the customer pays no loan fee to the installer.
8. That the customer claims the central subsidy (Rs 78,000 for a 3 kW system on the guideline's table). It is paid only after DISCOM inspection and credited to the loan account.
9. That the subsidy timing is outside the installer's control, and how the bank will adjust the EMI or tenure afterwards.
10. That the system is charged to the bank as security.
11. Your five-year service duty and who the customer calls.
12. Any commission you receive from the lender (ours).
13. Where to complain: the lender's grievance contact, and the RBI complaint portal named in the lender's documents.
