## The Headline Rate Is Usually Unreachable

> **What most New Zealand quotes get wrong.** They take the customer's headline buyback rate, multiply it by annual export, and present the result. On a flat plan that is roughly right. On a time-varying plan the headline figure is the peak, the peak falls in the evening, and an array without storage exports almost nothing then.

The gap between the advertised rate and the achievable rate is the central honesty problem in New Zealand quoting today, and it grew rather than shrank when time-varying plans became mandatory for the majors.

| What the customer sees advertised | What an array without storage actually earns |
| --- | --- |
| A high evening peak rate | Nothing, no generation at that hour |
| A midday trough rate | This is where nearly all export lands |
| A headline "up to" figure | Reachable only with storage |

A proposal that resolves this properly does three things: it shows exported volume by time of day, it applies the rate applicable to each period, and it states the effective average rate achieved. That last number is the honest one, and it is frequently a long way below the advertised peak.

The commercial upside is significant. A customer holding your document, showing an effective achieved rate, can immediately see that a competitor's quote applying the peak rate to total export is not credible.

## Recommend The Plan, Not Just The Hardware

With no government-mandated feed-in tariff, every rate is a commercial term set by the retailer, and the spread between plans available to a single household can be worth more than any equipment decision on the table.

Modelling the same system on two or three plans is straightforward with the right tool and it is among the most valuable things a proposal can contain. It also has a useful property: it is advice that costs you nothing and demonstrably serves the customer, which builds the credibility you need for the parts of the document that do sell something.

There is a caveat to state plainly. Because these are commercial terms, they can change, and a customer who switches plans for a good export rate may find it revised. That is a reason to disclose the risk, not to withhold the recommendation.

## Disclose The Rate Risk

A twenty-year projection built on a current peak rate is making an assumption about a number that nobody has guaranteed. The rule introduced in July 2026 sets the shape of time-varying pricing and explicitly not its level, and there is no floor.

The straightforward treatment is a sensitivity line: here is the projection at today's rate, here is the same projection if export rates fall materially. Two columns, no drama.

That framing has a second benefit. It makes visible that self-consumption value is insulated from rate risk while export revenue is not, because a kilowatt-hour the household uses avoids retail import regardless of what the retailer does with buyback. That is a genuine argument for prioritising self-consumption, and it lands better when it emerges from a sensitivity table than when it is asserted.

## Storage Belongs In The Main Comparison

On a time-varying plan the battery is not an upsell, it is the thing that makes the plan work. Without it the household exports into the trough and buys its evening energy at retail, which is worse than a decent flat plan.

That means storage should appear as a priced option in the primary comparison rather than as an appendix, and the comparison should show the mechanism rather than only the total. A customer who can see midday surplus being held and released into the evening understands what they are buying.

Keep the ordering honest, though. A kilowatt-hour consumed directly by the household avoids the retail import price, which on most plans exceeds any export rate available. So the battery's highest-value job is covering the household's own evening demand, and exporting into the peak is what it should do with whatever remains. A proposal that leads with peak-export arbitrage and neglects avoided import has the priorities backwards, even though the arbitrage story is more exciting.

## Worked Example: The Advertised Rate Versus The Achieved Rate

An illustrative calculation, not a specific customer project. Inputs: 6 kW array, household with modest daytime and strong evening consumption, on a time-varying plan whose advertised winter peak is high and whose midday rate is low.

| | Quoted at the advertised peak | Quoted at the achieved profile | With storage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Export at midday trough | Priced at peak | Priced at trough | Mostly stored instead |
| Export at evening peak | Assumed substantial | Nearly none, no generation | Residual after household demand |
| Evening household demand | Ignored | Imported at retail | Served from battery |
| Effective export rate achieved | Advertised peak | Far below it | Substantially higher |
| Is the quote accurate? | No | Yes | Yes |

The first column is the quote a competitor produces from a plan comparison website and a spreadsheet. It is wrong in a specific and checkable way, and its error runs in the customer's disfavour once the first year's statements arrive.

The second column is honest and looks worse. That is the commercial problem, and the third column is the answer to it. Storage does not merely improve the return, it is what makes the advertised peak rate reachable at all, and framing it that way is both accurate and considerably more compelling than presenting a battery as an efficiency upgrade.
