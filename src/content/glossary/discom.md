## What is a DISCOM?

A **DISCOM** is an electricity distribution company. It operates or administers the distribution service for a defined area and is usually the company named on the electricity bill. Its customer-facing responsibilities include the connection, meter, billing and local network process under applicable regulation.

> [!KEYTAKEAWAYS]
> - A DISCOM is the electricity distribution company on your bill. For rooftop solar it handles approval, the meter, interconnection and billing.
> - Approval route, capacity limits and export settlement depend on the state regulator and that utility's current procedure, so do not assume every DISCOM works the same way.
> - The 2024 consumer-rights amendment puts clocks on part of the process, for example a 15-day feasibility study and deemed acceptance up to 10 kW.
> - Keep dated receipts, the application number and written reasons for any query or rejection.

In rooftop solar, the DISCOM is the utility contact for an eligible grid connection. The exact approval route, technical limits, meter type, export settlement and billing rules depend on the state regulator and that utility's current procedure.

## DISCOM explained simply

The DISCOM is the company on your electricity bill. It owns or looks after the poles, wires and transformers from the local substation to your meter, reads the meter, sends the bill and handles complaints when supply fails. If you add rooftop solar, you deal with the same company for approval, the meter and the interconnection.

## Where a DISCOM sits in the power system

Power moves in stages. Generators produce it, transmission utilities carry it at high voltage to substations, and the DISCOM takes it from there:

1. It buys bulk power through long-term contracts, short-term arrangements and exchanges, or receives it as allocated.
2. It receives power from the transmission network at its substations.
3. It distributes power over medium-voltage feeders to local transformers.
4. It steps the voltage down and supplies customers through service connections.
5. It reads meters, raises bills and collects payment.
6. It maintains the network and responds to outages.

In many states the DISCOMs are successors to the integrated state electricity boards, split into separate generation, transmission and distribution companies in the reforms that followed the [Electricity Act, 2003](/glossary/electricity-act-2003/). The details of each state's structure differ.

## Statutory time limits for rooftop solar

> [!IMPORTANT] Statutory clocks for rooftop solar
> The Ministry of Power's Electricity (Rights of Consumers) Amendment Rules, 2024, notified on 22 February 2024, put clocks on part of the process. Rule 11(7) says the technical feasibility study for rooftop solar must be completed and the outcome communicated within 15 days, failing which the proposal is presumed technically feasible. Rule 11(7A) says a complete application for a rooftop system up to 10 kW is deemed accepted without a feasibility study. The same amendment treats the cost of strengthening the network, including the distribution transformer, for systems up to 5 kW as part of the DISCOM's own revenue requirement unless the State Commission sets a higher capacity. A state's own regulation and the DISCOM's circular decide how this works in practice, so ask for the application's actual status and date stamps. Sources: [SCC Online summary](https://www.scconline.com/blog/post/2024/02/27/mop-notifies-electricity-rights-of-consumer-amendment-rules-2024-legal-news/) and [Mercom India](https://www.mercomindia.com/rooftop-solar-10-kw-exempted-feasibility-study-mandate), checked 14 October 2026.

## How to identify and contact the correct utility

Use the electricity bill's company name, consumer number and service address. A district can have more than one utility or a special service area; a generic state list can point to the wrong operator. Use the official customer portal or bill contact details for status, meter disputes and grievance channels.

A private or state-owned DISCOM is not inherently faster or slower for every solar application. This page does not assign universal approval times, national company counts or operator performance figures, because current comparable data is not available. For a real project, the utility's written instruction and dated application record are more useful than a national average.

## Smart meters, RDSS and solar

The Revamped Distribution Sector Scheme (RDSS), launched in 2021 by the Ministry of Power with REC and PFC as nodal agencies, aims to improve DISCOM finances and reliability through results-linked support, including prepaid smart metering and distribution upgrades. For a solar customer the visible effect is usually the meter. Check what meter your DISCOM will install, whether it records import and export separately, and how the meter will be sealed and commissioned. The [bidirectional meter](/glossary/bidirectional-meter/) entry explains what the meter must record. Source: [PIB release on RDSS](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=1779726&reg=3&lang=2).

## What happens at commissioning

At commissioning the DISCOM or its authorised inspector typically checks the installed equipment against the approved application, the earthing and protection arrangement, the meter, and the settings of the inverter's grid protection. Ask which equipment documents the DISCOM wants, since a mismatch between the approved model and the installed model is a frequent cause of delay.

## Common problems for solar customers

- Documents missing or inconsistent at application.
- A feasibility response that is slow or unclear.
- Meter availability and scheduling of the commissioning visit.
- The installed equipment differing from the approved application.
- Billing set up incorrectly after commissioning, so import and export are not credited as agreed.
- Subsidy steps waiting on commissioning data.

## Best practices

1. Use the DISCOM's online portal where one exists and keep date-stamped receipts.
2. Keep one copy of every submission and the application number.
3. Ask for written reasons for any rejection or query.
4. Escalate to the nodal officer, then the Consumer Grievance Redressal Forum, if timelines slip.
5. Check the first few bills for import and export entries.
6. Use an installer who handles that DISCOM's process regularly, and avoid unregistered vendors if you expect subsidy. See [why empanelled vendor status matters](/blog/empanelled-vendor-pm-surya-ghar/).

## Why this matters when choosing an inverter

> [!CAUTION] Grid protection settings are not a workaround
> The site connection phase, permitted export, approved protection settings and metering arrangement can affect the inverter selection. The [Qbits on-grid range](/on-grid-inverter/) and [datasheets](/download-datasheets/) provide model information, but the DISCOM and installer must confirm the actual connection and applicable rules. Do not change grid protection settings to work around an approval or voltage problem.

## Common mistakes

- Treating all DISCOMs as identical when each has its own portal, forms and queues.
- Skipping the feasibility step or not recording its outcome.
- Choosing an unregistered vendor and expecting subsidy.
- Not keeping meter sealing and installation records.
- Letting an application sit in a queue without follow-up.

## Key takeaways

The DISCOM is the operational gatekeeper for grid-connected rooftop solar. Approval, the meter, commissioning and billing all pass through it, and the rules differ by state. Use the utility's written responses and dated records, and use the statutory time limits where they apply.
