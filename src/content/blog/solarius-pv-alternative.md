## Why People Look for a Solarius PV Alternative

Almost nobody leaves Solarius PV because the engineering is weak. The reasons that come up are about fit, delivery, and what the market now requires.

### The proposal is not what the product is for

Solarius PV produces engineering output. The financial report speaks in NPV, IRR, payback, and cash flow, which is the right language for a commercial client's finance function and the wrong language for a homeowner deciding on a Saturday morning. Teams whose bottleneck is getting a persuasive, branded, savings-first document in front of a customer quickly end up building that document by hand or buying a second tool. Neither is free.

This is a positioning fact rather than a defect. ACCA built a document-production workflow for professionals who sign drawings, and it does that job well. It just does not do the other job.

### Windows desktop delivery

Solarius PV is a Windows desktop application. For a studio with fixed workstations, a shared server, and a policy against cloud storage of project data, that is an advantage rather than a limitation. For a distributed team, a Mac-based practice, or anyone who wants a surveyor to open the live model from a phone on site, it is friction. Decide honestly which of those describes your team.

### The Italian rules changed underneath the financial model

This is the reason that catches people out, and it is specific.

Scambio sul Posto, the net-metering-style exchange that shaped Italian residential solar economics for years, closed to systems commissioned from 29 May 2025 under ARERA resolution 78/2025/R/efr. New installations monetise exported energy through Ritiro Dedicato or a market offer instead. Ritiro Dedicato pays in the region of 8 to 14 cents per kWh depending on the period and the zone, against a retail tariff that is a multiple of that.

The consequence is arithmetic, not politics. Under the old mechanism an annual energy balance was a defensible approximation, because exports netted against imports. Under the new one, a self-consumed kilowatt-hour is worth several times an exported one, so payback depends on when generation lands relative to the load, and on whether a battery moves it. The decisive modelling capability is now hourly self-consumption and storage dispatch.

That is not a criticism of Solarius PV specifically. It is a test every tool on your shortlist has to pass, including the one you already own. A financial template that still assumes the old netting logic will quietly overstate payback on every proposal it produces.

## What to Evaluate in a Solarius PV Alternative

Before the list, the criteria. These are the five that actually separate the options.

**BIM and CAD interoperability.** If your geometry originates in a coordinated model, this is the first filter and it eliminates most of the market. ACCA holds IFC buildingSMART certification and lists Revit, ArchiCAD, AllPlan, VectorWorks, DXF, and DWG. Accepting a DWG import is not the same thing.

**Hourly self-consumption and storage.** Post-29 May 2025, an Italian residential proposal that is not built on an hourly balance is guessing. Ask to see the hourly output, not a headline yield figure.

**Who the output is for.** An engineering report and a sales proposal are different artefacts. Decide which one your business is short of, then buy for that constraint rather than for the longer feature list.

**Delivery model.** Windows desktop, browser, or both. This is a workflow question with a right answer per team, not a quality ranking.

**Price you can actually verify.** Solarius PV is quote-based. A published price beats a guessed one and makes the internal approval conversation shorter. Our [solar design software pricing comparison](/blog/solar-design-software-pricing/) sets out where the transparent vendors sit.

## The Six Best Solarius PV Alternatives

### 1. SurgePV, for design and proposals from one record

**Best for:** engineering-led installers and design offices that need the technical work and the client-facing document to come out of the same project, in any market.

SurgePV runs $1,299 to $1,899 per user per year depending on team size, and that single licence covers 3D layout, 8,760-hour module-level shading, financial modelling, and white-label proposals. Nothing is gated behind an upper tier and there is no separate proposal product to buy.

The Italian argument is the specific one. SurgePV models hourly self-consumption and Ritiro Dedicato directly, so the proposal reflects the mechanism that actually applies to systems commissioned now, without a spreadsheet in the middle of the workflow. Add a battery to the design and the self-consumption fraction, and therefore the payback, updates in the same model rather than in a parallel calculation. For a market where the export price sits around 8 to 14 cents per kWh against a much higher retail tariff, that is where the whole business case is decided.

It is browser-delivered, which solves the distributed-team and Mac problem outright, and it exports DXF and DWG for CAD handoff.

**What you give up:** the deep BIM interoperability. SurgePV does not replicate ACCA's IFC certification or its native Revit, ArchiCAD, AllPlan, and VectorWorks path, and it is not inside the usBIM ecosystem. For a practice whose every job starts from a coordinated architectural model, that is a real cost and it may outweigh everything above.

### 2. PVsyst, when the contract names it

**Best for:** projects where a lender, investor, or independent engineer specifies bankable yield simulation.

PVsyst is the reference standard, and where it is named in a contract the comparison ends before it starts. No amount of feature parity elsewhere substitutes for a contractual requirement.

It is not a document-production suite and not a sales tool, so it sits alongside whatever else you run rather than replacing Solarius PV. Our [PVsyst review](/blog/pvsyst-review/) covers what it justifies, and the [PVsyst alternative](/blog/pvsyst-alternative/) guide covers where it is overkill.

### 3. PV*SOL, for European residential detail

**Best for:** European residential and small commercial simulation, at a price you can read off a page.

Valentin Software's PV*SOL is priced around EUR 585 to 845 depending on edition and is the closest direct competitor to Solarius PV on the simulation side. It is strong on detailed behaviour of European residential systems including battery and self-consumption, which is exactly the calculation the Italian rule change made decisive.

The trade is that its CAD and BIM story is weaker than ACCA's, and like Solarius PV it is a desktop product whose output is technical rather than sales-shaped. If you are leaving Solarius PV because the proposal is your bottleneck, PV*SOL does not fix that. The [PV*SOL review](/blog/pvsol-review/) goes through the editions.

### 4. HelioScope, for commercial and industrial engineering

**Best for:** C&I layout and simulation where the sales document is somebody else's problem.

HelioScope has a mature reputation among engineering teams, a solid 8,760-hour simulation, and good layout flexibility on larger roofs and ground mount. Pricing is per user and sits well above the entry-level tools.

It is not positioned as a proposal builder, and its interface is engineer-first, which in our experience slows down sales staff. Its European code and tariff depth is not what it is marketed on, unlike a product written in Italy for Italian practice. See the [HelioScope review](/blog/helioscope-review/).

### 5. Aurora Solar, for United States residential

**Best for:** US residential installers, and mostly irrelevant to a European design office.

Aurora is the strongest residential platform in the United States, with deep NEC wiring, strong 3D modelling, and a sales-mode proposal path. Third-party trackers report pricing in the region of $159 to $259 per user per month, which Aurora itself does not publish, so treat that as indicative and get a written quote. Capability is tiered across plans, so check which tier carries the hourly shading engine and the AI features before comparing.

Outside the US the coverage thins quickly, and it is not written for the Italian professional workflow of a signed relazione tecnica. Include it if your pipeline is genuinely American, and skip it otherwise. The [Aurora Solar review](/blog/aurora-solar-review/) and the [Aurora Solar alternative](/blog/aurora-solar-alternative/) comparison cover it properly.

### 6. OpenSolar, the free option

**Best for:** small residential installers with straightforward roofs and near-zero software budget.

OpenSolar has a free entry tier, a reasonable design experience for simple residential systems, and an active community. For a one-person operation that would otherwise use a spreadsheet, it is a real improvement.

The free model is funded by add-ons, so hardware integrations and some proposal features carry cost, and those stack. In our testing the simulation is not in the same class as a full hourly engine, and C&I shading gets thin fast. In our view it is not a substitute for an engineering suite. See the [OpenSolar review](/blog/opensolar-review/).

## The Case for Staying With Solarius PV

We should say this clearly, because a comparison page that concludes "switch" regardless of the reader is not worth reading.

If your deliverable is a technical report for an Italian design office, and you already work inside a BIM pipeline, Solarius PV's interoperability is a real advantage and there may be no reason to move. Designing the array against a coordinated IFC or Revit model instead of against traced aerial imagery removes an entire class of geometry error, and none of the alternatives in this comparison replicate that path. Add the IEC 60364 cable sizing, the automatic single-line diagrams exportable to PDF, DXF, and DWG, and the technical report output in five languages, and you have a document workflow that most solar platforms are not attempting.

The honest test is a single question: is the thing slowing your business down the drawing, or the deal? If it is the drawing, stay. If it is the deal, the tools built around the customer conversation will help more than another engineering suite will.

> **What most buyers get wrong.** They benchmark Solarius PV against Aurora or OpenSolar on sales features and conclude it is behind. It was never in that race. The fair comparison set is PVsyst, PV*SOL, and the engineering half of a platform like SurgePV, and against those it argues its case on completely different grounds.

## Verdict

Solarius PV is not a product people flee. It is a product people outgrow in one specific direction, and that direction is almost always the customer-facing end of the job. The engineering is sound, the BIM interoperability is genuinely the best in this comparison, and the document output was designed by people who understand what an Italian studio tecnico is actually paid to produce.

The reasons to look elsewhere are equally concrete. The sales proposal is not the product's focus. Windows desktop delivery either suits your team or fights it. And the ARERA change of 29 May 2025 reset what any Italian financial model has to calculate, which makes right now a sensible moment to test whatever you own against the rules that apply today rather than the ones your template was built on.

Three things to do next:

- Ask ACCA for a written quote naming your modules, seats, and term, because no directory price for Solarius PV is reliable enough to budget against.
- Run one real project through your current tool and one alternative, and compare the hourly self-consumption output and the Ritiro Dedicato revenue line specifically.
- Decide whether your constraint is the drawing or the deal, and buy for that constraint rather than for the longer feature list.
