> **Quick answers**
>
> - An on-grid inverter shuts down in an outage by design. That is anti-islanding, a safety requirement, not a fault.
> - A hybrid inverter only backs up the circuits someone deliberately wired to its backup output.
> - The deciding question is your outage load list, not a payback calculation.
> - Hybrid adds battery, enclosure, protection and backup distribution work to the installed scope.
> - Under net metering the grid is usually a better store for surplus than a battery.
> - MNRE assistance is based on eligible installed module capacity, so a battery does not increase it.

**Short version.** Choose on-grid when the utility supply is reliable enough that backup has no practical value at your site. Choose hybrid when specific loads must keep running through outages and you accept the battery as outage protection rather than a savings device. The load list settles it.

## Why an on-grid inverter goes dark in an outage

This is the single most misunderstood point in residential solar, and it surprises people who have just spent a significant sum on a system.

When the utility supply fails, a grid-tied inverter is required to disconnect and stop producing. The function is called anti-islanding. Without it, a rooftop system could continue energising a section of the distribution network that the utility believes is dead, putting line workers at risk during repairs. Grid interface standards used in India require this behaviour, and DISCOM approval depends on it.

So a 5 kW on-grid array on a bright afternoon produces nothing during a cut. The panels are fine. The inverter is fine. It is doing exactly what it was certified to do.

This matters commercially because customers who did not understand it at the point of sale often conclude the system is faulty. If outage cover is part of why someone is buying solar, that has to be designed in from the start, not discovered afterwards.

## What a hybrid inverter actually backs up

A hybrid inverter adds battery management and a separate backup output. During an outage it disconnects from the grid in the same way, then continues supplying that backup output from the battery and, where the model supports it, from the array.

Four limits decide what stays on, and they are independent of each other.

| Limit | Set by | Typical failure to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Continuous backup power | Inverter backup output rating | Assuming full inverter rating is available in backup mode |
| Surge capacity | Inverter surge rating and duration | Motor starting current for pumps, compressors and air conditioners |
| Usable energy | Battery capacity and depth of discharge | Quoting nameplate kWh rather than usable kWh |
| Which circuits | Installer wiring to the backup distribution board | Expecting whole home backup from a partial backup board |

The last row causes the most disputes. "Hybrid" describes the inverter, not the scope of the backup. If the installer wired lights, fans and one socket circuit to the backup board, that is what runs, whatever the battery can deliver.

Motor starting is the second common trap. A submersible pump or an air conditioner compressor draws several times its running current for a short period at startup. A backup design sized on running watts alone will trip when the compressor kicks in.

## Decide from your outage and load needs

Work from measurement, not from a bedroom count or a city average.

1. **List the loads that genuinely must run.** Be honest. Lights, fans, router and refrigerator is a different design from adding two air conditioners.
2. **Record power and expected run hours** from your own equipment labels, not from a generic table.
3. **Identify anything with a motor** and get its starting characteristics, because that sets the surge requirement.
4. **Identify anything interruption sensitive.** A short transfer break is fine for a fridge and not fine for a desktop without its own UPS.
5. **Have the installer size the battery and backup circuits** against that list and show the calculation.
6. **Compare complete installed quotes** on identical PV and installation assumptions, so the only variable is the storage scope.

The [battery sizing guide](/blog/battery-sizing-hybrid-solar/) works through a checked example. No rule of thumb substitutes for a load list.

## What hybrid adds to the installed scope

A meaningful comparison needs matched scope on both sides. Hybrid does not simply mean a more expensive inverter.

On the on-grid side, quotes vary in mounting system, metering arrangement, protective equipment, cable runs and monitoring. On the hybrid side, all of that still applies and then storage adds a compatible battery, a suitable location and enclosure, dedicated DC protection, a backup distribution board and the wiring to split backed up circuits from the rest.

A "battery ready" inverter quote is not the cost of a battery backed installation. It is the cost of the inverter. Ask for the completed backup scope itemised separately so you can see what the outage protection actually costs.

The [5 kW hybrid guide](/blog/5kw-hybrid-inverter-price-india/) sets out the questions that make quotes comparable. Avoid inferring a breakeven period without your own tariff, consumption, generation and battery usage assumptions.

## The battery economics most quotes skip

Under net metering, surplus generation is credited to you through the meter. That is effectively storage with no round trip loss and no equipment to replace.

A battery takes that same surplus, loses a portion to charge and discharge inefficiency, and returns the rest later. On pure energy arbitrage it is difficult for that to beat an export credit, and the battery is a consumable with a finite cycle life.

This is not an argument against batteries. It is an argument for buying one for the right reason. If outages at your site are frequent or costly, backup has real value and the battery is insurance. If the supply is reliable, a battery bought for savings is likely to disappoint. Decide which case you are in before comparing quotes.

## Can you add a battery later?

Treat retrofit as a design decision, not an assumed upgrade path.

A standard on-grid inverter has no battery port and no charge control, so adding storage generally means either replacing it with a hybrid unit or adding a separate AC coupled battery system alongside it. Both are legitimate, and both cost more than specifying correctly at the start.

Where a hybrid inverter is already installed, expansion is still bounded by supported battery voltage, current limits, communication protocol and approved battery models. Get the exact compatibility conditions and firmware requirements in writing before treating later expansion as a benefit you have paid for.

## PM Surya Ghar and grid connection

The [MNRE residential CFA guidelines](https://mnre.gov.in/en/notice/operational-guidelines-for-implementation-of-the-component-central-financial-assistance-to-residential-consumers-of-pm-surya-ghar-muft-bijli-yojana/) permit additional components such as storage, while central financial assistance is calculated from eligible installed solar module capacity. Adding a battery does not increase the assistance.

Neither inverter category guarantees approval on its own. Current vendor empanelment, domestic content conditions, technical requirements and DISCOM processes still apply, and they vary by state and by order. Never treat a quoted slab amount as assured for your specific application.

## Making the final call

If backup has no practical value at your site, an on-grid system is simpler to specify, simpler to maintain and has fewer components that wear out. That simplicity is a genuine advantage, not a compromise.

If outages interrupt work, spoil stock, or affect someone's health or safety at home, a correctly specified hybrid system can be justified even when the battery never pays for itself through bill savings.

The wrong way to decide is a generic payback figure. The right way is a load list, an honest view of your supply reliability, and matched quotes.
