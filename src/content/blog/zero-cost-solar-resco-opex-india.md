## How the RESCO Model Works: The Basics

RESCO stands for Renewable Energy Service Company. The model is structured around a [Power Purchase Agreement (PPA)](/glossary/ppa/), a long-term contract between the RESCO (as seller) and the premises owner or occupant (as buyer) for solar-generated electricity.

The mechanics are straightforward:

- The RESCO conducts a site survey at no charge.
- RESCO designs, funds, and installs the solar system on your roof.
- You provide rooftop access, structural approval, and sign the PPA.
- The RESCO's generation meter records every unit produced.
- You pay the RESCO for those units at the PPA tariff set in the contract.
- The grid continues to supply power when solar generation falls short.
- The RESCO handles all maintenance, monitoring, and insurance.

### Who Owns What Under RESCO?

Table: Who owns and who bears what under a RESCO arrangement

| Item | RESCO Ownership | Premises Owner |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solar panels | Yes | No |
| Inverter and electrical equipment | Yes | No |
| Rooftop access | No | Yes (license granted to RESCO) |
| Electricity savings | No | Yes (pays lower per-unit rate) |
| [PM Surya Ghar](/blog/pm-surya-ghar-yojana-complete-guide/) subsidy | RESCO may claim (commercial) | Not available for premises owner |
| Maintenance responsibility | RESCO | None |
| System performance risk | RESCO | None (fixed tariff) |

This ownership structure is the defining characteristic. The RESCO bears the capex risk, the performance risk, and the maintenance burden. The premises owner bears neither, but also does not own an appreciating asset. India's [MNRE RESCO framework](https://mnre.gov.in/) and the model PPA template published by government agencies provide the basis for commercial RESCO contracts across all states.

## PPA Tariff vs Grid Tariff: The Core Saving

The business case for RESCO pivots on the spread between the PPA tariff you pay and the grid tariff you would otherwise pay.

Table: How the saving per unit is worked out (no published PPA index; use your own bills and written offers)

| Consumer Type | Grid tariff | PPA tariff | Saving per unit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Commercial, industrial, institution or hotel | Your last twelve bills | Written RESCO offer | Grid tariff − PPA tariff |
| Residential | Your last twelve bills | Not typically offered | - |

> **Compare the contracted rate with your own tariff.** A RESCO's PPA rate is only a saving if it is below the grid rate it replaces: saving per unit = grid tariff − PPA rate. Read the rate and its escalation clause in the contract, and compare both with your last twelve bills.

The saving accrues immediately (from the first month the system is commissioned) without any upfront payment.

> [!EXAMPLE] 100 kW system, assumed ₹3/unit saving
> For a 100 kW system generating about 385 units per day (1,40,000 units per year), an assumed ₹3/unit saving translates to ₹4.2 Lakh per year (1,40,000 × ₹3), purely from switching to RESCO. Use the saving in your own offer.

## OPEX Model: The Variant of RESCO

The [OPEX model](/glossary/opex/) differs from the classic RESCO/PPA in one structural way: instead of a per-unit PPA tariff, the premises owner pays a fixed annual O&M fee to the solar service company. In some OPEX arrangements, the premises owner actually finances a portion of the installation (hybrid capex-OPEX).

Table: RESCO / PPA compared with the OPEX (O&M fee) model

| Feature | RESCO / PPA | OPEX (O&M fee model) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Payment structure | Per unit generated (₹/kWh) | Fixed annual fee (₹/year) |
| Risk alignment | RESCO bears generation risk | Premises owner bears some risk |
| Who owns the system | RESCO | Varies (often premises owner) |
| Upfront cost | Zero | Often zero (in pure OPEX) |
| Suited to | Large systems, stable load | Systems where ownership is desired |

For most C&I buyers, the PPA structure (RESCO) is the more common and cleaner model. OPEX is sometimes preferred by government institutions or PSUs that want to retain eventual ownership but cannot front the full capex.

## The RESCO Due Diligence Checklist

### The RESCO Due Diligence Checklist

Before signing a RESCO or PPA agreement, verify each of these eight dimensions:

1. **PPA tariff and escalation clause**: the tariff should be fixed or escalate no more than 2–3% annually. Tariffs that escalate at 5%+ annually may converge with grid tariffs within 8–10 years, eroding savings.
2. **Contract term**: 15–25 years is standard. Confirm the term aligns with your lease (if you rent the premises).
3. **Minimum generation guarantee**: the RESCO should guarantee a minimum annual generation (typically 85–90% of projected yield). If generation falls short, the RESCO credits the difference or offers compensation.
4. **Early termination penalty**: check the penalty for breaking the contract early. Common penalties: 1–3 years of projected PPA payments. Ensure you can absorb this if business circumstances change.
5. **Post-contract asset disposition**: confirm in writing what happens at contract expiry: removal vs. ownership transfer, transfer price, and timeline.
6. **RESCO financial health**: request the RESCO's audited balance sheet. A company with ₹10 crore net worth signing a ₹50 crore 20-year obligation is a credit risk. Check for existing institutional backing or parent guarantee.
7. **Insurance coverage**: the RESCO must hold comprehensive insurance for the system (all-risk, third-party liability). Get a copy of the insurance policy before commissioning.
8. **Performance monitoring access**: ensure you receive real-time or monthly generation data so you can verify the billed units independently.

## Common Mistakes in RESCO Contracts

Signing a RESCO contract without due diligence has cost many businesses dearly:

- **Missing the escalation clause**: a 5% annual PPA escalation on a 20-year contract means the tariff in Year 20 is about 2.5× the Year 1 tariff. By Year 12, the PPA tariff may match or exceed the grid tariff.
- **No generation guarantee**: if the contract does not guarantee minimum generation, and the RESCO's panels underperform due to soiling or shading, you pay a lower bill but the RESCO faces no consequence.
- **Choosing a small RESCO that cannot sustain 20 years**: RESCO companies with thin capitalisation or no institutional backing have closed operations mid-contract, leaving premises owners with abandoned equipment and no maintenance.
- **Not reading the roof damage liability clause**: who is responsible if the mounting structure damages the waterproofing membrane? If it is not in the contract, it is your problem.
- **Assuming RESCO is always cheaper than CAPEX**: for buyers who own the building, have long tenure (10+ years), and can access low-cost MSME or IREDA loans at 7–8%, CAPEX ownership often delivers a better financial outcome. ## What Happens After the RESCO Contract Ends?

At the end of a 15–25 year RESCO contract, three outcomes are possible depending on what was agreed at signing:

**Option 1: System Removal**
The RESCO removes all equipment (panels, inverter, mounting structure), restores the roof, and exits. The premises owner then has a fresh roof and the choice to install their own system, potentially at significantly lower panel costs than today's prices.

**Option 2: Ownership Transfer**
Many modern RESCO contracts include a transfer clause where ownership passes to the premises owner at expiry for ₹1 nominal or a small mutually agreed sum. At this point, the now-depreciated system begins generating fully free electricity for the premises owner for its remaining operational life (typically 5–10 more years on a well-maintained system).

**Option 3: Contract Renewal**
If both parties are satisfied, the contract is renewed at a renegotiated PPA tariff (typically lower, since the RESCO's capex has been fully recovered). This is often the most convenient outcome for both parties.

Negotiate the preferred exit clause explicitly at the time of signing, do not leave it to be resolved 20 years later when the original negotiators may be long gone.
