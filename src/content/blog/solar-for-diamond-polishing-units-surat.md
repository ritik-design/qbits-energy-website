Solar for the diamond industry in Surat pays on one condition: your daytime units inside the 07:00 to 18:00 window must be large enough to absorb the plant. Two Gujarat rules then decide the payback. The first is the set-off window in the GERC rooftop regulations. The second is a banking charge of Rs 1.10 to Rs 1.50 on every solar unit you consume. Neither shows up in the usual EPC payback sheet. The tariff schedule effective 1 April 2026 adds a third: a 60 paise per unit discount on grid units drawn between 11:00 and 17:00, which lowers what a solar unit saves.

This guide is for owners and managers of cutting and polishing units, typically 20 to 200 kW of connected load on an LT or small HT supply from DGVCL, who are about to ask an EPC (the engineering, procurement and construction contractor) for a quote. It reads the regulation and tariff text, adds Surat yield data, and works one payback with every assumption shown. We have no customer, price or installed result of our own to report, so none appears here.

## How do the GERC captive rules treat a diamond polishing unit?

A rooftop plant on a polishing unit you own is a captive project under clause 9.2 of the GERC regulations. Net metering is permitted from 1 kW to 1,000 kW, and gross metering is permitted above 10 kW up to 1,000 kW. The four limits that cap a plant, and how Gujarat's compare with other states, are in [our state-wise net-metering limits guide](/blog/net-metering-capacity-limits-commercial-by-state/). For a unit of the size in this guide the cap is rarely the problem. The settlement rules are.

| Rule | What clause 9.2 says | What it means for a polishing unit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Set-off window, LT demand-based consumer (9.2.7) | Solar generated between 07:00 and 18:00 in a billing cycle can be set off against consumption between 07:00 and 18:00 in the same billing cycle | A day-shift unit nets monthly. A weekly off does not create an export problem, because Sunday's generation offsets weekday consumption in the same cycle |
| Set-off window, HT consumer (9.2.6) | Same window, but within the same day | Idle days export. Surplus is bought at the surplus rates |
| Surplus compensation (9.2.9) | MSME manufacturing enterprises: Rs 2.25 per unit for the first 5 years, then 75% of a GUVNL bid average. Other consumers: 75% of that average | Surplus is paid below the cost of the unit you would otherwise have bought |
| Banking charge (9.2.11) | Rs 1.50 per unit of solar energy consumed for demand-based consumers. Rs 1.10 per unit for MSME manufacturing units and for consumers that are not demand-based | This is charged on solar you use, and it comes straight off the saving |
| Annual proof (9.2.5) | Captive status and consumption are to be proved each financial year to the Chief Electrical Inspector and the DISCOM | Keep ownership and consumption records. Failing the test turns the energy into third-party sale and attracts surcharges |

Two cautions on that table. The regulation as we read it does not define "MSME manufacturing" for this purpose, so ask DGVCL in writing which banking charge your Udyam registration and activity code attract. We also read the regulations as amended to November 2025 and found nothing that changes clause 9.2.11, but we did not search separate GERC orders. If DGVCL gives you a different number, use theirs.

This is the full clause-by-clause reading for Gujarat captive rooftop plants. Our [guide for Surat power loom and textile units](/blog/solar-for-power-looms-textile-units-surat/) keeps only the rows an HT mill needs, and our Morbi ceramic guide does the same for PGVCL supply. Both point back to this table.

The scheme names that come up in Gujarat solar talk, SURYA Gujarat and PM Surya Ghar, appear in the regulation in the residential clauses. Clause 9.2 gives a commercial consumer no subsidy. Our [SURYA Gujarat guide](/blog/surya-gujarat-scheme-guide/) explains what the schemes cover, and a polishing unit is outside them.

If you would rather not own the plant, clause 9.3 allows third-party sale, where a developer installs on your roof under a lease or power sale agreement. The same 07:00 to 18:00 window and the same banking charges apply in that clause. [Our RESCO guide](/blog/solar-resco-model-india-guide/) covers how those contracts are structured.

## What is a solar unit worth under the DGVCL tariff?

Under the DGVCL schedule effective 1 April 2026, a consumer with load up to and including 40 kW is on Rate Non-RGP and a consumer above 40 kW is on Rate LTMD (low-tension maximum demand). Contract demand up to 150 kVA is supplied at low and medium voltage. These are the figures for the two LT categories a polishing unit is likely to sit in.

| Item | Rate Non-RGP (to 40 kW) | Rate LTMD (above 40 kW) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed or demand charge per month | Rs 50 per kW for the first 10 kW, Rs 85 per kW for the next 30 kW | Rs 90 per kW for the first 40 kW of billing demand, Rs 130 for the next 20 kW, Rs 195 above 60 kW |
| Energy charge, post-paid | 435 paise per unit up to 10 kW, 465 paise above 10 kW | 460 paise per unit |
| Peak-hour addition, 07:00 to 11:00 and 18:00 to 22:00 | 45 paise per unit (above 10 kW) | 45 paise per unit |
| Discount, 11:00 to 17:00 | 60 paise per unit with a ToD-recording or smart meter | 60 paise per unit with a ToD-recording or smart meter |
| Billing demand | Not applicable | Highest of 85% of contract demand, actual maximum demand, or 6 kW |

Source: GERC tariff schedule for DGVCL, MGVCL, PGVCL and UGVCL, effective 1 April 2026, categories 4 and 5. The schedule leaves out FPPAS (the fuel and power purchase price adjustment), electricity duty and other taxes, which your bill adds on top.

Three things follow for a polishing unit that installs solar.

**A solar unit is worth less than the headline rate.** An LTMD unit drawn between 11:00 and 17:00 costs 460 minus 60, which is 400 paise. A unit drawn from 07:00 to 11:00 costs 460 plus 45, which is 505 paise. Solar generation sits mostly in the discounted window, so most of what you save is nearer Rs 4.00 than Rs 4.60. We found no equivalent discount clause in the 1 June 2024 schedule text we read, so a payback sum built on the older figures overstates the saving.

**Solar does not reduce the demand charge in any dependable way.** Billing demand is at least 85% of contract demand. A unit with 60 kW contract demand pays for at least 51 kW every month, which is 40 times Rs 90 plus 11 times Rs 130, or Rs 5,030 a month, whatever the solar plant does. A cloud passing over a polishing floor at the wrong quarter-hour can still set the month's maximum demand.

**The saving that remains has to be read from your own bill.** The schedule says FPPAS applies "in accordance with the Formula approved by the Gujarat Electricity Regulatory Commission from time to time". Electricity duty is also charged. Both are per-unit or percentage lines on the bill that a solar unit avoids if the bill levies them on grid units. We did not verify the current FPPAS rate, so the payback below treats these charges as a variable instead of a number.

## How much power does a rooftop in Surat produce?

PVGIS, the European Commission's Joint Research Centre tool, models a 1 kWp crystalline silicon system at Surat with 14% system losses and returns 1,550 kWh per year at a 15 degree south-facing tilt and 1,524 kWh at 10 degrees. Those are modelled values from reanalysis data, not a measurement at your roof. PVGIS's horizon is calculated from terrain, so it does not see the buildings next to you.

| Period | Modelled output per kWp per month (15 degree tilt) |
| --- | --- |
| March to May | About 156 kWh a month (159, 155, 154) |
| June to September | About 98 kWh a month (114, 84, 91, 104) |
| October to February | About 138 kWh a month |
| Lowest month, July | 84 kWh, about 53% of March |

The shape matters more than the annual figure. A plant sized and sold on a yearly average produces nearly half as much in July as in March, and your set-off is calculated every billing cycle. The worked example below uses 1,450 kWh per kWp per year, about 6% under the PVGIS figure, to allow for dust, shading and downtime. That haircut is our assumption, not a measured soiling loss.

## What is the payback of a 40 kWp plant on a polishing unit?

The case below is **illustrative**. It is no quote, no Qbits figure and no result from a real site, so replace each input with your own.

| Input | Illustrative value | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Supply | LTMD, 60 kW contract demand, LT demand-based | Typical small unit |
| Daytime consumption | 7,000 kWh a month, all inside 07:00 to 18:00, single day shift | Above the plant's output in every month, so nothing is exported |
| Plant size | 40 kWp | Chosen so March output (about 6,000 kWh) stays below the 7,000 kWh daytime load |
| Annual yield | 1,450 kWh per kWp, 58,000 kWh a year | PVGIS 1,550 less about 6% |
| Avoided energy rate | Rs 4.26 per unit | 75% of units at 400 paise (11:00 to 17:00) and 25% at 505 paise (07:00 to 11:00) |
| Banking charge | Rs 1.10 per unit, the MSME rate | Rs 1.50 is shown as a second case |
| Other per-unit charges avoided | Rs 0, 1.50 and 3.00 per unit | Stand-ins for FPPAS and similar lines. Read yours from the bill |
| Installed cost | Rs 35,000, 45,000 and 55,000 per kWp | Round numbers chosen to span a range, not market quotes |
| Running cost | 1% of installed cost a year | Cleaning and checks |
| Excluded | Demand-charge savings, tax depreciation, finance cost, degradation, inverter replacement | Not verified or case-specific |

Payback is installed cost divided by (units generated times net saving per unit, less running cost).

| Installed cost per kWp | Other charges avoided: Rs 0 per unit | Rs 1.50 per unit | Rs 3.00 per unit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rs 35,000 (Rs 14 lakh for 40 kWp) | 8.3 years | 5.5 years | 4.1 years |
| Rs 45,000 (Rs 18 lakh) | 10.9 years | 7.1 years | 5.3 years |
| Rs 55,000 (Rs 22 lakh) | 13.6 years | 8.9 years | 6.6 years |

At the banking charge of Rs 1.50, the Rs 45,000 row moves to 12.7, 7.9 and 5.7 years.

Read it like this. On the tariff schedule alone the payback is long, because Rs 4.26 less Rs 1.10 leaves Rs 3.16 a unit. The answer swings with the per-unit charges your bill adds to the energy rate. So ask any EPC to rebuild the sum from your last twelve bills, line by line. The [C&I payback and IRR guide](/blog/solar-irr-payback-ci-india/) walks through that method with other tariffs.

## Does solar suit the diamond industry's shift pattern?

For an LT demand-based polishing unit, only consumption between 07:00 and 18:00 can absorb solar. That gives a simple sizing test.

- **Day-only unit.** If the floor runs one shift inside the window, all of your consumption is eligible. Size to the daytime consumption of your weakest month, not to the roof.
- **Two shifts or 24 by 7.** Night shift consumption cannot absorb solar. Size to the units drawn between 07:00 and 18:00 only. A unit that draws half its energy at night should expect a plant half the size a total-consumption sum suggests.
- **Air-conditioned floors and chillers.** Cooling that runs through the sunny hours is the most solar-friendly load you have. Cooling that runs at night is not. Log it by hour before you decide.
- **Short shifts.** Al Jazeera reported on 13 August 2025 that Surat unit owners feared closures after the US raised tariffs on Indian goods. We have no current order-book data and do not model one. What matters for a solar decision is narrow: the plant pays only on the daytime units you actually consume, so size to today's load, not to the load the unit was built for.

Use the ToD register on your bill, where your meter records it, to read in-window units. If the meter does not record ToD, ask DGVCL, because the 60 paise discount is also tied to that meter.

## Roof load, shadow and cleaning on a Surat polishing unit

**Roof load.** Most units in clusters such as Varachha and Katargam are RCC buildings. Ask your EPC for the dead load of the proposed structure in kg per square metre and have a qualified structural engineer confirm the slab can carry it, including wind uplift and the fixings. Check the waterproofing and the parapet before anything is drilled.

**Shadow.** Your neighbours' buildings are the main risk. At Surat's latitude the sun at solar noon on 21 December is about 45 degrees above the horizon, which we calculated from latitude and the sun's declination. A neighbouring wall 12 m taller than your panels therefore casts a shadow of roughly 12 m at noon, and a longer one in the morning and afternoon. Water tanks, stair headrooms and lift rooms on your own roof cast shadows too. Ask for a shadow study for December, not June. Read [how shading cuts generation](/blog/solar-panel-shadow-effect-generation/) before you accept a layout. Inverters with several independent MPPT (maximum power point tracking) inputs let you keep shaded and unshaded strings apart, and the [QB 40/45/50KTLC-Plus](/our-products/QB-40-50KTLC-Plus/) is listed with 4 MPPTs and an IP66 enclosure.

**Monsoon and dust.** The PVGIS table shows July output near half of March. Rain washes panels, so the dry months carry the soiling risk. We have no measured soiling figure for Varachha or Katargam, so log output against a cleaning schedule through your first dry season and set the interval from your own data. Plan safe access and a water point for cleaning at the design stage.

## What are the DISCOM approval steps for a rooftop plant in Gujarat?

The Fourth Amendment (13 of 2024) to the GERC regulations sets the timeline below. The days are targets for each authority, and no one promises them for your particular file.

| Step | Authority | Time limit in the regulation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Registration and certificate | GEDA or the designated agency | 5 days from a complete application |
| Approval of single line, earthing and wiring diagrams | Chief Electrical Inspector | 10 days |
| Application with capacity and documents | DISCOM | You submit |
| Technical feasibility report | DISCOM, then the field office | 3 days to request, 10 days for the field report |
| In-principle consent or estimate | DISCOM head office | 2 days after the report |
| Connectivity agreement | You and the DISCOM | Within 15 days of consent, or 30 days if system strengthening is needed |
| Project completion | You | 6 months from the letter |
| Meter installation | DISCOM | 5 days after documents |
| Commissioning certificate | GEDA | 3 days after intimation |

At completion, the DISCOM asks for ownership proof, the Chief Electrical Inspector's installation approval, meter and CTPT test certificates, and IEC test reports for modules, inverters, cables and other equipment. The inverter must have anti-islanding (it shuts down if the grid goes dead), and plants above 10 kW need a second line of protection such as a no-volt relay. System strengthening charges apply above 6 kW. For LT consumers from 6 kW up to 100 kW, state-owned DISCOMs recover a per-kW charge as approved for new LT load. We did not look up the current figure. Our [net-meter approval timeline](/blog/net-meter-approval-timeline/) shows how long the process takes in practice.

GST on the equipment depends on classification and supply type. Ask the EPC to state the entry it relies on, and see our [GST on solar inverters guide](/blog/gst-on-solar-inverters-2026/). We did not open the GST notification for this post.

## What should you bring to an EPC for a quote?

1. Twelve bills, with the demand, energy, ToD, FPPAS and duty lines visible.
2. Your hourly daytime profile for a normal week, from the ToD register or a logger.
3. Contract demand, supply voltage and whether DGVCL treats you as an MSME manufacturing unit for banking.
4. A roof drawing with neighbouring building heights and your own rooftop obstacles.
5. A request that the proposal state the banking charge, the avoided energy rate by time band, and what it assumes for FPPAS.

For a day-shift polishing unit with a steady daytime load, solar can make sense in Gujarat.
