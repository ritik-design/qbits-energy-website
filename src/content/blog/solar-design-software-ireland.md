Irish solar has an unusual quirk that most design software ignores: the export rate depends on which supplier the customer happens to be with, and the standard range runs from 15.2 to 32.0 cents per kWh. That is roughly a two-to-one spread on the same exported kilowatt-hour. On an export-heavy system, moving a customer to a better Clean Export Guarantee tariff can be worth more than adding panels. Layer on an SEAI grant that tapers and stops at 4 kWp, an NC6 notification that must be filed 20 working days before installation, and planning exemptions that vanish inside Architectural Conservation Areas, and Ireland rewards a design tool that models the specifics. This guide compares the platforms Irish installers should shortlist.

## The Grant Taper Shapes The System Size

The SEAI residential grant is not a flat rate and the structure has a direct effect on the sizing conversation.

| System size | Grant calculation | Total grant |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 kWp | 700 per kWp | 700 euros |
| 2 kWp | 700 per kWp | 1,400 euros |
| 3 kWp | 700 for first 2, 200 for next 1 | 1,600 euros |
| 4 kWp and above | 700 for first 2, 200 for next 2 | 1,800 euros maximum |

The marginal grant drops from 700 to 200 euros per kWp after the second kilowatt, and to zero after the fourth. In effective-cost terms the first 2 kWp are heavily subsidised, the next 2 lightly, and everything beyond that is fully customer-funded.

That does not mean systems should stop at 4 kWp. It means the proposal should show the effective cost per kWp changing across the system, so the customer understands why the fifth kilowatt costs more than the first. A quote showing one blended price per kWp hides the structure and makes the larger system look worse than it is once export income is included.

## The Export Rate Is A Variable, Not A Constant

> **What most Irish quotes get wrong.** They apply a single national CEG figure. With standard rates spanning 15.2 to 32.0 cents per kWh, a proposal built on a mid-range assumption is materially wrong for customers at either end, and it misses a recommendation worth real money.

On a household exporting 2,500 kWh a year, the difference between a 15.2 cent tariff and a 25.0 cent one is roughly 245 euros annually. Over a typical system life that comfortably exceeds the cost of several additional panels. Advising the customer to review their CEG supplier is therefore a legitimate part of the proposal, and it costs the installer nothing.

Two further details belong in the numbers. The first 400 euros of annual export income is tax-exempt through the end of 2028, so gross export revenue is not net benefit above that line. And because avoided import is worth more than any CEG rate, self-consumption still dominates, which means the hourly split between consumed and exported energy matters as much here as anywhere. ## Connection, Planning And Standards

**NC6 versus NC7.** NC6 is notification for single-phase under 6 kW (25A) or three-phase under 11 kW (16A per phase), and the form must reach ESB Networks at least 20 working days before installation starts. NC7 is a mini-generation application for single-phase 6 to 50 kW or three-phase 11 to 50 kW. The threshold changes both the process and the lead time, so a design that drifts across it changes the project timeline. SurgePV's [Ireland compliance library](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/ireland) covers the process.

**Planning.** S.I. 493/2022 exempts most residential work: rooftop projection up to 15 cm on pitched roofs or 50 cm on flat, and ground-mounted arrays up to 25 square metres, 2 metres high, 2 metres from boundaries. Protected Structures and Architectural Conservation Areas are the exception and need full planning permission, which is a substantial timeline addition that should surface at survey rather than after.

**Standards and contractors.** EN 50549 governs grid connection protection. Part L of the 2022 building regulations applies. Contractors must be RECI-registered. The Commission for Regulation of Utilities regulates export. SEAI grant eligibility has its own registration requirements, covered in SurgePV's [SEAI grants guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/ireland/guides/seai-grants).

**Irish irradiance.** Low winter sun angles and high cloud cover mean generation is concentrated into a short summer season with significant diffuse-light contribution. A yield model tuned for southern Europe will misstate both the annual figure and its seasonal distribution, which matters for a self-consumption calculation.

## Worked Example: 5 kWp In Cork, Two Different CEG Suppliers

An illustrative calculation, not a specific customer project. Inputs: 5 kWp, 900 kWh per kWp specific yield, 4,500 kWh generation, 4,200 kWh consumption, import at 33 cents per kWh, self-consumption 38%, SEAI grant 1,800 euros.

| Line | CEG at 15.2 cents | CEG at 25.0 cents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Generation | 4,500 kWh | 4,500 kWh |
| Self-consumed | 1,710 kWh | 1,710 kWh |
| Exported | 2,790 kWh | 2,790 kWh |
| Avoided import at 33c | EUR 564 | EUR 564 |
| Gross export income | EUR 424 | EUR 698 |
| Taxable export above EUR 400 exemption | EUR 24 | EUR 298 |
| Year 1 benefit before tax | EUR 988 | EUR 1,262 |

The supplier choice is worth 274 euros a year on an identical system, which is roughly the value of adding a kilowatt of panels without the capital cost. It also pushes the customer past the 400 euro tax exemption, which the proposal should note rather than ignore. This is the single easiest recommendation an Irish installer can make, and it only appears if the tool models the actual tariff rather than a national average.
