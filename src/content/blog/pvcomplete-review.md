PVComplete is one of the few solar design products that started from the engineer's side of the business rather than the salesperson's. Its core product, PVCAD, is a plugin that runs inside Autodesk AutoCAD, which means the file you finish with is a real CAD drawing rather than an export that a drafter has to rebuild. That is an unusual position in a market where almost every competitor has spent the last decade building browser-based sales tools. This review looks at what PVComplete is in 2026, what changed when Enact Solar acquired it in January, what the product genuinely does better than anything else, where it stops fitting the buyer, and which alternatives deserve a look first.

**Short version.** PVComplete is a CAD-native solar design suite built around PVCAD, an AutoCAD plugin that produces permit-ready drawing sets, paired with PVSketch for browser-based early layouts. It suits engineering-led commercial and utility teams whose deliverable is a stamped drawing. It is a poor fit for residential sales organisations. Enact Solar acquired the company in January 2026, so confirm roadmap and support before signing a long term.

Most reviews of design software compare the same axis: how quickly can a salesperson produce something a homeowner will sign. PVComplete is not competing on that axis, and judging it there produces a misleading verdict. The right question is narrower. Does a CAD-native workflow save an engineering team enough drafting time to justify carrying AutoCAD and the training that goes with it?

## What Happened to PVComplete in January 2026

Enact Solar announced the acquisition of PVComplete on 13 January 2026, as reported by Solar Power World and Solar Builder. Financial terms were not disclosed. PVComplete's chief executive and chief technology officer joined Enact in the same roles, which is usually a signal that the acquirer wants the team and the product rather than just the customer list.

The stated rationale is a combined platform spanning design, proposal generation, asset management, and fleet monitoring. In plain terms, Enact already had the operations and monitoring side and bought the engineering side.

This matters to a buyer in two ways. First, the products are not discontinued. PVCAD and PVSketch are still being sold, so this is a review of a live product, not an obituary. Second, an acquired product's roadmap is set by the acquirer's strategy, not the original one. Features that serve the combined platform get investment. Features that only served the standalone PVComplete customer may not. If you are evaluating PVComplete now, that is a question to put to a salesperson in writing, and we cover the broader alternative landscape in our [Enact Solar alternative guide](/blog/enact-solar-alternative/).

## What PVComplete Actually Is

Four products, split across two workflows and two scales.

**PVSketch** is a browser tool. You draw a rough array on satellite imagery, get a layout and an energy estimate, and produce something customer-facing early in a deal. It is deliberately light and there has historically been a free tier for very low project volumes, which is how a lot of people first encounter the company.

**PVCAD** is the reason PVComplete exists. It is an AutoCAD plugin, and inside AutoCAD it generates array layouts, single-line diagrams, bills of material, topography handling, and energy modelling, aimed at commercial and industrial rooftops and ground mounts.

**PVSketch Mega** and **PVCAD Mega** extend the same pattern to utility-scale work, with tracker and fixed-tilt handling and terrain analysis.

The intended flow is that a developer or seller starts a project in PVSketch and hands it to an engineer who finishes it in PVCAD. That handoff is the product thesis. Most competitors either serve the seller and stop before engineering, or serve the engineer and leave the seller with nothing to show a customer.

## What PVComplete Genuinely Does Well

**CAD-native output is the whole argument, and it holds.** Almost every other design platform produces a proposal-grade visual and, at best, an export. Somebody then rebuilds that in CAD to make a drawing set an authority will accept. PVCAD skips the rebuild, because the design was born in AutoCAD. For a team whose deliverable is a permit package, that removes a step rather than speeding one up, and removing a step is worth more than making one faster.

**Drawing quality that survives a plan check.** Single-line diagrams, dimensioned layouts, and bills of material generated inside AutoCAD carry the layer structure, blocks, and title-block conventions a drafting team already uses. That means an engineer can override anything by hand. Tools that generate a locked PDF give you a good drawing you cannot edit. Tools that generate a CAD file give you a starting point you own. Engineering teams strongly prefer the second, and often only realise why after the first plan-check comment comes back.

**It fits engineering-led C&I workflows without argument.** Commercial rooftops are messy. Setbacks, existing mechanical equipment, structural zones, and access paths are exactly the things a CAD environment was built to represent. Browser layout tools handle them with approximations. AutoCAD handles them with geometry.

**It bridges the sales sketch and the engineering drawing.** The PVSketch to PVCAD handoff addresses a genuine organisational problem. In most solar companies the sales layout and the engineering layout are two unrelated artefacts, and the gap between them is where sold system size quietly stops matching buildable system size. A shared path between the two is a real structural fix, not a feature.

**Utility-scale is a first-class case.** The Mega products treat terrain and tracker layout as core rather than an afterthought.

## Where PVComplete Falls Short

**It assumes CAD competence.** This is the honest headline limitation. PVCAD is a plugin, and a plugin inherits the host application's learning curve. A salesperson cannot pick it up in an afternoon, and a solar designer who has never worked in AutoCAD will not be productive quickly. That is a hiring and training constraint, not a software bug, but it decides whether the purchase works.

**It is heavier than simple residential work needs.** A clean 6 kW gable roof does not require an AutoCAD environment. For a residential installer running volume, the drawing set is often a standardised template and the bottleneck is closing the customer, not drafting the plan. Buying an engineering-first suite to solve a sales problem is the most common way teams waste money on this category.

**The customer-facing side is lighter than a dedicated sales platform.** PVSketch exists and does the early-stage job, but the suite is positioned around the engineering half, and PVSketch reads as the front end to it rather than the flagship. Compared against platforms whose entire reason for existing is the customer presentation and the close, the proposal workflow is a supporting act in our assessment. That is a reasonable trade for an engineering company to make. It is a problem if you expected one tool to do both jobs at the same standard.

**Desktop and cloud do not mix cleanly.** PVCAD's engineering work happens on a desktop AutoCAD installation. Collaboration, version control, and remote review then follow whatever your CAD file management already is, rather than being handled by the design tool. Teams used to a browser platform where everyone opens the same live project will find this a step backwards.

**Post-acquisition uncertainty.** Not a product flaw, but a buying risk. Ask what happens to each of the four products over the next 24 months and get the answer in the contract if it matters to you.

> **What most buyers get wrong.** They compare PVComplete's design speed against a browser tool and conclude it is slow. The comparison is wrong. Measure the whole path from site data to an approved permit set, including the CAD redraw the browser tool forces on you. If your team does that redraw, PVComplete may already be faster. If your team never touches CAD, it is not.

## Who Should Buy PVComplete

Buy it if most of these describe you.

- Your deliverable is a stamped, permit-ready drawing set, not a sales proposal.
- Your team already runs AutoCAD, so the Autodesk licence is a sunk cost and the skills exist.
- Your work is commercial, industrial, or utility-scale, where layout complexity is genuine rather than incidental.
- You currently pay a drafter to redraw designs that came out of another tool, and you can quantify that time.
- You want the sales sketch and the engineering drawing to share a lineage instead of diverging.

At that profile the arithmetic usually works. One removed redraw step per project, across a few hundred projects a year, is a bigger number than the licence.

## Who Should Not Buy PVComplete

- **Residential sales organisations.** The value is in the CAD half, and you will not use it. A sales-first platform will convert better and cost less.
- **Teams with no CAD skills and no plan to hire them.** A plugin cannot rescue you from the host application.
- **Fully remote or distributed design teams** who need many people in the same live project. Desktop CAD plus file management is a different operating model.
- **Anyone who needs a lender-accepted bankability report as the standard output.** PVCAD models energy, but when financing documents name PVsyst, the named tool wins. See our [PVsyst review](/blog/pvsyst-review/) for where that line sits.
- **Buyers who need pricing certainty today.** If you cannot get a firm written quote and a roadmap commitment post-acquisition, that alone is a reason to wait or to shortlist elsewhere.

## Verdict

PVComplete is a good product with an unusually clear point of view, and the point of view is correct for the customer it was built for. Solar design tools mostly optimise the sale. PVComplete optimises the drawing. If the drawing is what your business ships, that is not a niche preference, it is the whole job, and PVCAD removes an entire redraw step that browser-based competitors quietly leave in your process.

The limitations are the direct cost of that choice rather than defects. You need CAD competence. You need an Autodesk licence, bought or already owned. The customer-facing workflow is a supporting product, not the flagship. And a four-product suite priced per seat adds up faster than a single-licence tool. None of that is fixable by negotiation, because it is what the product is.

The Enact acquisition does not change the verdict on the software, but it changes the diligence. Get roadmap commitments in writing before a multi-year term.

For most teams reading a review like this, our recommendation is SurgePV, because the common requirement is design plus proposals from one record with DXF and DWG available downstream, at $1,299 to $1,899 per user per year and without an AutoCAD dependency. If your deliverable really is a stamped drawing set and your engineers already live in AutoCAD, PVComplete is the better tool and you should buy it on that basis.

Three things to do next:

- Time your current path from site data to approved permit set, including any CAD redraw, so you know what a CAD-native tool would actually save.
- Get a written all-in quote covering every PVComplete product your seat mix needs, plus AutoCAD if you do not already own it.
- Rebuild two recent projects in a shortlisted alternative and compare the finished drawing and proposal, not the feature list.
