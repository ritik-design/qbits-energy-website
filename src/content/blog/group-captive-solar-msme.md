## What Group Captive Solar Actually Is

A group captive plant is a solar plant owned by a company formed as a special purpose vehicle (SPV). It is usually ground mounted and 2 MW to 20 MW in size. The captive users are the shareholders of that SPV. A developer typically holds part of the equity, builds and operates the plant, and sells the balance equity to the consuming businesses.

The legal basis is Section 2(8) of the Electricity Act 2003 read with Rule 3 of the Electricity Rules 2005. A group captive SPV is treated as an association of persons. Two conditions must hold every financial year:

1. Captive users hold not less than 26% of the ownership of the plant in aggregate, with voting rights.
2. Captive users consume not less than 51% of the annual generation, in proportion to their ownership shares, with a permitted variation of up to 10%.

The Supreme Court confirmed this in Dakshin Gujarat Vij Company Limited v Gayatri Shakti Paper and Board Limited (2023). An SPV counts as an association of persons, and the proportionality requirement applies to it. The Court also laid down a unitary qualifying ratio. Each captive user should consume roughly 1.96% of generation for every 1% of ownership held, within the 10% band. This comes from the [judgment analysis on Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=2658f828-bf96-4bf2-ba7e-0a5a0482791a), 2026.

In practice, a typical structure looks like this. A developer builds a 5 MW plant. Four to ten MSMEs collectively subscribe 26% or more of the SPV equity, each in proportion to its contracted offtake. Each member signs a power purchase agreement with the SPV for its share of generation. The power is wheeled through the state grid to each member's factory under open access permission.

## Why the 26% and 51% Numbers Matter So Much

These two thresholds are not formalities. They are the entire economics of the model. Captive status is what makes the surcharge exemptions possible.

Section 42(2) of the Electricity Act 2003 requires open access consumers to pay a cross subsidy surcharge (CSS). It compensates the DISCOM for losing high-tariff industrial customers. But the proviso to that section is key. No surcharge is leviable where open access carries power from a captive generating plant to the owner's own use. The same logic exempts captive power from the additional surcharge most states levy.

CSS alone runs ₹1.4 to ₹2.5 per unit in major industrial states in 2026, according to [Bridgeway Power's CSS rate analysis](https://bridgewaypower.in/blog/cross-subsidy-surcharge-css-2026), 2026. Additional surcharge adds ₹0.2 to ₹1.1 per unit. So a third-party open access buyer pays ₹1.6 to ₹3.6 per unit in charges that a captive buyer does not. That gap is the return on your 26% equity.

The catch: both tests are verified annually, usually by the state commission or the DISCOM. Fail either test in a financial year and the plant is not captive for that year. The power you drew is reclassified as third-party open access. CSS and additional surcharge become payable, sometimes with interest. Every serious group captive agreement therefore includes monthly consumption tracking against the ownership ratio and a default allocation mechanism.

The regulatory ground shifted recently. The Ministry of Power notified the Electricity (Amendment) Rules 2025 in September 2025. These formally recognised energy storage and clarified consumers' rights to own or lease storage, according to [CMS IndusLaw](https://cms-induslaw.com/en/ind/publication/energy-infrastructure-natural-resources-law-corner-bulletin-september-and-october-2025), 2025. The captive rule amendments that followed in 2026 kept the 26% and 51% thresholds. They simplified the proportionality compliance that had caused most disputes, according to [Saur Energy](https://www.saurenergy.com/solar-energy-news/govt-amends-captive-power-rules-to-reduce-group-captive-disputes-11216539), 2026. In June 2026 the Karnataka High Court quashed the state regulator's captive verification procedure. It was inconsistent with Rule 3 and the Dakshin Gujarat ruling, according to [Energetica India](https://energetica-india.net/news/karnataka-high-court-quashes-kercs-captive-verification-procedure), 2026. The direction of travel is clearer rules, not looser ones.

## Why Group Captive Suits MSMEs Specifically

Group captive solves four problems that stop most MSMEs from going solar at meaningful scale.

**Problem 1: no usable roof or land.** A 500 kW plant needs about 2 acres of land or roughly 50,000 square feet of shadow-free roof. Most MSMEs have neither. In group captive, the plant sits on land near a substation somewhere with good irradiance. Nothing is built on your premises.

**Problem 2: individual load is too small.** A standalone captive plant under about 2 MW has poor economics. Open access permissions, evacuation infrastructure, and O&M all carry fixed costs. Pooling eight MSMEs into one 5 MW plant spreads those costs. Open access solar tariffs for industrial buyers run ₹4 to ₹5 per unit. Grid tariffs run ₹8.5 to ₹10, according to [Heaven Designs' open access guide](https://heavendesigns.in/blog/open-access-in-solar-parks-powering-indias-transition-to-sustainable-industrial-energy/), 2026. The captive surcharge exemption improves the number further.

**Problem 3: no project management capacity.** A captive plant needs land acquisition, connectivity approval, EPC contracting, and 25 years of O&M. In group captive the developer does all of this. The MSME's job is to buy equity, sign a PPA, and consume power.

**Problem 4: capital discipline.** An MSME with a 300 kW load might invest ₹40 lakh to ₹70 lakh in group captive equity for its proportional share. That is one quarter to one third the cost of owning a 300 kW rooftop plant outright. It also avoids the roof problem entirely. There is also a financing angle. Banks and NBFCs now lend against group captive equity subscriptions. They treat the contracted PPA savings as repayment capacity. ## Group Captive vs Third-Party PPA: The Charge Stack

The cleanest comparison is the charge stack on one unit of electricity under both models. The numbers below are indicative for a Gujarat or Maharashtra HT consumer in 2026. Your state's rates will differ.

| Charge component | Third-party open access PPA | Group captive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PPA tariff (₹/unit) | 3.80 | 3.80 |
| Wheeling and transmission (₹/unit) | 0.90 | 0.90 |
| Cross subsidy surcharge (₹/unit) | 1.40 to 2.50 | 0 |
| Additional surcharge (₹/unit) | 0.20 to 1.10 | 0 |
| Losses, scheduling, banking (₹/unit) | 0.35 | 0.35 |
| **Landed cost (₹/unit)** | **6.65 to 8.65** | **5.05** |
| Savings vs ₹8.50 grid tariff | negligible to ₹1.85 | ₹3.45 |

The structure of this worked example follows the comparison published by [Heaven Green Energy](https://www.heavengreenenergy.com/glossary/cross-subsidy-surcharge), 2026. There the group captive option beat the third-party PPA purely on the surcharge exemption.

Two honest caveats. First, some states waive CSS on renewable open access for a period or for certain categories. Check your state tariff order before assuming the full gap. Tamil Nadu and Rajasthan have used such waivers at times. Second, third-party PPA needs zero capital and zero compliance burden. If you have no cash to invest, or your consumption is volatile, the third-party route with a CSS waiver can still win. This is a genuine trade-off, not a formality.

For diesel-dependent MSMEs, also compare against your backup economics. Group captive power at ₹5 per unit undercuts diesel generation at ₹18 to ₹22 per unit by a wide margin. Our [solar vs diesel generator](/blog/solar-vs-diesel-generator-india/) analysis shows the numbers.

One more nuance on timing. Solar generates between roughly 9 AM and 5 PM, so group captive directly replaces your daytime grid draw. Under time-of-day tariffs, the units solar displaces are often the most expensive peak and morning-slot units. That pushes the effective saving above the blended tariff in the table. Ask your developer to model savings against your actual 15-minute load curve, not your annual average. The gap between an average-based model and an interval-data model is often 10% to 15% of claimed savings, in either direction.

## How to Evaluate a Group Captive Offer

When a developer pitches you a slot in a group captive project, run this checklist before signing anything.

1. **Verify the SPV and your shareholding.** You must hold actual equity with voting rights in the company that owns the plant, proportional to your offtake. Ask for the share subscription agreement, not a marketing brochure.
2. **Get the landed cost in writing.** That means PPA tariff plus wheeling, banking, losses, scheduling fees, and any state electricity duty, for your specific HT connection. If the quote only shows the PPA tariff, it is incomplete.
3. **Check the captive compliance mechanism.** How is monthly consumption tracked against the 51% and proportionality tests? What happens if another member under-consumes? Look for default allocation and true-up clauses.
4. **Read the equity exit terms.** Most agreements lock you in for 5 to 15 years. Understand the buy-back formula, who can buy your shares, and what happens if you shut or move your factory.
5. **Scrutinise the generation guarantee.** A capacity utilisation factor of 17% to 21% is realistic for Indian ground-mount solar. If the savings model assumes 22% or more, discount it.
6. **Confirm open access permissions and timelines.** The plant needs connectivity and your unit needs open access approval from the state nodal agency. Ask for the application status, not promises.
7. **Check the developer's operating track record.** Ask for two running group captive plants with reference consumers you can call. Our [Tirupur textile case study](/blog/case-study-tirupur-textile/) shows what documented, verifiable performance looks like in practice.

## Risks and Trade-offs: What Most MSMEs Get Wrong

> **What most MSMEs get wrong:** they evaluate a group captive offer on the PPA tariff alone. The tariff is the smallest part of the decision. The landed charge stack, the annual captive compliance risk, and the equity exit terms decide whether the deal actually pays.

**Compliance risk is real.** The 51% test is annual and collective. If your factory has a bad year and consumption drops, or another member drops out, captive status can fail for everyone. The 2026 rule amendments reduced disputes over proportionality, but the thresholds themselves remain. [Saur Energy](https://www.saurenergy.com/solar-energy-news/govt-amends-captive-power-rules-to-reduce-group-captive-disputes-11216539) reported this in 2026. State regulators also actively audit captive claims. The Karnataka verification fight that ended in the High Court in 2026 started as exactly such an audit.

**Your capital is illiquid.** Equity in an SPV is not a fixed deposit. Exit depends on the shareholders' agreement and finding a buyer for your slice. If you might relocate, downsize, or sell the business within five years, group captive is the wrong instrument. A rooftop plant or a third-party PPA fits better.

**You give up direct tax benefits.** The SPV claims the 40% accelerated depreciation, not you. The benefit reaches you indirectly through the tariff. Businesses wanting depreciation on their own books should compare against an owned plant. Our [solar inverter depreciation guide](/blog/solar-inverter-depreciation-in-india/) explains the mechanics.

**Generation risk sits with you.** As a shareholder, monsoon-year underperformance is partly your problem, not just the developer's. Check whether the agreement guarantees minimum generation or merely estimates it.

**Regulatory risk cuts both ways.** States have periodically proposed removing or capping the captive CSS exemption. Nothing on the horizon removes it in 2026. But a 25-year PPA will outlive several tariff orders. Price that uncertainty honestly. Even if the exemption halves in year 10, the deal usually still beats grid power.

The exception worth noting: some states currently waive CSS on renewable third-party PPAs. There the group captive premium shrinks to the additional surcharge and the tariff discount. In that narrow case, zero-investment third-party PPA can be the better deal until the waiver expires.

## Is Group Captive Right for Your MSME? A Quick Fit Test

Answer five questions honestly.

1. Is your contract demand 100 kW or more? Below that, most states will not grant open access at all.
2. Do you spend more than ₹3 lakh a month on grid power? Below that, the legal and administrative overhead eats the saving.
3. Can you commit ₹30 lakh to ₹80 lakh of equity for 5 or more years? If not, look at RESCO or third-party PPA instead.
4. Is your consumption stable and mostly in daylight hours? Volatile or night-heavy loads weaken both compliance and savings.
5. Do you lack roof or land for your own plant? If you have a good owned roof, an owned rooftop plant usually beats group captive on total lifetime benefit.

Four or five yes answers means group captive deserves a serious evaluation. Two or fewer means another structure fits better. The [open access solar guide](/blog/open-access-solar-india-explained/) maps the full set of alternatives.
