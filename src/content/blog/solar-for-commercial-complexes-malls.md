Solar for a commercial building starts with one decision: which electricity meter the plant will sit behind. That meter, and not the phrase "common area", sets how big the plant can be and who benefits. If the mall has one DISCOM (distribution company) connection and the tenants are sub-metered behind it, the whole building's load is available to the plant. If every tenant has its own DISCOM meter, only the common-area meter is.

In the worked case below, a 5 lakh sq ft mall in Surat could put about 500 kWp on its roof. Behind one meter the plant uses 99% of its output. On a common-area meter alone the same plant would use 62%, and a plant of 100 to 200 kWp fits that meter better.

This guide is for facility managers, owner's representatives and asset managers of malls, multiplexes, office towers and mixed commercial complexes. Read it before you invite an EPC (engineering, procurement and construction contractor) to quote. It covers the rule text of four states, the Gujarat tariff schedule for a mall-sized HT (high-tension) connection, and the sub-metering and renewable provisions of the Energy Conservation and Sustainable Building Code 2024. Nothing here names a customer, quotes a Qbits price or reports an installed result. Qbits has none to report.

## Which meter should a mall's solar plant sit behind?

A mall's solar plant belongs to a meter, because net metering is an arrangement between one consumer number and the DISCOM. That connection has a contract demand and a tariff. A complex can have its metering arranged in three common ways, and each gives a different answer.

| Arrangement | What the plant can offset | Sizing limit that applies | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **One HT connection for the whole complex**, tenants sub-metered by the owner or maintenance company | Everything behind that meter: common areas, central AC, and the tenants' sub-metered consumption | The state cap on that connection (sanctioned load or contract demand), then the roof | Who holds the consumer number, and whether tenant billing is by the sub-meter reading |
| **Common-area connection only**, each tenant on its own DISCOM meter | Only what the common-area meter records: lifts, escalators, common lighting, pumps, parking, fire and sewage treatment plant (STP) systems, and central AC if it sits on that meter | Common-area contract demand, and the daytime common-area load | Whether chillers are on the common meter or billed to tenants through chilled-water meters |
| **Mixed**: tenants on their own meters, plus some units and the chiller plant on the owner's connection | The owner's connection | That connection's cap | Which loads sit where, from the single-line diagram |

The sub-metering that ECSBC 2024 asks for makes this easier to read. For a building with a contract demand of 120 kVA or more, clause 8.2.6 and its Table 8.2 require two things. One is a separate sub-meter for the heating, ventilation and air conditioning (HVAC) system and components. The other is a meter on the renewable power source. For a shopping complex, Table 8.3 adds sub-meters for facade, common-area and exterior lighting, and for elevators, escalators and moving walks. In a tenant-based building, the clause also says tenants must be given tap-off points to install electrical sub-meters.

If your building was designed to that code, the half-hourly data you need to size a plant may already sit on the building management system. If not, ask for it before you ask for a quote.

Residential societies follow a different rule set. Karnataka's 2026 regulation lets residential apartments use virtual net metering for common-area consumption, and its category list does not include commercial complexes (clause 4.5.1). We did not read any text that gives a commercial complex the same route. Our [warehouse guide](/blog/solar-for-warehouses-logistics-parks/) reads the group and virtual net-metering rules by state for commercial tenants. It reaches the same finding: where the route exists for commercial consumers, it is narrow.

## Where is the daytime load in a mall?

A mall's load largely coincides with the sun, because it opens at about 10 am and closes at about 10 pm. The loads that matter are these.

| Load | On the common meter? | Fit with a solar day |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Central air conditioning (chillers, cooling towers, chilled-water and condenser pumps, air-handling units) | Often, if the owner runs the plant. Often not, if tenants run their own units | Strong, and usually the largest. Starts before opening, runs to closing |
| Lifts, escalators, moving walks | Yes | Good during opening hours. Idle load overnight |
| Common lighting, facade and signage | Yes | Interior lighting is daytime. Facade and signage lighting is mostly evening, after the sun |
| Water pumps, STP, parking ventilation | Yes | Partial. Some run all day, some on timers |
| Fire pumps and life-safety systems | Yes | Standby. They must work in an outage, which an on-grid plant does not cover |
| EV charging | Often | Daytime charging fits. Overnight charging does not |
| Tenant shops, food courts, cinemas, offices | Behind the main meter if sub-metered, otherwise on their own meters | Daytime for most, evening for cinemas and restaurants |

We found no primary-source figure for how much of an Indian mall's energy goes to air conditioning. Vendor blogs quote 45% to 60% and one single-building study quotes more than 75%. We could not tie any of these to a measurement, so none is used in the calculation. Your own HVAC sub-meter gives the one number that shows whether solar is a daytime match for your building or only a small offset.

Facade lighting and signage, a notable part of a mall's evening bill, will not be served by solar without a battery. A plant is sized to the daytime load. It is not an answer to the night.

## Do chiller starts affect an on-grid solar plant?

A chiller plant is a motor load with a large starting surge, and a grid-tied inverter neither holds the bus voltage nor supplies that surge. The inverter is built to follow the grid and to stop when the grid goes away.

So on a normal day nothing about a chiller start changes when you add an on-grid plant. The compressor starts, the surge comes from the DISCOM connection or the transformer, and the solar output simply adds to the board. Our home [lift and pump guide](/blog/solar-inverter-for-lift-water-pump-motor-loads/) makes the same point at household scale. It cites one pump maker's published starting-current range of 4 to 6.5 times full-load current for direct-on-line starts. For a chiller the multiple depends on the compressor, the starter and whether a variable-frequency drive is fitted, so take it from the chiller maker.

Here is the ratio, with assumed numbers. A 300 kW chiller motor started direct-on-line at six times full-load current would draw the equivalent of about 1,800 kW for a fraction of a second. A 500 kWp plant at Surat peaks at about 350 kW in our simulation. At its very best the plant could cover about a fifth of that surge, and in practice an inverter does not supply it.

The grid carries the surge, and the cable, transformer and protection must be sized for it as they were before the plant. A soft starter or a drive cuts the surge, and it is worth asking for before any bigger transformer.

Two checks follow.

1. **Voltage dip.** A start that pulls the bus voltage down can push inverters out of their voltage window and trip them for the reconnect delay. Ask the EPC what voltage and frequency trip settings the inverters will use, and compare them with the dip your chiller starts cause. The settings come from the grid code and your DISCOM, and we did not open them for this post.
2. **Blackouts and the diesel generator (DG).** An on-grid plant powers nothing in an outage. The inverters shut down by design, so the air conditioning, lifts and lights run on the DG set or not at all. Putting solar on a DG-fed bus needs a deliberate design with an export limit and a controller. Our [DG synchronisation guide](/blog/solar-dg-synchronisation-on-grid-inverter/) covers the architectures and the DG limits that set the solar cap. Karnataka's 2026 regulation also lets a behind-the-meter plant exceed the sanctioned load if it has a voltage-reference source, such as a generator, a battery or an inverter that sets its own reference (clause 4.6.2). It does not tell you how to build one.

Power factor is the third effect. Chillers and lifts are inductive loads. An inverter at unity power factor takes kW off the grid and leaves the kVAr, so the power factor at the meter falls.

On Gujarat's HTP-1 the power-factor charge is 1% of energy charges for each 1% below 90%. ECSBC 2024 clause 8.2.7 asks a compliant building to hold 0.97 at the point of connection. Keep your capacitor bank and automatic power factor correction working, and check the bill after commissioning.

## How much of a mall roof can carry solar panels?

A mall roof is a service deck, not an open warehouse roof, and most of its area is already spoken for. The table lists what takes the space, with our assumed share of a roof for the worked case later. These are our assumptions, not a standard. Replace them with a measured roof drawing.

| What occupies the roof | Assumed share of roof |
| --- | --- |
| Cooling towers, chillers, air-handling units, exhaust and ducts | 22% |
| Lift and stair machine rooms, water tanks, service rooms | 10% |
| Atrium skylights, rooflights and smoke vents | 8% |
| Fire-access routes, edge setbacks, walkways and maintenance clearance | 15% |
| Buffer around tall items that cast shadow | 10% |
| **Left for panels** | **35%** |

The checks below are questions for your structural engineer, fire adviser and insurer. None of them comes from a state rule we read.

- **Structure.** Mall roofs are often designed for plant and a parking or terrace live load, not for a dense array on a mounting frame. Ask for the roof's design load in kg per square metre and for a structural engineer's sign-off on frames, ballast, wind uplift and any new penetrations.
- **Waterproofing.** A mall's top floor often sits under a membrane that is the building's main defence against leaks. Ask whether the frame is ballasted or fixed through the membrane, who repairs the membrane if it is breached, and whether the membrane supplier must approve it.
- **Heat, humidity and soiling.** Cooling-tower drift, chiller exhaust and kitchen exhaust all land on or near the roof. Ask the EPC how far panels will sit from them, and who cleans.
- **Shading.** Parapets, lift machine rooms, water tanks, cooling towers, signage frames, neighbouring towers and the building's own upper blocks all cast shadows, and shadows move through the year. Ask for a shading study that shows the array at 09:00, 12:00 and 15:00 on 21 December.
- **Lightning and earthing.** Ask how the array's earthing ties into the building's lightning protection, and where a tall signage structure sits relative to the array.
- **Fire access.** A mall roof can be a refuge or an access route for firefighters, and some roofs carry helipads. Keep those routes clear, and check with the fire authority before design, not after. The draft Quality Control Manual from MNRE (25 February 2023, section 6.3) lists general fire-safety requirements for rooftop plants. They include convenient roof access, pathways to specific roof areas, an emergency exit from the roof, covered and protected DC cables, load-breaking DC isolators and marked isolation points. The manual is a draft. We did not open the National Building Code 2016 fire part or any state fire-service circular, so the clearances for your building are a question for the fire authority.
- **Insurance.** Tell the property insurer before the plant goes live, and ask whether the fire policy and the plant's own policy interact.
- **Approval.** For plants above a certain size an electrical inspector's approval may apply. Telangana's regulation requires insurance and certification from the Chief Electrical Inspector to Government (CEIG) above 56 kWp, as our [warehouse post](/blog/solar-for-warehouses-logistics-parks/) records. The inverter-side checks are in our [CEIG inspection guide](/blog/ceig-inspection-solar-plant-inverter-checks/).

## How big a plant fits a 5 lakh sq ft mall? A worked case in Surat

**Illustrative only.** Nothing in this case is a quote, a Qbits figure or a result from a site, and every input is stated so you can change it.

### Step 1: roof area to kWp

| Assumption | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Built-up area | 5 lakh sq ft, about 46,450 square metres |
| Roof area | 1,00,000 sq ft, about 9,290 square metres (assumed footprint) |
| Share left for panels | 35%, about 3,250 square metres (table above) |
| Roof area per kWp | 6.5 square metres (assumption) |
| Plant that fits | About 500 kWp, or about 910 modules of 550 Wp |

### Step 2: yield

PVGIS for Surat (21.17, 72.83), 1 kWp at a 10 degree tilt facing south, with 14% system loss, gives 1,524 kWh per kWp a year (ERA5, 2005 to 2023). We take 10% off for dust, heat and downtime, our assumption, and use the hourly series for 2015 to 2019. That gives about **1,371 kWh per kWp a year**, or about 6.86 lakh kWh from 500 kWp. The largest single hour in the five years is about 350 kW from that plant.

### Step 3: the load

We built two stylised loads and a simple hourly pattern. Replace them with your own half-hourly meter data.

| | Whole mall behind one HT meter | Common-area connection only |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10:00 to 22:00 | 1,800 kW | 150 kW |
| 08:00 to 10:00 (pre-opening, pre-cooling) | 700 kW | 45 kW |
| All other hours | 350 kW | 45 kW |
| Annual consumption | About 96.7 lakh kWh | About 8.5 lakh kWh |
| Assumed contract demand | 2,500 kVA | 400 kVA |
| What it includes | Chillers, tenants, common areas | Lifts, escalators, common lighting, pumps, parking, fire and STP. No chillers, no tenants |

### Step 4: how much of the plant the load can use

Gujarat settles an HT consumer's captive solar on a same-day basis between 07:00 and 18:00 (clause 9.2.6 of the Third Amendment). "Used" below is solar output inside that window that the load takes in the same hour. We count nothing for surplus.

| Plant | Output a year | Whole mall: used | Whole mall: share of its energy | Common area: used | Common area: share of its energy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 100 kWp | 1.37 lakh kWh | 99% | 1.4% | 99% | 15.9% |
| 200 kWp | 2.74 lakh kWh | 99% | 2.8% | 97% | 31.3% |
| 300 kWp | 4.11 lakh kWh | 99% | 4.2% | 90% | 43.1% |
| 500 kWp (roof-fill) | 6.86 lakh kWh | 99% | 7.0% | 62% | 49.9% |

Read the last two columns together. A common-area meter with a 150 kW daytime load fills up quickly, and the 500 kWp roof-fill plant then exports more than a third of its output. Behind a meter that carries the whole mall, the same plant is a rounding error against the load, so the roof, not the load, is the limit. Roof area is a poor guide to plant size. The meter is the guide.

### Step 5: what a solar unit is worth

A mall's HT connection would normally fall under Rate HTP-1, which applies to HT consumers contracted for 100 kVA and above "requiring the power supply for the purposes not specified in any other HT Categories". We assume a mall falls there. Check which rate your bill shows.

| HTP-1 item (GERC schedule effective 1 April 2026) | Figure |
| --- | --- |
| Demand charge, up to contract demand | Rs 150 per kVA a month for the first 500 kVA, Rs 260 for the next 500, Rs 475 above 1,000 |
| Billing demand | Highest of actual maximum demand, 85% of contract demand, or 100 kVA |
| Energy charge | 400 paise a unit up to 500 kVA of billing demand, 420 paise from above 500 to 2,500 kVA |
| Peak addition, 07:00 to 11:00 and 18:00 to 22:00 | 45 paise a unit up to 500 kVA, 85 paise above |
| Discount, 11:00 to 17:00 | 60 paise a unit |
| Power factor | 1% of energy charges for each 1% below 90% |

PVGIS puts about 21% of annual output between 07:00 and 11:00, 74% between 11:00 and 17:00 and 5% elsewhere. That weights a solar unit at about Rs 3.93 for the whole-mall connection (billing demand above 500 kVA) and about Rs 3.65 for the common-area connection (billing demand under 500 kVA).

Gujarat then charges Rs 1.50 a unit of solar consumed by a demand-based consumer (GERC clause 9.2.11). The net is about **Rs 2.43** a unit for the whole mall and **Rs 2.15** a unit for the common area. Our [Surat diamond-unit guide](/blog/solar-for-diamond-polishing-units-surat/) reads the full Gujarat captive set-off and banking clauses.

**Solar does not cut the fixed charge.** At 2,500 kVA, billing demand is at least 2,125 kVA whatever the roof does, and a cloud over the array can still set the maximum demand. We count no demand saving.

### Step 6: payback

Payback here is installed cost divided by the yearly saving. The saving is units used times net saving per unit, less a running cost of 1% of installed cost a year. The three costs per kWp are round numbers picked to span a range. They are neither a Qbits price nor a market quote, and we found no sourced 2026 market range to replace them.

The middle column removes the banking charge, as some states do for rooftop net metering (Telangana's regulation exempts it). The right column is not a tariff for any state. It stands for a state with a higher energy rate, or a bill with extra per-unit lines.

| Plant and meter | Cost per kWp | Net after Gujarat banking | Net with no banking charge | Stand-in Rs 6.00 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 500 kWp, whole mall (Rs 2.43) | Rs 35,000 | 11.8 years | 7.0 years | 4.5 years |
| | Rs 45,000 | 15.8 years | 9.2 years | 5.8 years |
| | Rs 55,000 | 20.0 years | 11.5 years | 7.2 years |
| 200 kWp, common area (Rs 2.15) | Rs 35,000 | 13.9 years | 7.7 years | 4.6 years |
| | Rs 45,000 | 18.6 years | 10.2 years | 6.0 years |
| | Rs 55,000 | 23.7 years | 12.7 years | 7.4 years |
| 500 kWp, common area (Rs 2.15) | Rs 45,000 | 32.6 years | 16.9 years | 9.7 years |

Two lessons follow. A plant that is fully used pays back in about the same time whatever its size, so the whole-mall 500 kWp and the common-area 200 kWp land close together. A plant too big for its meter pays back far later. The common-area 500 kWp at the middle cost takes 33 years, against 19 for the 200 kWp, because the last 300 kWp sells its output at the surplus rate, which we counted as zero.

The Gujarat banking charge is the swing factor, so read your own bill line by line. Our [state-wise limits guide](/blog/net-metering-capacity-limits-commercial-by-state/) shows how other states treat the surplus.

## What size cap does the state put on the connection?

Whichever meter you pick, the state caps the plant at a number tied to that connection. The full table for 13 states, with the AC or DC basis, is in our net-metering limits guide. For the two connections in the example:

| State | What the rule says | 2,500 kVA whole-mall connection | 400 kVA common-area connection |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gujarat | No capacity restriction for captive use up to sanctioned load or contract demand (Third Amendment, 2022) | Up to 2,500 kW, roof-limited | Up to 400 kW |
| Maharashtra | 5 MW or contract demand or sanctioned load, whichever is lower (MERC statement of reasons, 2023). Net-metered capacity on a transformer or feeder is not above 70% of its rating unless the licensee allows more (regulation 5.2) | Roof-limited, subject to the 70% transformer rule | Up to 400 kW AC, subject to the same rule |
| Karnataka | Up to the lower of sanctioned load or contract demand, or 1,000 kW, under net metering (KERC 2026, clause 4.7) | 1,000 kW | 400 kW |
| Telangana | Net metering up to 500 kWp. For commercial and other consumers, at most 80% of sanctioned load or contracted demand (clause 4.9) | 500 kWp | 320 kWp |

On a mall's whole connection, none of the four limits stops a 500 kWp plant. On a 400 kVA common-area connection the cap is close to the size that fits the load, so the two limits happen to agree.

Karnataka also offers a behind-the-meter route to all consumer categories, with no sale of surplus and a required reverse-power relay to stop any injection (clauses 4.6 and 5.6.4). For a building whose daytime load always exceeds the plant, that removes the surplus question altogether.

## Who gets the savings from a mall's solar plant?

The saving appears on one bill, the bill of the consumer-number holder, and how it reaches anyone else is a service-charge question. We describe the methods in use without giving legal conclusions, so take advice before you choose one.

- **Common-area charge goes down.** The owner or association pays a lower common-area electricity bill, and the maintenance charge, or common area maintenance (CAM) line, falls for every tenant in proportion to how common costs are shared. This is the simplest method. It needs a clear note on how the saving appears in the CAM reconciliation.
- **Owner keeps it.** Where the owner funded the plant on its own roof and bears the common-area bill, the saving stays with the owner. Tenants see no change.
- **Recharged at a solar tariff.** A RESCO or the owner bills tenants or the maintenance company for solar units at a per-unit rate. That needs a separate agreement and an energy meter on the plant's output.
- **Tenant sub-meter billing.** If tenants are billed by sub-meter, the question is whether the rate is the DISCOM tariff, a blended rate, or a rate that includes the solar offset. We did not read any rule that settles this for a commercial complex. The texts we saw that touch single-point connections (Punjab's regulations as annexed to a PSPCL petition, Karnataka's 2026 text) deal with residential associations and apartments. The Punjab text says that in a residential association with a single-point connection, charges raised on individual consumption must be on a no-profit-no-loss basis. That does not settle a mall. It does show the kind of limit a regulator can put on a single-point connection.
- **Purpose of supply.** The GERC tariff schedule says power supplied to a consumer must be used for the purpose for which supply is taken. Ask your DISCOM how that sits with a plant whose output reaches sub-metered tenants.

Whatever you choose, write the method into the lease or the maintenance agreement before the plant goes live, and keep the solar meter reading in the monthly reconciliation.

## Do HT or LT supply and contract demand change the plan?

A large complex is nearly always on an HT connection, and the contract demand then drives both the cap and the fixed charge. In Gujarat's tariff, HT rates apply from a contract demand of 100 kVA. An eligible consumer with a connected load above 100 kW or kVA is put on an 11 kV three-phase connection (Regulation 8(2), row iv, as amended in 2022).

In Maharashtra an HT consumer may connect the plant on the LT bus bar of its transformer with the net meter on the HT side (regulation 6.4). In Telangana a plant above 56 kWp can connect to an 11 or 33 kV feeder (clause 4.20). Which applies to you is a question for your DISCOM, and it changes the demand charge.

Contract demand raises two points. First, most state caps are tied to it, so a plant larger than it needs either a higher contract demand or a different route. MERC says a consumer may apply to raise contract demand, and that raises the fixed charge too.

Second, solar rarely lowers billing demand on a mall. The maximum demand often falls on a hot afternoon, when the sun is past its peak and the chillers are at full load, and a cloud can reset it. Size on energy, and treat any demand saving as a bonus you must measure.

## Which ownership route fits a commercial complex?

The route depends on who holds the connection and who pays the bill. The table sets out the usual fit for six common situations.

| Situation | Route that usually fits | What to check first |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Owner-occupied** complex, one HT connection, you pay the bill | Own capex on the main meter, sized to the daytime load and then the roof | Contract demand, state cap, roof load and waterproofing |
| **Owner-occupied, no capital** | RESCO (renewable energy service company) or OPEX (operating-expense) model on a roof lease | State's third-party rules (see our [warehouse guide](/blog/solar-for-warehouses-logistics-parks/) for Gujarat, Karnataka and Telangana) |
| **Multi-tenant, one HT connection, tenants sub-metered** | Owner or maintenance company holds the plant on the main meter, with a written method for passing on the saving | CAM method, lease clauses, who pays for roof repairs |
| **Multi-tenant, tenants on own DISCOM meters** | Plant on the common-area connection only, sized to its daytime load. Tenant-level plants only if the roof is divided and the state allows it | Common-area load from the sub-meters, roof allocation |
| **Leased to one anchor tenant or one operator** | The operator installs if it holds the premises on rental basis and the state allows it, or the owner funds and sells to the operator | Lease term against payback, who owns the plant at lease end |
| **Strata-titled or society-owned commercial block** | The society or association holds the plant on its common-area connection, with a members' resolution | Approval by members, insurance, who owns the roof |

A roof lease is a different contract from a power agreement, and the roof owner, the maintenance company and the connection holder may be three parties. A 25-year agreement on a roof with a 9-year head lease is a trap for both sides. The contract structures are explained in our [RESCO and OPEX guide](/blog/zero-cost-solar-resco-opex-india/).

## What does ECSBC 2024 require for rooftop solar on a commercial building?

The Energy Conservation and Sustainable Building Code 2024 (ECSBC) is issued by the Bureau of Energy Efficiency for commercial and office buildings. By its scope it applies to buildings or complexes with a connected load of 100 kW or more or a contract demand of 120 kVA or more. It excludes residential buildings. Its definition of a building complex includes a group of buildings "on lease and sold as shops or office space". We did not check which states have notified it or how it is enforced.

Clause 8.2.11 reads, in part: "All buildings shall have provisions for installation of renewable energy systems." Its sub-clause (a) says the building "shall have onsite renewable energy generation capacity in meeting at least 4% of total contract demand of the building on annual basis or covering minimum of 50% of the available roof area, which ever feasible for ECSBC buildings". ECSBC Plus needs a minimum of 7.5% of total contract demand (Table 8.9), and Super ECSBC 15% (Table 8.10).

The same clause says states with an open access policy may meet the target with off-site green power plus on-site sources. It asks for space on the main service panel for a future renewable circuit breaker. It also asks the design documents to show inverter and meter locations, conduit routes and structural loads for the roof.

Applied to our mall, a 2,500 kVA contract demand gives a 4% figure of 100 kW, 187.5 kW for Plus and 375 kW for Super. A 500 kWp plant is 20% of contract demand. The text does not say how the percentage works for a complex with several connections, so ask your assessor.

Two cautions apply to the printed text. First, the sub-clause also says the rooftop system "should give yield at least 0.46 kWh/sqm/yr". That cannot be a yearly figure, because our own Surat model gives about 290 kWh per square metre of module a year. The unit looks like a misprint, so ask BEE or your assessor what it means.

Second, the code calls for EV charging at 20% of parking capacity for ECSBC, 25% for Plus and 35% for Super (as printed). That adds a daytime load that solar can feed.

For the green-building ratings (IGBC, GRIHA and LEED), we did not read the current credit text and state no requirement. Whichever rating you pursue, the evidence you will need is the same: a dated single-line diagram, the plant's generation meter readings, and the building's own consumption figures.

We did not open ECBC 2017, the earlier code, so we state nothing from it.

## What to bring to an EPC

1. A year of HT bills with every line readable: demand, energy, time-of-day (ToD), fuel and power purchase price adjustment (FPPAS), power factor and duty.
2. A single-line diagram showing every meter, sub-meter and the chiller plant.
3. Half-hourly load data for the HVAC, lift, lighting and common-area sub-meters, for a normal week and the lowest-load week.
4. Contract demand, sanctioned load, supply voltage, tariff category and the consumer-number holder.
5. A roof drawing with plant, towers, tanks, skylights, fire-access routes and structural loads marked.
6. Your lease and maintenance agreement: who owns the roof, how common costs are shared, any clause on rooftop equipment.
7. A written proposal that names the cap it designed to and the banking or grid charge it assumed. It should show the value of a solar unit by time band, the dust allowance, the shading study and its assumption for surplus.

## How should a facility manager decide?

Decide the meter first, then the size. Work out the common-area or whole-building daytime load from your own sub-meters, size the plant to that load and the state cap, and only then ask whether the roof can hold it. Write down how the saving reaches the people who pay for it before the plant is built.
