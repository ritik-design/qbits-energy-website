Search for the **best solar design software** and you get ranked lists that never say who the ranking is for. A residential installer closing a 5 kW rooftop in forty minutes and a consultant writing a P90 report for a lender are not buying the same product. The installer needs a proposal a homeowner will sign. The consultant needs an energy model a third party can audit and reproduce.

This guide compares 13 tools on the axes that decide the purchase, then splits the picks by segment: residential installer, commercial and industrial EPC, utility-scale developer, and consultant producing bankable reports. ## How to Choose Solar Design Software: Eight Axes That Decide It

Feature lists are nearly useless here, because every vendor claims every feature. The eight axes below separate tools that look identical on a comparison grid. Score your requirement on each before booking a demo, then run one real past project through every shortlisted tool rather than the vendor's sample site.

1. **Design fidelity against speed.** Decide which failure hurts more: a lost sale, or a redesign after site survey. No tool optimises for both.
2. **Simulation engine and loss tree.** Ask whether soiling, thermal, mismatch, wiring, incidence angle and degradation losses are separately editable. One opaque derate factor is not a model.
3. **Shading analysis method.** Ray-traced module-level shading, horizon-only shading and a flat obstruction shadow are three different accuracies. See [shading analysis](/glossary/shading-analysis/).
4. **CAD import and export.** Ground mount and most C&I work starts from a DWG survey. A tool that cannot ingest contours and export a markable drawing is a sales tool.
5. **Proposal and financial modelling.** Can it produce a branded document with payback, cashflow and tariff escalation, in your currency, without a second subscription?
6. **CRM integration.** A design tool with no pipeline behind it creates double data entry. Some teams pair a design platform with a solar CRM such as [Sunbase](/blog/sunbase-review/).
7. **Pricing model.** Per-project billing punishes high-volume residential teams. Quote-only billing punishes small teams with no leverage.
8. **India applicability.** DISCOM tariff slabs, rupee modelling, GST treatment, and the SKUs you actually procure. The [India guide](/blog/solar-design-software-india/) goes deeper.

## Best Solar Design Software for Residential Installers

For rooftops under about 10 kW, the deliverable is a proposal a homeowner signs. Simulation precision beyond a decent loss tree rarely changes the sale. Time from address to branded PDF does. OpenSolar, Arka 360 and Solargraf all optimise for that loop, and Aurora Solar does it with the strongest US sales output of the group.

Run a revision test, not a first-design test. Build one real past project in each tool, then change the module, change the inverter, and move a shading obstruction. Count the minutes for the revision. Residential teams revise far more often than they design from scratch, and the tools diverge sharply on revision cost.

Watch the billing unit too. Per-project and per-credit plans cost more than a flat seat once volume rises, and residential volume rises fastest. Aurora and HelioScope both publish a monthly project allowance, which matters if your team quotes far more jobs than it closes.

## Best Solar Design Software for Commercial and Industrial EPCs

C&I means multiple roof planes, mixed azimuths, plant load profiles, three-phase constraints, and a customer who may hire an independent engineer. HelioScope is the common answer because it produces credible module-level shading and an 8,760-hour production profile fast enough to bid with. Its own pricing page states accuracy within 1% of PVsyst, which is the vendor's claim rather than an independent finding.

Two limits to plan around. HelioScope Basic caps system size at 1.25 MW DC and Pro at 5 MW DC, per the published plan comparison, while PVsyst export and single-axis trackers sit on the quote-only Enterprise tier. If your pipeline crosses those thresholds, price that conversation before standardising.

Where the business case turns on demand charges and storage dispatch rather than kWh, [Energy Toolbase](/blog/energy-toolbase-review/) is a better economics layer than any design tool. It models tariffs and dispatch, not layout, so teams run it alongside a design platform.

## Best Solar Design Software for Utility-Scale Developers

Utility-scale design is a terrain problem before it is a PV problem. Row layout, cut and fill, road access, tracker geometry and cable routing dominate the work, and all of it starts from surveyed contours. PVcase runs natively inside AutoCAD, which suits teams whose civil engineers already live there. RatedPower automates whole-plant layout and balance-of-system sizing, which suits developers screening many sites fast.

Both are quote-only, so budget a procurement cycle rather than a credit card. The [utility-scale software checklist](/blog/utility-scale-solar-design-software/) covers the outputs an independent engineer will ask for. Drone survey belongs here too, though it is a different category: [Scanifly](/blog/scanifly-review/) builds an as-built 3D model from photogrammetry rather than satellite imagery, so it competes with your survey process, not your design tool.

## Why PVsyst Still Owns Bankable Reports, and When You Need It

PVsyst persists for a structural reason, not a technical one. Lenders, independent engineers and offtakers standardised on its report format and loss tree, so a PVsyst output is reviewable by someone who has never met you. That reproducibility is the product. At CHF 700 per year for PVsyst 8 Professional, checked on the vendor's product page on 24 September 2026, it is also among the cheapest per-seat licences here. Grouped orders take a published discount of 5% for 2 to 4 licences, 15% for 5 to 9, and 20% for 10 or more.

You need it when a third party reviews your energy model: debt finance, an offtake agreement, an EPC contract carrying a performance clause, or a tender that names the tool.

You do not need it for a 5 kW rooftop nobody outside your company will audit. Paying for reproducibility that no one consumes is the most common overspend in this category. For the head-to-head, see [HelioScope versus PVsyst](/blog/helioscope-vs-pvsyst/); the [P50 and P90](/glossary/p50-p90/) entry explains the probability bands a bankable report reports.

## Free and Low-Cost Solar Design Software, and Where It Runs Out

Free means someone else pays, and it matters who. OpenSolar states on its own site that the platform is free to solar professionals, funded by product manufacturers, finance companies, distributors, permitting companies and education providers. SolarEdge Designer states it carries no licence or subscription fee. Both are genuinely usable, and both have a defined ceiling:

- **Vendor scope.** SolarEdge Designer is built around SolarEdge inverters and optimisers. Reasonable if you are a SolarEdge-only installer, a hard stop if you are not.
- **Partner-funded defaults.** A platform funded by manufacturers and financiers carries commercial relationships inside the product. Check which equipment and finance options surface by default.
- **Bankable output.** Neither is the format a lender expects on a financed project. That gap is why a PVsyst seat sits alongside a free tool at many firms.
- **Support depth.** Free tiers rarely carry the response times a live C&I bid needs.

## What Solar Design Software Costs in 2026

Published seat prices span zero to roughly USD 2,640 per user per year, with Energy Toolbase higher because it is an economics platform rather than a design tool. The structural pattern matters more than any single figure: the residential and sales end of the market publishes list prices, while the utility-scale end almost never does. PVcase, RatedPower and Scanifly all quote per account.

That split has a consequence. Quote-only vendors price against your perceived budget, so a five-person EPC and a fifty-person developer can pay very different rates for the same product. Flat published pricing removes that asymmetry, which is worth something even when the headline number is higher.

List price is also not total cost. Add the second subscription for whatever your primary tool does not do: proposals on top of a simulation engine, or a bankable model on top of a proposal tool. ## India-Specific Requirements Most Global Tools Skip

An Indian EPC evaluating a US-built tool should test four things the demo will not show. None appear on a feature grid, and all four are where global platforms quietly fail an Indian project.

**Tariff libraries.** Retail tariffs are set by state regulators and differ by [DISCOM](/glossary/discom/), consumer category and slab. A generic rate produces a payback number that will not survive contact with a real bill. Confirm the tool holds your state's current slabs, or that you can override them per project.

**Subsidy logic.** Under PM Surya Ghar Muft Bijli Yojana, central financial assistance for residential rooftop is 60% of system cost up to 2 kW and 40% of the additional cost from 2 kW to 3 kW, capped at 3 kW. At benchmark prices that is ₹30,000 for 1 kW, ₹60,000 for 2 kW, and ₹78,000 for 3 kW and above, according to the Ministry of New and Renewable Energy operational guidelines (2024). One detail design software often gets wrong: assistance is set by rated DC module capacity, not by inverter rating. Benchmark costs are revised periodically and eligibility is never automatic, so verify current slabs on the national portal and read the [PM Surya Ghar subsidy breakdown](/blog/pm-surya-ghar-subsidy-amount/).

**Rupee financial modelling.** Payback, IRR and cashflow need to run in rupees with Indian GST treatment and, for C&I, accelerated depreciation. Converting a dollar model at a fixed rate is not Indian financial modelling.

**Approval workflow.** Net metering application, feasibility approval and inspection are DISCOM processes with their own document sets, and they vary by state. Software will not file them, but the drawings and single-line diagram it exports should match what your DISCOM accepts. The [net metering guide](/blog/how-to-apply-net-metering-india/) covers the sequence.

## The Contrarian Take: Most Installers Overbuy Simulation and Underbuy Proposal Speed

Here is the argument plainly. For Indian residential work, most installers buy more simulation depth than their projects can use, and far less proposal speed than their conversion rate needs. The reason is that simulation depth is legible in a demo and proposal speed is not.

Consider where the uncertainty in a 5 kW unshaded rooftop actually sits. Weather dataset choice, soiling assumptions in a dusty Indian city, and real module degradation dominate the error bar. Swapping a browser engine for a desktop one moves a fraction of a percent while those inputs move several percent. Paying for engine precision while guessing at soiling is a badly allocated budget.

Now flip it, because the inverse mistake is more expensive. C&I EPCs bidding against an independent engineer routinely underbuy simulation and overbuy proposal polish. A polished proposal loses to a reviewable energy model every time an engineer is in the room.

The rule: buy simulation depth in proportion to how many external reviewers read your output, and proposal speed in proportion to how many quotes you issue per closed deal. Most residential teams have a high second number and a zero first number. Most C&I teams have the opposite. Very few have both, and those are the only teams that genuinely need two subscriptions.

## Worked Example: Checking a String Against a Real Inverter Voltage Window

Design software sizes strings against the component library it ships. That library can be stale, and it may not hold the exact SKU you are ordering. This is where software selection meets hardware selection, so here is the arithmetic the tool performs, made visible. It is a worked example, not field data.

Inputs. Module: 685 Wp n-type TOPCon, Voc 47.98 V, Vmp 39.87 V, Voc temperature coefficient minus 0.26 %/°C, Vmp temperature coefficient minus 0.30 %/°C, all at standard test conditions of 25 °C. Inverter: a three-phase on-grid unit rated 10 kW, maximum DC input 1,100 V, [MPPT](/glossary/mppt/) window 180 V to 1,000 V.

Step 1, cold-case open-circuit voltage at a design low of 0 °C:

Voc(0 °C) = 47.98 × (1 + (-0.26 / 100) × (0 - 25)) = 47.98 × 1.065 = 51.10 V per module.

Step 2, hot-case maximum power voltage at a design cell temperature of 65 °C:

Vmp(65 °C) = 39.87 × (1 + (-0.30 / 100) × (65 - 25)) = 39.87 × 0.88 = 35.09 V per module.

Step 3, the two binding limits:

- Maximum modules per string = 1,100 V ÷ 51.10 V = 21.5, so 21 modules.
- Minimum modules per string = 180 V ÷ 35.09 V = 5.13, so 6 modules.

The valid window is 6 to 21 modules per string. A string of 22 exceeds maximum DC input on a cold winter morning, which is a fault condition, not a warning. The failure appears only in the cold case, which is exactly the case a rushed design skips.

Run the same check against the model you are actually buying. Voltage windows and per-MPPT current limits differ between models inside one product range, so a library entry for a neighbouring SKU is not good enough. For overcurrent protection see [OCP practice in India](/blog/solar-string-sizing-ocp-india/), and for the DC to AC ratio above it see [DC oversizing](/blog/dc-oversizing-in-solar/).

The best solar design software is the cheapest tool that produces the deliverable your reviewer accepts. That turns a feature comparison into one question about who reads your output.
