## What a Banquet Hall Actually Consumes

Before sizing anything, it helps to see where the electricity goes. A wedding hall hosting a full-day function consumes 500 to 2,000 units in a single day, according to [Tristar Green Energy Solutions](https://www.tristarenergy.in/blog/solar-for-wedding-halls-marriage-mandapam) (2026). The dominant load is always cooling.

**Typical load breakdown for a 1,000-guest banquet hall (industry-observed ranges):**

| Load | Connected Capacity | Share of Event-Day Units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| HVAC (chillers or package ACs) | 40 to 120 kW | 50 to 70% |
| Lighting (hall, facade, parking) | 8 to 20 kW | 8 to 12% |
| Kitchen and catering equipment | 10 to 25 kW | 10 to 15% |
| Sound, DJ, and stage systems | 3 to 8 kW | 2 to 4% |
| Water pumps, lifts, misc. | 5 to 15 kW | 5 to 8% |
| Offices, rooms, idle-day base load | 5 to 15 kW | 3 to 6% |

Three features of this profile matter for solar design. First, the load is spiky: a hall may run 150 kW during a Saturday wedding and 15 kW on an idle Tuesday. Second, it is seasonal, with peak bookings from November to February and again in April and May. Third, it is evening-heavy, because muhuratam timings, receptions, and dinners all run from about 6 pm to midnight.

Idle days still cost money. Security lighting, office loads, refrigeration, and pre-event preparation draw 50 to 150 units daily even with no booking. That base load is entirely daytime-addressable by solar.

## The Evening-Load Problem: Why Owners Think Solar Is Wrong for Them

The core concern is real. Solar panels generate between roughly 8 am and 6 pm. Banquet revenue events run 6 pm to midnight. If solar could only serve loads in real time, a marriage hall would waste most of what it generates. Real-time self-consumption might reach only 20 to 30 percent.

This is where most online advice stops, and where most hall owners give up. But the premise is wrong. It assumes solar energy must be consumed the instant it is produced. Grid-connected commercial solar in India does not work that way.

Under a net metering arrangement, every unit exported during the day earns a credit on your bill. Every unit you draw at night is netted against those credits. Your hall physically runs on grid power at 9 pm, but financially it runs on the solar you exported at 1 pm. Our [complete guide to net metering in India](/blog/net-metering-india-complete-guide/) explains the billing mechanics state by state.

The exception you must check: some states have moved larger commercial consumers to gross metering or net billing. Under those rules, exported units earn a lower feed-in rate than the retail tariff you pay at night. Even then, the feed-in rate typically returns Rs 3 to 4 per unit of value. The [gross metering vs net metering](/blog/gross-metering-vs-net-metering/) comparison shows how the economics shift. Confirm your DISCOM's current rule before finalizing system size.

## Three Ways to Solve the Evening-Load Problem

There are three working architectures for evening-heavy commercial venues. They can also be combined.

### Option 1: On-grid solar with net metering (the virtual battery)

This is the default answer for halls in cities with reliable grids. A standard 3-phase on-grid system generates all day, serves the daytime base load directly, and exports the surplus. Night consumption is offset by the credits. There is no battery to buy, maintain, or replace. That keeps system cost at Rs 40,000 to 55,000 per kW and payback under 4.5 years.

The system dies during a grid outage because of anti-islanding protection. If your venue cannot tolerate any interruption during a function, you still need the diesel generator you already own. That brings us to the third option.

### Option 2: Hybrid inverter plus battery

A hybrid system stores part of the daytime surplus in batteries and discharges it in the evening. This makes sense in two situations. Either your DISCOM offers poor export terms, or you face frequent evening power cuts and want silent, instant backup for critical loads.

The practical design is to back up only lighting, sound, and essential circuits, not the full HVAC load. Backing up 100 kW of air conditioning for four hours needs roughly 400 kWh of storage. At current LFP prices of Rs 22,000 to 28,000 per kWh, that is not economical for most halls. A 30 to 60 kWh bank covering critical loads costs Rs 7 to 17 lakh and keeps the event alive through cuts. The [battery sizing guide for hybrid solar](/blog/battery-sizing-hybrid-solar/) walks through the calculation.

### Option 3: DG hybridization (solar synchronized with your generator)

Most banquet halls already own a 62.5 to 200 kVA diesel generator. Diesel generation costs Rs 22 to 28 per unit once fuel and maintenance are counted (industry-observed range, 2026). A solar-DG synchronization controller lets the solar plant keep running during outages, with the generator throttled down to minimum loading. Every solar unit generated during a cut displaces a diesel unit worth four to five times more.

This architecture suits halls in Tier-2 and Tier-3 cities with unreliable grids and multi-hour outages during wedding season. The [solar vs diesel generator](/blog/solar-vs-diesel-generator-india/) analysis compares the two on cost per unit over ten years.

## Sizing the System: 50 to 200 kW Halls

Size the plant to your annual consumption and roof area, not to your peak connected load. The evening-load problem means daytime real-time matching is not the constraint.

**Sizing reference for banquet and marriage halls:**

| Hall Profile | Sanctioned Load | Suggested Solar | Roof Area Needed | Annual Generation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Small hall, 300 to 500 guests | 40 to 60 kW | 25 to 40 kW | 1,800 to 3,000 sq ft | 35,000 to 58,000 units |
| Mid-size hall, 500 to 1,000 guests | 60 to 120 kW | 50 to 100 kW | 3,500 to 7,500 sq ft | 70,000 to 1,60,000 units |
| Large venue or lawn plus hall | 120 to 250 kW | 100 to 200 kW | 7,500 to 15,000 sq ft | 1,40,000 to 3,20,000 units |

Three sizing rules we apply on C&I projects. First, keep solar capacity within your DISCOM's net metering limit, which in most states is tied to sanctioned load or transformer capacity. Second, use 3-phase string inverters of 25 to 50 kW each rather than a single large central unit. Multiple inverters mean one fault never takes down the whole plant. Our [3-phase solar inverter](/blog/3-phase-solar-inverter/) guide explains why string architecture dominates this segment. Third, check structural loading. Banquet halls often have large flat RCC roofs that are ideal, but pre-engineered steel sheds need a structural certificate before panel mounting.

Generation planning uses 1,400 to 1,600 units per kW per year for most of India, higher in Rajasthan and Gujarat, lower in the northeast and Kerala. The [solar inverter for factory](/blog/solar-inverter-for-factory/) guide covers C&I design considerations that apply equally to large venues.

## The Tradeoffs: Honest Limits of Each Architecture

No architecture wins everywhere. Here is the comparison we walk venue owners through before they sign anything.

| | Net Metering Only | Hybrid + Battery | Solar + DG Sync |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Upfront cost (100 kW) | Lowest, Rs 45 to 55 lakh | Higher by Rs 7 to 17 lakh | Low plus controller cost |
| Works in grid outage | No | Yes, critical loads only | Yes, with DG running |
| Storage maintenance | None | Battery replacement in 10 to 12 years | DG maintenance as usual |
| Best when grid is | Reliable | Unreliable, short cuts | Unreliable, long cuts |
| Depends on DISCOM policy | Heavily | Partly | Hardly |

> **What most banquet hall owners get wrong.** They compare solar against their peak evening load and conclude it cannot help. The right comparison is annual units generated against annual units billed. Under net metering, the sun does not need to shine during your event for solar to pay for it.

Two honest limitations. First, if your state shifts commercial consumers of your size to gross metering at a low feed-in rate, the evening-offset value drops. A hybrid design then starts making more sense. The [on-grid vs hybrid vs off-grid decision guide](/blog/on-grid-vs-hybrid-vs-off-grid-decision-guide/) maps that choice. Second, roof ownership matters. Halls on leased premises need landlord consent and a long enough lease to recover the investment. A 25-year asset on a 5-year lease is a poor bet.

## Mistakes Banquet Hall Owners Make With Solar

These are the errors we see repeatedly in C&I venue projects.

1. **Sizing to peak load instead of annual units.** A 150 kW peak does not mean a 150 kW plant. Size from your last 12 months of bills and roof area.
2. **Ignoring the metering regulation.** Signing an EPC contract before confirming net metering eligibility with the DISCOM has killed projects at the approval stage.
3. **Buying batteries sized for the full HVAC load.** Back up lights, sound, and essential circuits. Let the DG handle air conditioning during long cuts.
4. **Accepting a single central inverter.** Multiple 3-phase string inverters isolate faults and simplify service during wedding season, when downtime is measured in cancelled bookings.
5. **Skipping the structural check.** A 100 kW plant adds roughly 12 to 15 kg per square foot. Get a structural certificate for steel-shed roofs.
6. **Choosing on sticker price over warranty and service.** An inverter failure during a booked weekend costs more than the price gap between brands.
