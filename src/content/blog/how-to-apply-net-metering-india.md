**Short version.** Confirm the arrangement your DISCOM currently offers, check your sanctioned connection can carry the proposed capacity, apply through the official route with a complete document set, and track the application by stage. Most delay comes from incomplete submissions and from problems that were visible before applying.

## Understand what you are applying for

Net metering is not a national scheme with one process. It is a connection arrangement administered by your distribution utility under regulations made by the state electricity regulatory commission. Capacity limits, eligible consumer categories, permitted export, settlement method and charges are set at that level and revised by order.

Two consequences follow. A procedure described by someone in another state may not apply to you. And a tariff or settlement outcome quoted from elsewhere should not be used in your project economics.

Net and gross metering also produce different billing outcomes, and not every DISCOM offers both to every category. The [gross versus net guide](/blog/gross-metering-vs-net-metering/) explains the distinction. Your DISCOM's current order controls which is available to you.

## Before you apply, check three things

These checks are free, and each one prevents a rejection that costs weeks.

1. **Sanctioned load and connection type.** Read them from the bill. Your permitted solar capacity is usually tied to sanctioned load, not to available roof area. A design sized to the roof and not to the connection is the single most common reason a proposal has to be revised.
2. **Phase.** A three phase inverter needs a three phase service. If capacity pushes you past your DISCOM's single phase threshold, a connection upgrade becomes part of the project rather than an afterthought. The [single versus three phase guide](/blog/single-vs-3-phase-inverter/) covers this.
3. **Transformer headroom.** Many DISCOMs cap total solar connected to a distribution transformer as a proportion of its rating. If your local transformer is already at that limit, feasibility can be refused for reasons entirely outside your property. Ask whether this constraint applies locally before committing to equipment.

## The application sequence

Treat this as a process map. Your DISCOM's current instructions govern the exact stage order and naming.

1. **Confirm the rule and the arrangement.** Consumer category, sanctioned connection, permitted capacity and the export arrangement currently offered.
2. **Prepare the proposed system.** Module capacity, exact inverter model designation, phase, single line diagram, and the equipment certificates the applicable rules require. [Verifying the inverter model against your DISCOM](/blog/discom-approval-solar-inverter-net-metering/) covers how to confirm acceptance before you pay, since there is no national approved inverter list.
3. **Submit through the official route.** Keep the application reference number and a copy of every document exactly as submitted.
4. **Technical feasibility.** The utility assesses the proposal against the network and the regulations. This is where an application is genuinely decided.
5. **Respond to clarifications promptly.** Use the same reference. Applications frequently sit waiting on a document nobody told the consumer was outstanding, so check status rather than assuming silence means progress.
6. **Install under the accepted design.** Build what was approved. Changing the inverter model or capacity after feasibility generally means revisiting the approval.
7. **Inspection and meter work.** The utility or its authorised agency installs and seals the meter. Keep the commissioning and test reports.
8. **Collect the approval records.** Keep the connection approval and check the first statement against the approved arrangement.

For PM Surya Ghar, begin at the [official portal](https://pmsuryaghar.gov.in/) and follow the linked state and DISCOM process, then check the scheme specific vendor and equipment conditions separately.

## Documents to prepare

The mandatory list is utility specific. A typical file includes the following.

| Document | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| Recent electricity bill | Establishes consumer number, category and sanctioned load |
| Identity and ownership proof | Ownership or a no objection from the owner where relevant |
| Proposed capacity details | Module count, wattage and total DC capacity |
| Inverter datasheet | For the exact model designation, not a brand or capacity label |
| Single line diagram | Showing grid, PV, protection, isolation and metering |
| Equipment certificates | As required by the applicable rules and scheme conditions |
| Installer details | Including empanelment status where the scheme requires it |
| Accepted quotation | Scope of work and responsibility split |

Ask the installer to state which of these they will produce, which they will submit, and which they will hand to you. The [vendor verification guide](/blog/empanelled-vendor-pm-surya-ghar/) covers the installer check, and the [wiring explainer](/blog/solar-inverter-wiring-diagram/) helps you read the single line diagram rather than just filing it.

## What technical feasibility actually examines

Understanding this stage helps you avoid the problems it finds.

The utility is checking whether your proposed system can connect safely without adversely affecting the network. That typically covers proposed capacity against sanctioned load, phase and connection suitability, available headroom on the distribution transformer, the protection and isolation arrangement, and whether the equipment satisfies the conditions in force.

If feasibility is refused, ask for the reason in writing. A refusal based on transformer loading is a different problem from one based on an oversized proposal, and only one of them is solved by revising your design.

## Meter installation and commissioning

The utility, or an agency it authorises, installs and seals the meter. An installer should not seal, alter or replace a utility meter, and an offer to do so should change how you view the rest of their work.

Before the inspection, make sure the installed system matches the approved design, that protection and isolation are in place and labelled, and that commissioning test results are recorded. The inspection expects to find an installation ready for connection, not one already exporting.

Do not energise and export before the approved arrangement is in place. Doing so can breach your connection agreement and complicate the approval you are waiting for.

## Charges to ask about before you apply

Application costs are set by the DISCOM and the state commission, and they vary enough that quoting figures would mislead. What is reasonably consistent is the list of categories, and asking about each one avoids an unwelcome surprise late in the process.

- **Application or processing fee**, payable at submission.
- **Meter cost.** Whether the bidirectional meter is supplied by the utility at a prescribed charge, or procured by the consumer to a specification.
- **Testing and inspection charges** for the commissioning visit.
- **Security deposit revision.** Some utilities revise the deposit where the connection arrangement changes.
- **Connection upgrade cost**, if capacity requires moving from single phase to three phase.
- **Re-inspection charges**, if the first inspection fails and a second visit is needed.

Ask for these in writing at the quotation stage and establish whether the installer's price includes them or whether they are payable by you directly to the utility. A quote that is silent on the point usually means the second.

## If the application is pending

Find the current stage and any outstanding request before escalating. Keep a dated log of portal status changes and correspondence, because an escalation supported by a record is treated differently from one that is not.

Where your state commission prescribes a timeline for a stage, cite that stage specifically rather than complaining about the total elapsed time. The [approval timeline guide](/blog/net-meter-approval-timeline/) sets out stage specific follow up without promising a universal deadline.

## After approval

Check your first statement against the approved arrangement. Confirm that import and export are being recorded as expected, that the settlement method matches what was approved, and that any banking or carry forward provision is reflected.

Raise discrepancies early, with the approval document and meter readings attached. Billing corrections are considerably easier in the first cycle than after several.
