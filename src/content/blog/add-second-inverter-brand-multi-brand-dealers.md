**Add a second inverter brand to your dealership only if it fills a gap in your enquiry log, passes the same evidence checks as your first brand, puts its terms in writing, and survives a 90-day pilot with exit rules you wrote beforehand.** Most dealers work in the opposite order. They meet a representative, like the margin on the first call, and find the problems after the stock arrives.

This guide is for a dealer or distributor who already sells one or more inverter brands. It is not about becoming a first-time dealer. It gives you nine checks, a channel-conflict test, a pilot design with an illustrative scorecard, and a table of signals to proceed, pilot or walk away. It lists questions to ask, not any brand's answers, and it describes no Qbits dealer terms.

This is general information as of **30 October 2026**, not legal, tax or financial advice. Have a lawyer and an accountant read any draft agreement.

> **Short version**
> - Start with your own enquiry log, not the brand's brochure. A new brand earns its place by covering a class you lose today, or by serving a buyer the incumbent does not.
> - Read your current agreement first. Exclusivity, purchase targets and volume rebates on your existing brand can make a second brand cost more than it earns.
> - Ask every commercial question as a written line item. A number you do not hold in writing is not a term.
> - Pilot before you commit: a fixed exposure cap, two or three capacity classes, checkpoints at day 30, 60 and 90, and red lines that end the pilot regardless of score.

## What are the nine checks before you add an inverter brand to your dealership?

Run the checks in this order. The early ones are cheap and can end the idea in a week.

| # | Check | The question it answers | Cost to run |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Demand and fit | Which classes do you lose or never quote today? | Your own log, a few hours |
| 2 | Product evidence | Do current datasheets, certificates and registrations match the exact models? | Document requests, a week |
| 3 | Compliance and procurement fit | Can the models be sold and used in the projects you serve? | Reading, plus authority checks |
| 4 | Commercial terms in writing | What do you actually pay, owe and risk? | A question list and a reply |
| 5 | Service and warranty process | Who attends a failed unit, how fast, with which spares? | A written process, not a promise |
| 6 | Channel conflict | Will it cannibalise your incumbent or your installers? | Arithmetic on your own sales |
| 7 | Pilot | Does it work in your district, with your installers? | 90 days and a capped exposure |
| 8 | Risks | What if the brand, the rules or the credit change? | A short register |
| 9 | Due diligence on the brand | Is the company what it says it is? | Public records and references |

## Check 1: Which gap would a second brand fill?

A second brand needs a defined gap to fill, so decide the gap before you speak to any brand. Our guide to [stock planning for new dealers](/blog/solar-inverter-stock-planning-new-dealers/) sets out an enquiry log with the fields that matter: capacity asked, phase, subsidy or private job, quote date, won or lost, and the reason. You already have a history, so use it.

Sort your lost and unquoted enquiries by capacity class and by the reason recorded. Then place each class in one of four rows.

| Position | What your log shows | What a second brand could add | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gap | Enquiries you do not quote, because the incumbent has no suitable model | A product where you currently have none | Low overlap, but you must prove demand with more than three or four enquiries |
| Weak spot | You quote but lose, with "price" or "model not suitable" as the reason | A second price point or a better-fitting spec | The incumbent may react with a better offer, which you could have asked for without a new brand |
| Stock-out | You lose because stock was not there | Supply depth, not a new brand | A second brand is an expensive fix for a planning fault |
| Overlap | You win these today with the incumbent | A bargaining position over the incumbent | The new brand splits one demand across two stock lines |

Two things follow. First, if most of your reason for adding a brand sits in the "stock-out" or "overlap" rows, you are buying bargaining power, not coverage. Say so to yourself honestly. Second, a "gap" class needs enough rows to trust. Use at least 8 to 12 weeks of data and say in writing when the sample is small.

Also count the other side: the installers who buy from you. Ask five or six which brands they are asked for by name, which they avoid and why. Their answers show whether a challenger faces brand resistance at the customer's door.

## Check 2: Does the product evidence hold up on identical rows?

A challenger brand's datasheet is a claim, not evidence of performance. Read it against the same rows you use for any brand, and compare it with your incumbent's sheet.

Our comparison posts show the method on real documents: [Qbits vs Solis](/blog/qbits-vs-solis/) puts two brands side by side from their own current datasheets, class by class, and the sibling comparisons against GoodWe and Havells use the same rows. The method matters more than any verdict in them. For any brand you are evaluating, do this:

1. **Get the exact model code and the datasheet version and date,** including the country version of the unit you would receive.
2. **Fill the same rows for each brand:** AC rating, number of MPPT trackers, current per tracker, DC voltage window, efficiency as printed (and which efficiency it is), IP rating, operating temperature and derating, weight and communication.
3. **Mark every blank cell** as "not printed". A blank is a question for the brand, not a zero.
4. **Run your own string layouts** through the tracker map. A model that looks cheaper but needs one more unit per roof is not cheaper.
5. **Treat every figure as the brand's own claim,** unless an independent test report states it.

Then ask for the documents that sit behind the sheet: current test certificates with issue dates, the registration number or licence record for each exact model, the user manual and any change notices. A standard printed on a datasheet is not the registration record, so keep both in the file.

## Check 3: Can you legally sell and use the models in your projects?

Compliance fit decides whether you can sell the model into the projects you serve. It has three layers, and a different body decides each one.

**Product conformity.** The Solar Systems, Devices and Components Goods Order, 2025 is the controlling national document for inverter safety and performance standards, and BIS is the certifying body. Our [regulations guide](/blog/solar-inverter-regulations-india-2026-bis-iec-compliance/) sets out the order's inverter entries, the separate route for units above 200 kW (a conditional self-certification route that, as that guide records, runs to 31 December 2026 or a further order, whichever is earlier), and an eight-step stocking checklist. Use that checklist for every exact model. It also records that MNRE's ALMM lists cover modules and cells, not inverters, so a brand claiming "ALMM approved" for an inverter should be asked which list it means.

**DISCOM acceptance.** The guide on [DISCOM approval for inverters](/blog/discom-approval-solar-inverter-net-metering/) explains why there is no single national list: acceptance is decided by your own DISCOM, per model and per revision. If you serve several DISCOMs, make a grid with models down the side and DISCOMs across the top, and fill each cell only with a document or a written confirmation. An empty cell is "unknown".

**Tender and scheme fit.** Many of your installers work on scheme or tender jobs with their own equipment conditions. Ask yourself which of your last twenty orders came from such jobs, and check whether the challenger's evidence suits them before you stock it.

Tax treatment is a separate matter. The challenger's models may fall under a GST entry that differs from what you are used to, so ask the brand to state the HSN code and the rate it will invoice, in writing, and take the reading to your accountant.

## Check 4: Which commercial terms should you request in writing?

This is a question list, not a statement of what any brand offers. Send the same list to the challenger and, where it applies, to your incumbent. Ask for the reply in a document with a date, not on a call.

| Term | What to ask | Why it matters to a second brand |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Price validity | For how many days is a quoted price held, and what happens on an order placed within that period? | A short validity against a long order cycle erodes your quote margin |
| Margin structure | Is the margin a discount on list price, an invoice rebate, a quarterly rebate or a mix? On which base, and when is it earned? | A rebate paid in arrears is not margin until it is credited |
| Payment and credit | Advance, payment on delivery, or credit? Limit, due date, security, charges and suspension rules | Credit is exposure you carry from the first invoice |
| Minimum order | Per order, per quarter or per year? Is it a target or a binding obligation? | A binding minimum on a pilot defeats its purpose |
| Territory and exclusivity | Named area, exclusive or non-exclusive, reserved accounts, online and government sales, lead ownership | See the section on exclusivity below |
| Stock and consignment | Is stock bought, on consignment, or shipped against each order? Who holds title and insurance? | Consignment changes both your cash and your tax records |
| Returns | Which stock can be returned, in what condition, in what window, with whose freight and what credit? | Slow movers are the real cost of a trial |
| Price protection | If the brand cuts its price after you bought stock, do you get a credit note for unsold units? | Without it, a price cut hurts you twice |
| Marketing support | What is funded, on what evidence, and when is it reimbursed? | A promised fund that needs approval each time is not a budget |
| Agreement clauses | Term, renewal, termination notice, exit stock, non-compete, brand use | Use the clause list in the [dealer agreement checklist](/blog/solar-dealer-agreement-checklist/) |

For credit, targets and territory together, the [credit and territory review guide](/blog/solar-distributor-credit-territory-terms/) shows how to turn each into a formula with named inputs before you accept it. Run the same arithmetic on the challenger's schedule that you would on any facility.

**Exclusivity cuts both ways.** If the challenger offers you an exclusive territory, ask what it asks in return, and read your incumbent's agreement for any clause that stops you carrying a second brand. For how Competition Act section 3(4) treats exclusive arrangements, we read it for the [franchise versus dealership guide](/blog/solar-franchise-vs-dealership/), which covers the text and its limits. Our reading there is that such terms are not automatically unlawful and not automatically safe, so take legal advice on the actual wording.

If Qbits is one of the brands on your shortlist, the [Qbits partner application](/become-our-partner/) is where that conversation starts. The application opens a review, and this guide describes no Qbits terms. Put each question in writing and rely only on the reply.

## Check 5: What service process will you be running?

A second brand multiplies the number of service routes your dealership operates. Each one has its own form, evidence list, courier rule and escalation contact, and your staff must learn all of them.

Do not compare warranty terms on the basis of a headline number. Ask each brand for its written terms for the exact model and compare them yourself with your accountant and lawyer. This guide states no warranty term for any brand.

What you can ask for now is the process. Our guide to [how dealers handle inverter service claims](/blog/dealer-inverter-service-claim-process/) has a table of fourteen items to request in writing before you sign, from the RMA form to the escalation role. Add two lines that matter more for a challenger:

- **Turnaround as a written commitment per stage,** not "as soon as possible". Mark an unanswered line "not provided" and treat it as unknown.
- **Spares held in India.** Which spares are held in the country and which are shipped on request, and who pays freight both ways.

If the challenger will not put its process in writing, ask your incumbent the same questions. Then compare what each will sign. Run one dummy case from intake to closure before the pilot's first sale.

## Check 6: Will a second brand cannibalise your first?

A second brand competes with your first, even if you never mention one to the other. Test these pressure points before you commit.

**Your incumbent's agreement.** Read it for:

- an exclusivity or "primary supplier" clause;
- purchase targets that count towards a rebate or a credit limit;
- brand-use rules that stop you showing two brands together;
- a termination right if you carry a competing line.

**The rebate effect.** Volume rebates can make a modest shift in purchases expensive. Here is an illustration with assumed numbers.

*Illustrative, all figures assumed.* Your incumbent pays a 1.5% rebate on all purchases in a quarter once they pass Rs 50,00,000. You buy Rs 60,00,000 and earn Rs 90,000 (1.5% of Rs 60,00,000). You move 20% of that volume, Rs 12,00,000, to the challenger. Your incumbent purchases fall to Rs 48,00,000, below the threshold, so the rebate is lost. Rs 90,000 is 7.5% of the Rs 12,00,000 you moved. The challenger must therefore give you 7.5 points of extra net margin on that volume before you are better off than before. Your own agreement will have different thresholds, but run the same arithmetic.

**Price discipline across brands.** Decide before the first quote how you will price the two brands against each other. Keep the same quote margin floor on both, so that your sales team does not default to the one that is easier to sell. Do not use one brand as a loss leader for the other without deciding why.

**Positioning against the incumbent.** Position the challenger by class and buyer, not by price. A gap class is a clean reason to offer it. Offering it as "the cheaper one" in classes where the incumbent already wins trains your installers to ask for a discount on the incumbent.

**Bundling and installer loyalty.** Avoid bundling the challenger into an incumbent deal as a sweetener. It teaches the customer to value neither. For installers, find out which brand they have been trained on, because a technician who knows one brand's commissioning menu will make errors on another until trained. Tell your installers what the pilot is and how long it runs, so a failed pilot does not look like abandonment of the brand they bought.

## Check 7: How do you run a 90-day pilot with exit criteria?

A pilot is a small, time-bound and measurable first order. It has a cap on exposure, a short list of classes, checkpoints, and exit criteria written before the first dispatch.

**Design the pilot.**

1. **Set an exposure cap.** Exposure is stock bought, plus invoices unpaid, plus service costs not yet recovered. State it as a rupee figure.
2. **Choose two or three classes,** preferably from your "gap" or "weak spot" rows.
3. **Choose six to ten installers** who agree to try it, with a note of what you will do if a unit fails.
4. **Write the exit criteria** and give them to your own team. Share the dates with the brand.
5. **Record every enquiry, quote, order and service case** in the same log you use for your incumbent.

*Illustrative, all figures assumed.* Your inverter stock at cost is Rs 40,00,000. You cap pilot exposure at 10% of that, which is Rs 4,00,000. You target sell-through of 60% of pilot stock at cost within the 90 days.

**Use checkpoints.**

| Day | What must be true | If not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | Written commercial schedule, service process and certificate set in hand; dummy service case run | Do not place the first order |
| 30 | Orders dispatched as written; DISCOM position for pilot models confirmed in writing; first installer feedback | Pause orders until the gap is closed |
| 60 | At least one real or simulated service case closed within the written stages; sell-through on track | Extend only with a written fix and a date |
| 90 | Scorecard complete; red lines checked | Decide: proceed, extend 60 days once, or stop |

**Use a scorecard, and add red lines.** The scorecard turns a mixed experience into a number. The red lines stop a high score from hiding a serious problem.

*Illustrative scorecard, all weights and scores assumed.* Score each line from 0 to 5 at day 90. Weighted points are weight multiplied by score divided by 5.

| Criterion | Weight | Score (0 to 5) | Weighted points |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Documents and registrations match the exact models sold | 20 | 4 | 16 |
| DISCOM acceptance confirmed in writing for the models sold | 15 | 4 | 12 |
| Written commercial schedule received and followed | 15 | 3 | 9 |
| Service stages met against written commitments | 20 | 3 | 12 |
| Sell-through of pilot stock | 15 | 2 | 6 |
| Installer feedback and repeat orders | 10 | 4 | 8 |
| No price conflict between your brands | 5 | 4 | 4 |
| **Total** | **100** | | **67** |

Set the decision bands before the pilot starts. In this illustration: 75 or more is "proceed", 60 to 74 is "extend once, with named fixes", and below 60 is "stop". A score of 67 sits in the middle. The weak lines are sell-through (6 of 15) and service stages (12 of 20), so the extension would target those two and no others. Change the weights to suit your business, but fix them before the first dispatch, not after you see the result.

**Red lines end the pilot at any score:**

- a certificate or registration that cannot be matched to a delivered model;
- a DISCOM refusal of a pilot model that the brand had said would be accepted;
- the written terms not delivered by day 30, or changed after orders;
- a payment or credit-note obligation by the brand that is missed beyond the period you set;
- a service case that stops with no named escalation reply.

## Check 8: Which risks go into a register?

Keep a one-page register for the challenger, reviewed at each checkpoint.

| Risk | What to check | What you can do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Brand continuity | What happens to open claims, spares and warranty duties if the brand exits India or changes its entity | Ask for a written continuity clause; do not hold deep stock |
| Import duty or QCO change | Whether the models depend on a duty position or an interim evidence route; the regulations guide records that the above-200 kW route is time-limited | Keep a "stop-stock" trigger and a named person who rechecks the order |
| DISCOM list change | Whether your DISCOM revises its accepted-equipment document during the pilot | Record the document date; reconfirm before each new order |
| Unpaid credit exposure | Brand credit given to you, and customer credit you give | Keep both inside the exposure cap; review the payment clock, not only the days |
| Slow-moving stock | A model revision that leaves old stock behind | Ask what happens to stock when a model changes |

## Check 9: Is the brand what it says it is?

Due diligence on the brand comes before pilot time, not after. Send the same request to every brand.

1. **Company registration.** Ask for the legal name, registration number and registered address, then match them against the public company records on the Ministry of Corporate Affairs portal. We could not open the MCA pages when we wrote this guide, so check what each page shows rather than relying on our description.
2. **GST registration.** Ask for the GSTIN and check the name and address against the taxpayer search on the GST portal. Again, we could not open the GST pages to describe the fields, so check them yourself. Match the entity on the quote, the agreement and the invoice.
3. **Address and premises.** Visit the registered or dispatch address if you can. Check it is the address on your documents.
4. **Product liability.** Ask whether the brand holds product liability cover and ask for the certificate. Do not accept a verbal answer.
5. **Reference checks.** Speak to three dealers you choose yourself, one of whom left the brand. Ask what they ordered, how credit and returns worked in practice, and how a failed unit was handled.
6. **Site visit.** Where the brand has a stock point or service centre, visit and ask to see a repair record, a spares shelf and a test setup.
7. **Litigation and complaints.** Ask your lawyer to search for disputes involving the company, and ask the brand directly about open complaints.

Send every brand the same blank sheet, and compare only what each brand puts in a document.

## Should you proceed, pilot first or walk away?

Use this after checks 1 to 6 and again after the pilot.

| Signal | Proceed | Pilot first | Walk away |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Demand | A class you lose repeatedly, with enough log rows | A plausible gap, but a thin sample | Only overlap or stock-out reasons |
| Evidence | Documents match exact models and registrations check out | Some documents pending, with dates | Certificates missing or do not match the model |
| DISCOM | Written acceptance for the models you would sell | Acceptance expected, to be confirmed by day 30 | Refusal, or "ask your installer" |
| Terms | A dated written schedule that answers the question list | Most lines answered, with named gaps | Verbal only, or lines changing on each call |
| Service | Stages, spares and escalation in writing | Process described, numbers pending | "Standard process" and nothing else |
| Incumbent | No clause or rebate cost, or a cost you have priced | A cost you can absorb in a capped pilot | A termination clause or a rebate loss you cannot cover |
| Brand | Registration, GST and references check out | One loose end being closed | Entity or address does not match, or references declined |

If the signals sit across two columns, take the more cautious one.
