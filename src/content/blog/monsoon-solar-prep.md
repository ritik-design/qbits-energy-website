June arrives with a familiar rhythm in India: the humidity climbs, the sky thickens, and within days the southwest monsoon announces itself along the Kerala coast before marching north. For the roughly 10 million Indian homes now running on solar, this seasonal shift carries a specific engineering implication, the same rain that breaks summer heat also brings cloud cover, lightning, driving water, and weeks of reduced irradiance that can mask genuine faults as ordinary weather.

The homeowner who prepared nothing may not notice a problem until the electricity bill in September looks wrong. The homeowner who worked through a structured pre-monsoon checklist arrives at October with a verified, clean system and a monitoring baseline that separates cloud-cover dips from actual equipment failures.

> **Direct answer.** Monsoon solar prep for Indian homes covers 10 priority tasks: panel inspection for micro-cracks and soiling, inverter IP rating verification, earthing continuity check, MC4 connector waterproofing, junction box seal inspection, string voltage measurement, SPD testing, monitoring alert configuration, tree trimming, and mounting frame structural check. Systems with IP66 inverters and verified earthing require the least corrective work; IP21/IP44 inverters installed in semi-open locations are the highest-risk items. The Qbits 10-Point Monsoon Readiness Protocol below organises these tasks in priority order.

> **TL;DR**
> - Four hazards concentrate during June–September: lightning (2,500+ deaths/year nationally), driving rain into IP21/IP44 enclosures, sustained cloud cover that masks genuine faults as weather, and 60–80 km/h wind gusts on mounting frames.
> - IP66 is the recommended inverter rating for exposed monsoon installations; IP21/IP44 units must sit in a fully enclosed, waterproof cabinet.

Each task in this guide is written for an existing solar system owner who wants to perform a methodical annual review, not for someone still choosing equipment. Where a task requires a licensed electrician, that is noted explicitly. Where a visual or basic-instrument check is sufficient, the steps are spelled out so you can complete them yourself on a clear morning before the first rains hit your city.

## Why Monsoon Is the Most Dangerous Season for Indian Solar Systems

The Indian solar industry commonly focuses marketing attention on summer, peak irradiance, high generation, fastest payback. Monsoon is treated as a nuisance rather than a risk event. That framing is technically wrong.

Four distinct hazards concentrate between June and September that do not exist in the same combination during any other season:

- **Lightning**: the monsoon brings India's highest lightning strike frequency. The [Indian Meteorological Department](https://mausam.imd.gov.in/) records approximately 2,500 lightning deaths per year, with strike frequency peaking during June–September. A direct or nearby strike can induce thousands of volts of transient surge through DC strings and the grid connection simultaneously, destroying inverter power stages, MPPT circuits, and monitoring hardware in milliseconds.
- **Driving rain and water ingress**: sustained monsoon rain creates conditions that standard IP65 equipment handles safely, but IP21/IP44 inverters (designed for indoor use) can allow water to enter terminal blocks, trigger corrosion, and cause ground faults over weeks rather than hours.
- **Sustained cloud cover**: four to eight weeks of below-average irradiance means fault-related generation drops are easily attributed to weather. A string that lost a panel to micro-cracking in April may show no alarming generation dip until November, by which time the crack has propagated further.
- **Structural wind loading**: monsoon storms can bring gusts of 60–80 km/h across peninsular India. Mounting frames that developed corrosion or loose fasteners during summer heat expansion may fail under lateral wind load.

> **2,500+** lightning deaths per year in India, with the highest frequency during the June–September monsoon window. Ground-based and elevated structures (including rooftop solar installations) are statistically affected by nearby strikes even when not directly hit, through induced transients. *Source - [India Meteorological Department, Annual Report](https://mausam.imd.gov.in/), 2025.*

The good news is that all four hazards are manageable with a single annual pre-monsoon review. The [solar inverter maintenance](/blog/inverter-maintenance-india/) guide published on this site covers the broader annual maintenance calendar; this article focuses specifically on the pre-June window and the monsoon-specific risk items.

## The Qbits 10-Point Monsoon Readiness Protocol

### The Framework

The ten tasks below are sequenced by consequence of failure, the item with the highest safety or financial impact if skipped comes first. A homeowner can complete tasks 1, 4, 5, 9, and 10 without professional equipment. Tasks 2, 3, 6, 7, and 8 require either a multimeter and basic electrical familiarity or a licensed electrician, depending on your comfort level.

1. **Inverter IP rating and location audit**: Confirms the first and most consequential variable: whether your inverter is physically rated for its installation environment.
2. **Earthing and grounding continuity**: Verifies the safety net that absorbs lightning-induced transients before they reach sensitive electronics.
3. **Surge protection device (SPD) inspection**: Confirms the sacrificial component that absorbs transients is still functional and has not already been consumed by a prior event.
4. **MC4 connector inspection**: Visual check for cracking, partial mating, and brand mismatches that break the IP67 seal.
5. **Junction box seal inspection**: Physical check of the panel-side termination enclosures for cracked gaskets and standing water entry points.
6. **String voltage measurement**: Baseline Voc reading per string before monsoon cloud cover makes generation data unreliable.
7. **Panel inspection for micro-cracks and soiling**: Visual and ground-level performance comparison to catch degraded panels before cloud cover masks the signature.
8. **Monitoring alert configuration**: Setting generation floor alerts so cloud-cover dips and genuine faults produce different notification signatures.
9. **Tree trimming and shading audit**: Eliminates the single most avoidable cause of monsoon generation loss.
10. **Mounting frame structural check**: Confirms the racking will hold under monsoon wind loads.

Each task is expanded below with the specific action to take, the tools needed, and the pass/fail criterion.

## Task 1: Inverter IP Rating and Location Audit

The IP (Ingress Protection) rating printed on your inverter's nameplate is the single most important monsoon readiness variable for homeowners with outdoor or semi-outdoor installations. The number tells you exactly how much water exposure the inverter can survive.

| IP Code | Dust Protection | Water Protection | Monsoon Suitability |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **IP21** | No protection | Vertically dripping water only | ✗ Must be in sealed room |
| **IP44** | Protected against 1mm+ solids | Water splashing from any direction | ✗ Not adequate for driving rain |
| **IP65** | Fully dust-tight | Water jets from any direction | ✓ Adequate in covered location |
| **IP66** | Fully dust-tight | Powerful water jets, heavy rain | ✓ Suitable for fully exposed outdoor mounting |
| **IP67** | Fully dust-tight | Immersion to 1 m for 30 min | ✓ Exceeds monsoon requirements |

Locate the nameplate on your inverter (usually a metal or printed label on the housing) and read the IP code. Then compare it against the actual installation environment:

- **Fully enclosed plant room or indoor location**: IP44 or above is adequate.
- **Covered outdoor area (balcony soffit, shed roof, verandah ceiling)**: IP65 minimum, IP66 recommended.
- **Exposed outdoor wall or rooftop enclosure**: IP66 mandatory.

If you find an IP21 or IP44 inverter mounted in a location that receives driving rain or is within a metre of an opening to the outside, this is a priority-one corrective action before monsoon, not after. Water ingress into terminal blocks creates slow corrosion paths that cause ground faults weeks after the event, and the failure signature (intermittent earth fault alarms, gradually rising leakage current) is easily misread. Relocating an inverter or adding a weather enclosure is installation work best handled by an established residential solar EPC rather than attempted as a DIY fix.

Qbits on-grid (TLS, TLD series) and hybrid (HS, HT series) inverters carry IP66 ratings as standard, which is why they can be mounted on exposed exterior walls without a separate weather enclosure. The [IP66 climate survival guide](/blog/ip65-vs-ip66-solar-inverters-weather-protection-guide/) details the specific IEC 60529 test conditions behind that rating if you want to understand what "powerful water jets" means in engineering terms.

## Task 2: Earthing and Grounding Continuity Verification

[Earthing](/glossary/earthing/) in a solar system serves two distinct functions. Equipment earthing bonds the metallic enclosures (mounting frames, inverter chassis, junction box lids) to a common earth electrode so that if a live conductor accidentally contacts any metal part, fault current flows to earth rather than through a person. DC functional earthing (used in some transformer-based systems) keeps the DC bus at a defined potential relative to earth to reduce EMI and protect against common-mode ground faults.

Both must be intact before monsoon, because lightning-induced transients look for the lowest-impedance path to earth. If that path is a broken bond or a corroded connection, the transient routes through circuit boards instead.

| Earthing Point | What to Check | Pass Criterion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Earth electrode (pit or plate) | Visual inspection of electrode and riser cable | No corrosion, cable fully intact to surface |
| Mounting frame bonds | Continuity test between any two frame members | < 1 Ω between any two frame points |
| Inverter chassis bond | Continuity test from chassis lug to earth bar | < 1 Ω |
| Junction box enclosures | Continuity test from enclosure to frame | < 1 Ω |
| Earth bar connections | All lugs tight, no oxidation on copper | No visible corrosion, all bolts finger-tight + quarter turn |

A standard multimeter in continuity mode is sufficient for the frame and chassis checks. Set the probes between two points; if the resistance reads above 1 Ω or the continuity beeper does not sound, trace the bond back to find the broken or corroded link.

The earth electrode test is more involved, it requires an earth resistance tester (also called a Megger or earth tester) to measure actual electrode resistance. For residential systems, the electrode resistance should not exceed 10 Ω per IS 3043; ideally below 5 Ω. The [IEC 60364-5-54](https://www.iec.ch/) standard defines acceptable earthing conductor sizes and electrode configurations for low-voltage electrical installations, the same framework Indian standards reference. If you do not own an earth tester, this is a task for your EPC or a licensed electrician, the underlying electrical and CEIG drawings prepared at commissioning are the reference document a technician should be checking the as-built earthing layout against. The [solar inverter grounding](/blog/solar-inverter-grounding/) article covers the IS 3043 requirements in detail, including the difference between rod electrodes, plate electrodes, and grid electrodes for different soil conditions.

> Monsoon is actually the best time to test earth resistance, wet soil has lower resistivity, so a reading during June/July represents a favourable condition. If resistance is high even when the soil is wet, it will be significantly worse in the dry season.

## Task 3: Surge Protection Device (SPD) Testing

A [surge protection device](/glossary/spd/) on a solar system typically sits in two locations: on the DC side (between the string combiner or inverter DC terminals and earth) and on the AC side (between the grid connection breaker and the inverter AC terminals). Some quality inverters include SPD ports internally; others require external Type 2 SPD modules wired into the distribution board.

The SPD absorbs transient overvoltages (the brief but extreme voltage spikes induced by nearby lightning strikes or grid switching events) by conducting the surge energy to earth through a metal oxide varistor (MOV). The problem is that each surge event partially degrades the MOV. An SPD that has absorbed two or three significant surges over its lifetime may appear intact externally while offering near-zero protection.

The pre-monsoon SPD check takes three minutes:

- **Visual status window**: Most Type 2 SPD modules include a green/red status indicator. Green means the MOV is functional; red means it has been consumed and the module must be replaced before monsoon.
- **Physical integrity**: Check the DIN rail clip is fully seated and the module is not cracked or discoloured.
- **Wiring continuity**: Confirm the protective earth wire from the SPD is intact and the lug is tight. A disconnected earth wire means the SPD has nowhere to divert surge energy.
- **Replacement schedule**: Even without a status indicator trip, MOV-based SPDs should be replaced every five years or after any known significant lightning event in your area.

The [surge protection guide](/blog/how-to-choose-solar-inverters-with-surge-protection/) covers SPD sizing (Type 1, 2, and 3 classifications) and the IEC 61643-11 standards that govern DC-side SPD ratings for solar systems. If your system does not have an SPD at all, installation before monsoon is a priority task, the cost of a Type 2 SPD (₹1,500–₹4,000 depending on voltage rating) is a small fraction of an inverter replacement.

## Task 4: MC4 Connector Inspection and Waterproofing

[MC4 connectors](/glossary/mc4-connector/) are the cable-to-cable connectors that join solar panels to strings and strings to the inverter DC input. They are rated IP67 when properly mated, which means a correctly assembled male-female pair resists water immersion to 1 metre for 30 minutes. In practice, several failure modes break that seal specifically during the monsoon season:

- **UV-induced housing cracking**: MC4 polymer housings degrade under two to three years of direct sun exposure. Hairline cracks on the outer housing admit water during monsoon even when the connector appears mated.
- **Partial mating**: An MC4 that has not fully clicked into place (the audible click is the locking collar engaging) has a gap in the sealing gasket. Water tracks along the cable into the connector body.
- **Brand mismatching**: MC4 is a connector format, not a single manufacturer. Male connectors from Brand A mated with female connectors from Brand B often do not achieve the designed IP67 seal because gasket diameters differ by tenths of a millimetre.
- **Open (unmated) connectors**: Unused string inputs on the inverter DC side may have open MC4 connectors. These must have properly rated dust caps seated and locked before monsoon.

Walk each string from the panels to the inverter and physically handle each connector pair. Squeeze the housing; if the plastic has softened or shows white stress marks, plan replacement. Attempt to pull the pair apart with moderate hand pressure; a properly locked MC4 should not release without a tool.

## Task 5: Junction Box Seal Inspection

The [junction box](/glossary/junction-box/) on the back of each solar panel houses the bypass diodes and the soldered connections between cell strings and the output cables. It is factory-sealed with a silicone gasket, but that gasket ages. After three to five years in India's thermal cycling environment (surface temperatures swinging from 25 °C overnight to 70 °C+ on summer afternoons) the silicone can shrink and pull away from the housing lip, leaving a gap.

Water that enters a junction box creates a low-resistance path between cell connections, causing bypass diodes to conduct when they should not and creating partial shading signatures in the generation data. In extreme cases, water contact with a live DC connection causes a ground fault that the inverter detects as a Residual Current Device (RCD) trip.

To inspect junction boxes, work early morning when panels are cool enough to handle safely (or wait for a cloudy day). Inspect the gasket line around the lid on each panel, it should be continuous with no visible gaps, lifting edges, or silicone shrinkage. Press lightly on the lid; it should not flex or show play against the mounting screws. If the lid screws are visible, confirm they are fully tightened.

If you find a damaged junction box seal, the correct response is professional repair or panel replacement, do not apply RTV silicone over the existing gasket as a field fix, because the adhesion to aged silicone is unreliable and may fail within one monsoon season.

## Task 6: String Voltage Measurement Before Cloud Cover Sets In

String voltage measurement is a baseline task best completed in May or early June before sustained cloud cover reduces morning irradiance below the threshold where measurements are meaningful. The purpose is to record the open-circuit voltage (Voc) of each string while conditions are stable, so that any fault developing during monsoon shows up as a deviation from the baseline rather than being attributed to cloud cover.

The measurement requires a DC-rated multimeter (capable of at least 600 V DC) and access to the inverter DC input terminals or a combiner box. Always work with the inverter DC disconnect open and follow safe isolation procedure: open the AC breaker first, then the DC disconnect, wait 5 minutes for DC bus capacitors to discharge.

| Measurement | Formula | Example (3-panel string, 400 Wp) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| String Voc (measured) | Sum of panel Voc × number of panels | 3 × 41.5 V = 124.5 V |
| Temperature-corrected Voc | Panel Voc × (1 + Voc temp coeff × ΔT) | At 35 °C ambient: ~120.8 V |
| Acceptable range | ±5% of corrected Voc | 114.8 V – 126.8 V |

If a measured string Voc falls more than 5% below the corrected target, one or more panels in the string has a degraded or open-circuit cell connection. Record this string for priority investigation when weather clears.

This ties directly into the [inverter MPPT](/blog/inverter-mppt/) tracking function, a string with reduced Voc operates at a different MPPT operating point, and the inverter may partially compensate, masking the problem in energy output data until the discrepancy is significant. The [solar inverter troubleshooting](/blog/solar-inverter-troubleshooting/) guide provides a fuller diagnostic tree for string voltage anomalies.

## Task 7: Panel Inspection for Micro-Cracks, Soiling, and Delamination

Panel inspection before monsoon addresses two different problems at the same time: clearing pre-monsoon soiling that rain will not fully remove, and identifying physical panel damage that cloud cover will mask in generation data.

### Pre-Monsoon Soiling

May in most of India is the peak soiling month, low wind, high temperature, maximum bird activity, and pollen season. A panel surface in late May carries layers of baked mineral deposits, bird droppings (which etch the anti-reflective coating if left long enough), and dust cemented by morning condensation cycles. Rain in June washes loose dust but does not dissolve mineral or organic deposits.

Clean panels before monsoon with:
- Soft brush (no abrasives) or microfibre cloth
- Distilled or RO water, hard water leaves calcium deposits that worsen soiling over time
- Work early morning when panels are cool to avoid thermal shock and water spotting

### Physical Damage Identification

Micro-cracks in solar cells are invisible to the naked eye but produce characteristic performance signatures. A field inspection without electroluminescence (EL) imaging equipment relies on:

- **Visual scan from ground level**: Delamination appears as bubbling or yellowing between the cell layer and the back sheet. Snail trails (brown/black lines following cell cracks) become visible when backlit by low-angle morning sun.
- **Thermal hot spots**: On a clear morning, a significantly warmer panel compared to its neighbours (felt by hovering a hand 5–10 cm above the surface without touching) indicates a failing cell generating heat instead of current.
- **String current comparison**: If your inverter provides per-string current data, compare string currents against each other on a clear morning. A string with a cracked panel shows lower Isc relative to peers.

> **15–25%.** The typical yield loss from a single cell micro-crack that has progressed to a partial open-circuit condition within a module, per [NREL field testing data](https://www.nrel.gov/) on PID and mechanical degradation in field-deployed modules. The loss is masked during cloud-cover periods because all strings underperform, making the cracked panel indistinguishable from weather effects until irradiance returns.

## Task 8: Monitoring Alert Configuration for Monsoon Generation Patterns

This task requires no physical work on the system, only a configuration change in your inverter monitoring platform. It is also the task most homeowners skip, which is why genuine faults hide behind monsoon weather for months.

The goal is to set a generation floor alert calibrated to monsoon irradiance levels rather than summer levels. A system that alerts you when daily generation falls below 22 units will trigger every day in July (even when the system is performing perfectly) because 22 units is the summer baseline. The same system performing correctly might generate only 10 units on a heavily overcast July day.

### Setting Up Monsoon-Calibrated Alerts

The [WhatsApp solar monitoring](/blog/whatsapp-solar-monitoring/) integration available on Qbits inverters allows configuring generation alerts per day-type (clear, partly cloudy, overcast). If your monitoring system supports irradiance-proportional alerts, use that feature. If it supports only absolute thresholds, set two separate alerts:

1. **Zero-generation alert (fault indicator)**: Trigger if generation is zero after 9:00 AM local time on any day. This catches inverter trips, grid disconnection, and total communication loss regardless of weather.
2. **Low-generation alert (monsoon calibrated)**: Set to 30–40% of your summer baseline daily generation. For a 5 kW system generating 22 units in May, set the monsoon alert at 7–9 units. A reading below this floor during monsoon is a fault signal, not a cloud event.

The [solar inverter monitoring systems](/blog/solar-inverter-monitoring-systems-in-india/) guide covers the specific alert configuration menus for major inverter brands operating in India. The principle of separating weather-baseline alerts from fault alerts is consistent across platforms.

| Alert Type | Summer Threshold | Monsoon Threshold | What It Catches |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Zero generation | > 0 units by 9 AM | > 0 units by 9 AM | Inverter trip, grid disconnect |
| Low generation (fault) | < 22 units/day | < 8 units/day | String fault, panel failure, shading fault |
| High deviation (string fault) | > 15% below fleet avg | > 15% below fleet avg | Single string degradation |

## Task 9: Tree Trimming and Shading Audit

Tree trimming delivers the highest financial return per hour of pre-monsoon effort for systems with trees in the vicinity, and the monsoon window is when the problem worsens fastest. Deciduous and semi-deciduous trees common on Indian residential plots (peepal, mango, neem, gulmohar) develop their maximum canopy between May and October, driven by monsoon rainfall. A branch that cast a 20-minute morning shadow in January may cast a 3-hour shadow in August.

The economic impact is disproportionate to what most homeowners expect. A single panel partially shaded by a branch for 3 hours on 120 monsoon days loses approximately 360 panel-hours of generation. In a standard string inverter topology, that one shaded panel pulls down the output of every panel in the same string, a single bad actor affecting perhaps 30% of total system output for those 360 hours. [IRENA's India solar resource data](https://www.irena.org/) confirms that monsoon irradiance across peninsular India drops 50–65% below the April–May peak, compressing the effective generation window for any shading-affected system even further.

Before trimming, conduct a shading analysis:

- Photograph each panel array from ground level at 7 AM, 10 AM, 1 PM, 4 PM, and 6 PM on a clear day in May before leaf growth peaks.
- Mark on the photos which panels receive shadow, from which direction, and at what time.
- Identify the specific branches causing the shadow, these are the trimming candidates.

Engage a tree trimming contractor with the photos and mark the branches that need clearance. Request a minimum 2-metre clearance between any branch tip (at full leaf growth) and the nearest panel edge. Allow for one monsoon season of growth on top of the current canopy extent.

The [shading analysis](/glossary/shading-analysis/) glossary entry explains how shade on a crystalline silicon string inverter system propagates through the string, including the bypass diode mechanism that limits (but does not eliminate) the shade impact in modern panels. The [mounting structure](/glossary/mounting-structure/) entries also cover options for re-tilting or re-orienting panels in cases where tree shading is persistent and trimming is not an option.

## Task 10: Mounting Frame Structural Check

Mounting frames are frequently inspected for corrosion but rarely checked for fastener torque and connection integrity, the two variables most likely to cause wind-load failure during a monsoon storm.

### What to Inspect

Work systematically from the roof surface upward:

- **Roof penetrations and flashings**: Each penetration point (where a lag bolt or through-bolt anchors the racking rail to the roof structure) should have an intact waterproof flashing or sealant. Lifted or cracked flashings allow water to track down into the roof deck.
- **Rail-to-post connections**: Shake each rail post by hand. Any rocking indicates a loose clamp or a stripped anchor. Tighten to the manufacturer's torque specification, typically 20–30 Nm for M8 bolts, 35–45 Nm for M10 bolts.
- **Mid-clamps and end-clamps**: The clamps that hold panels to the rails can loosen through thermal cycling. Check that each panel cannot be lifted off its clamps by hand. End clamps are the highest-failure-rate point because they see full cantilever load in wind.
- **Corrosion on galvanised or anodised surfaces**: Surface corrosion on galvanised steel appears as white powder (zinc oxide); this is acceptable. Deep rust exposing bare steel requires corrosion treatment and reassessment of structural integrity. Anodised aluminium frames rarely corrode but can develop galvanic corrosion where in contact with non-aluminium fasteners, look for white powdery residue around steel bolts in aluminium rails.

| Component | Check Action | Pass Criterion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Roof penetration | Inspect flashing seal | No lifting, cracking, or visible gap |
| Rail-to-post bolt | Torque wrench or hex key check | No movement when hand-loaded |
| Mid-clamp | Attempt to lift panel | Panel does not move from clamp |
| Frame member | Visual corrosion inspection | No exposed bare steel |
| Fastener material | Check for galvanic pairs | No white or orange powder at contact points |

A frame that passes this check will handle typical monsoon gusts (60–80 km/h) without issue. A frame that fails on any criterion should be corrected before the first monsoon storm, not scheduled for the post-monsoon season. For larger arrays, the wind-load calculations behind the original rail spacing and anchor spec typically come from a structural and civil engineering review done at design time, that document is worth pulling out when deciding whether an observed issue is cosmetic or structural.

## How to Prioritise If You Cannot Do Everything at Once

Most homeowners can block half a day for the physical checks and an hour for the monitoring configuration. If time or access limitations mean you must triage, use this priority order:

**Pros of addressing all 10 tasks before first monsoon rain**
- ✓ Full protection against all four monsoon hazard categories (lightning, water ingress, shading, structural)
- ✓ Monitoring alerts calibrated before cloud cover begins, so fault signatures are clean from day one
- ✓ Warranty compliance, most inverter warranties require earthing and SPD to be maintained; failing to do so may void the warranty claim on lightning damage

**Cons of deferring any task**
- ✗ Lightning damage (Tasks 2 and 3 deferred) is typically not covered under inverter warranty if earthing or SPD is absent
- ✗ Cloud cover (Task 8 deferred) means genuine faults will be discovered in September or October instead of July
- ✗ Shading (Task 9 deferred) causes generation losses that cannot be recovered retroactively
- ✗ Water ingress (Tasks 1, 4, 5 deferred) causes slow corrosion that does not produce immediate visible failure

If you can complete only five tasks, do Tasks 1, 2, 3, 8, and 9 in that order. IP rating verification and earthing/SPD check address the catastrophic failure modes; monitoring configuration and tree trimming address the financial loss modes.

## Common Mistakes Homeowners Make Before Monsoon

Even homeowners who intend to prepare thoroughly make predictable errors during the pre-monsoon rush:

- **Cleaning panels on a hot afternoon**: Thermal shock from cold water on a 70 °C panel surface can crack the glass and the anti-reflective coating. Always clean early morning.
- **Assuming the EPC already did the earthing check at commissioning**: Commissioning checks earthing at installation. Earthing degrades over time through corrosion and soil movement. Annual verification is the homeowner's responsibility.
- **Replacing the SPD status indicator without replacing the module**: Some SPD modules have replaceable indicator windows. Replacing only the indicator leaves an exhausted MOV inside the module, with a false green indicator signalling false safety.
- **Using WD-40 on MC4 connectors**: WD-40 is a water displacer, not a connector lubricant, and it attacks the rubber gasket material over time, reducing IP rating. Use only connectors and lubricants rated for the MC4 housing polymer.
- **Closing the inverter DC disconnect immediately after a storm**: Wait 30 minutes after a nearby lightning strike before re-energising the system. Allow transient voltages to dissipate and give the SPD time to reset to its steady-state condition.

The [12-year solar inverter warranty](/blog/12-year-solar-inverter-warranty/) article notes that warranty claims for storm damage are routinely examined for evidence of proper SPD installation and earthing compliance, two of the most commonly cited grounds for warranty denial on surge-damaged units.
