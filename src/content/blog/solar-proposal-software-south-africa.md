They are buying the certainty that the lights stay on, and the financial return is the justification rather than the motive. A document that reports rands saved and says nothing about which circuits survive an outage is answering the second question and ignoring the first. Layer on a 10 kVA residential connection cap, mandatory SSEG approval before energizing, and a Section 12B deduction that transforms commercial economics, and the local requirements diverge sharply from the global default. ## What A South African Proposal Has To Get Right

**Backup capability, quantified.** Not "battery backup included" but which loads stay energised and for how long under a realistic outage. That is a dispatch calculation against a load profile, and it is the answer to the customer's actual question.

**The capacity cap as a design constraint.** With residential single-phase around 10 kVA, a high-consumption household cannot cover its load from the array alone. The proposal has to explain which loads are covered and why, rather than implying full independence.

**Section 12B at the customer's tax rate.** A 125% first-year deduction with no size limit is powerful, but it is a deduction, so the cash effect depends on the buyer's rate. Showing it at a generic rate either overstates or understates the return. SurgePV's [South Africa compliance library](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/south-africa) covers the wider regulatory picture.

**SSEG sequence and timing.** Pre-application notification before installation, written approval before energizing. Setting that expectation up front removes the most common source of post-sale friction. See SurgePV's [Eskom SSEG registration guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/south-africa/guides/eskom-sseg-registration).

**The 25 kVA sign-off threshold.** Above 25 kVA an ECSA Professional Engineer is required, per the October 2025 update covered in the [DoL versus ECSA sign-off guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/south-africa/guides/dol-vs-ecsa-sign-off). That is a real cost and it belongs in the quoted price.

## Selling Resilience Without Overselling It

> **What most South African quotes get wrong.** They either ignore backup entirely and quote a payback, or they promise "backup power" without saying what that means in practice. Both fail. The first ignores the buying motive. The second sets an expectation the system will not meet on a cloudy winter evening, and the customer finds out during an outage.

The honest version is specific: these circuits, this many hours, under these conditions, with this much degradation over the battery's life. That requires modelling the battery against the actual load rather than quoting nameplate capacity. A 10 kWh battery does not deliver 10 kWh of useful backup, and a customer who discovers the difference during load-shedding will not treat it as a technical nuance.

There is a commercial upside to precision here. A proposal that says "essential circuits for approximately six hours, or the full house for roughly two" is more useful and more credible than one promising backup in the abstract, and it opens the conversation about upsizing. Our [solar plus storage design software](/blog/solar-plus-storage-design-software/) comparison covers the dispatch modelling this needs.

For commercial buyers the calculation shifts. Three-phase capacity headroom, daytime-aligned industrial load, and Section 12B combine to make C&I the strongest segment in the market. The proposal there leads with the deduction and the avoided-import figure, with resilience as the supporting argument rather than the headline.

## Worked Example: 100 kVA Commercial Rooftop, Section 12B Effect

An illustrative calculation, not a specific customer project. Inputs: 100 kVA three-phase, 120 kWp array, 1,750 kWh per kWp specific yield, 210,000 kWh generation, 85% self-consumed, import at R2.40 per kWh commercial, installed cost R1,800,000, corporate tax rate 27%.

| Line | Without Section 12B | With Section 12B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Installed cost | R1,800,000 | R1,800,000 |
| First-year deduction at 125% | R0 | R2,250,000 |
| Tax saved at 27% | R0 | R607,500 |
| Effective net cost | R1,800,000 | R1,192,500 |
| Annual avoided import (178,500 kWh at R2.40) | R428,400 | R428,400 |
| Simple payback | 4.2 years | 2.8 years |

Section 12B removes roughly a third of the effective capital cost and pulls payback down by nearly a year and a half. Proposals that mention it as a footnote rather than modelling it at the buyer's rate are leaving the strongest argument in the document unmade. Note the deduction exceeds the asset cost, which is what the 125% rate means in practice.
