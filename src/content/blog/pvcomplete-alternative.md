## Why Teams Look for a PVComplete Alternative

### The Enact Solar acquisition

Enact Solar announced its acquisition of PVComplete on 13 January 2026. PVComplete's chief executive and chief technology officer moved to Enact in the same roles, and financial terms were not disclosed. The stated plan is a combined platform covering design, proposals, asset management, and monitoring.

Read that factually. Leadership moving across in the same roles usually signals the acquirer wanted the team and the technology, not just a customer list, and plenty of acquisitions in this market have gone well for customers. The products are actively sold and supported today.

What genuinely changes is who decides what gets built. An acquired product's roadmap follows the acquirer's strategy. Capabilities that strengthen the combined platform tend to get funded, and capabilities that only mattered to standalone customers may not. That is how integrations generally work, not a prediction about PVComplete specifically.

So the honest advice is narrow. Do not treat the acquisition as a reason to rip the product out. Treat it as a reason to ask Enact, in writing, what the development and support commitment is for each of the four PVComplete products over the next 24 months, before committing to a multi-year term. A clear answer you are comfortable with removes this as a factor. Our [PVComplete review](/blog/pvcomplete-review/) goes deeper on the product itself.

### The CAD competence requirement

This is the older and, for most teams, the bigger reason. PVCAD is a plugin, and a plugin inherits its host application's learning curve. AutoCAD is not a tool a solar salesperson picks up between site visits.

That creates three costs: the Autodesk licence, a four-figure annual line item per seat if you do not already own it; training time for designers who have never drafted; and a hiring constraint, because a posting requiring AutoCAD fluency draws a smaller pool.

If your engineering team already lives in AutoCAD, none of that applies. If it does not, you are buying an engineering-first suite to solve a workflow you will never fully use.

### The other reasons

**Desktop workflow.** PVCAD work happens on a local AutoCAD installation, so collaboration and version control follow your file management rather than the design tool.

**Product stacking.** The suite is four products: PVSketch and PVSketch Mega in the browser, PVCAD and PVCAD Mega in AutoCAD. Sellers in one, engineers in another, utility work in a third. Priced per seat, that adds up.

**Pricing opacity.** PVComplete's own site was unreachable when we checked, and we could not verify a current public price list. Third-party ranges exist but disagree with each other. For a buyer building a budget, that is friction.

## What to Evaluate in a Replacement

Five questions decide this, and they are worth answering before you book any demos.

**What is your actual deliverable?** A stamped permit drawing set and a signed sales proposal are different products. Tools optimise for one or the other.

**Do you need a native CAD file or an exported one?** Native means the design was born in AutoCAD and an engineer edits it directly. Exported means a DXF or DWG lands in your drafting team's inbox and they finish it. The second is fine when your standards are light and painful when they are elaborate.

**Who is doing the design?** If sellers produce first-pass layouts, a browser tool wins on adoption alone. If a small engineering team does everything, tool complexity matters less than output quality.

**Does financing name a tool?** If your loan documents or independent engineer specify PVsyst, that is settled.

**What is the all-in annual cost?** Count every product, every seat, and the Autodesk licence if you do not already own it.

## Six PVComplete Alternatives Compared

### 1. SurgePV, the all-in-one pick

**Best for:** Teams that want design and customer-facing proposals from one project record, with a CAD file available downstream but not required day to day.

SurgePV runs $1,299 to $1,899 per user per year depending on seat count, and it leads this list because it matches the most common requirement rather than an edge case. Layout, module-level shading, financial modelling, bill of materials, single-line diagrams, and white-label proposals live in one licence and one project. Sellers and engineers work from the same record instead of two artefacts that quietly drift apart.

For anyone weighing PVComplete, the [DXF and DWG export](https://surgepv.com/solar-autocad-integration) matters more than any other feature. Your drafting team still gets a real CAD file to finish and own, but nobody on the design side needs an Autodesk seat or AutoCAD fluency. The [8,760-hour shading engine](https://surgepv.com/shadow-analysis) is included on every paid plan rather than gated on a tier.

**What you give up:** Working natively inside AutoCAD. If your drawing standards are elaborate, the export is a head start rather than a finished set. SurgePV is also newer, so the installed base is smaller.

### 2. HelioScope, for C&I engineering

**Best for:** Commercial and industrial engineering teams who want defensible yield reporting and layout flexibility, and who already have a separate proposal process.

If what pulled you toward PVComplete was commercial layout capability and credible energy modelling rather than the CAD file specifically, HelioScope is the closer comparison and the shorter learning curve. Strong reputation among engineers, mature hourly simulation, and it handles messy commercial rooftops well.

**What you give up:** HelioScope is not positioned as a permit drawing tool, so most teams keep a CAD step downstream, and it is not positioned as a proposal platform either, so quoting usually happens somewhere else. The interface is engineer-first, which in our experience slows non-technical sales staff down. Confirm current scope with the vendor. Our [HelioScope alternative guide](/blog/helioscope-alternative/) covers the trade-offs.

### 3. PVcase, for terrain-aware ground mount

**Best for:** Utility-scale developers and ground-mount EPCs where slope, cut and fill, and tracker geometry are the hard part of the job.

PVcase is the other serious AutoCAD-native product in solar, which makes it the closest like-for-like alternative to PVCAD Mega. Terrain is core rather than an add-on, and it shows in how the tool handles real topography instead of assuming a flat plane.

**What you give up:** Nothing on the CAD dependency, because PVcase keeps it. If your reason for leaving PVComplete was the AutoCAD requirement, this is not your answer. It is aimed at ground mount, so it is a weak fit for a rooftop-heavy portfolio, and it is not positioned as a customer-facing proposal platform, so plan for separate quoting. See the [PVcase alternative comparison](/blog/pvcase-alternative/).

### 4. Aurora Solar, for US residential

**Best for:** Residential sales organisations in the United States where the bottleneck is closing the customer, not drafting the plan.

If you arrived at PVComplete by searching for solar design software generally and your business is residential, you were in the wrong aisle. Aurora is built for remote roof assessment and the sales conversation. A clean 6 kW gable roof does not need an AutoCAD environment.

**What you give up:** Cost, mainly. Aurora does not publish a full public price list, and third-party reports place it toward the upper end of the category with several capabilities on higher tiers, so get a written quote rather than budgeting from those figures. Its code and tariff coverage is built around the United States. The [Aurora Solar alternative guide](/blog/aurora-solar-alternative/) has the full picture.

### 5. PVsyst, for bankable simulation

**Best for:** Financed projects where a lender or independent engineer names PVsyst in the documents.

PVsyst is not a design platform and does not pretend to be. It is the simulation output the finance side accepts, which makes it a requirement rather than a choice on financed work. Treat it as a line item alongside your design tool.

**What you give up:** Everything either side of the simulation. PVsyst is positioned as a simulation environment rather than a proposal, drawing, or sales tool, and the desktop interface is built for modelling rigour rather than turnaround speed. Our [PVsyst alternative guide](/blog/pvsyst-alternative/) explains where the requirement genuinely binds.

### 6. OpenSolar, the free option

**Best for:** Small residential installers where fixed software cost has to stay near zero.

OpenSolar has a genuinely free entry tier and a reasonable design experience for straightforward residential roofs. For a two-person installer doing simple systems, it removes the software line item entirely.

**What you give up:** OpenSolar publishes a free core with paid add-ons, so price your likely add-on mix rather than assuming the effective cost stays at zero as volume grows. In our assessment the simulation is not aimed at PVsyst-class hourly modelling, commercial shading is not its strongest case, and it is not positioned as a permit drawing tool. Confirm all three against a real project. The [OpenSolar alternative comparison](/blog/opensolar-alternative/) covers where the model stops working.

## The Honest Caveat About Leaving PVComplete

There is one thing on this list that no alternative properly replaces, and pretending otherwise would waste your time.

PVCAD produces the drawing inside AutoCAD. Not an export, not a PDF, not a starting point. The file your engineer finishes in is the file the design was born in, with your layer structure, your blocks, and your title block conventions intact. Every alternative here except PVcase produces a file that somebody then opens and finishes.

For a team whose deliverable is a stamped permit set, that difference is not a feature preference. It removes an entire step rather than speeding one up. Across a few hundred projects a year, one eliminated redraw is a bigger number than any licence on this page.

So the fair test is specific. If you currently pay a drafter to rebuild designs from another tool, quantify that time before you switch, because you may be about to reintroduce a cost you had already removed. If you have no redraw step, because your permit sets are standardised templates or somebody else stamps them, the CAD-native advantage is something you pay for and do not use.

## Verdict

PVComplete is a good product with a clear point of view, and the acquisition does not change that. It changes your diligence, not your assessment of the software. Ask Enact what the commitment is for each product, get it in writing, and if the answer holds up then the ownership change should not decide anything.

What should decide it is the CAD question, which predates January 2026 entirely. If your engineers live in AutoCAD and your product is a stamped drawing set, the native workflow is worth the licence, the training, and the desktop constraint. No browser tool on this list matches it.

For everyone else, and that is the larger group, the requirement is design plus proposals from one project record with a CAD file available when drafting needs one. SurgePV covers that at $1,299 to $1,899 per user per year without an Autodesk dependency, which is why it leads this list. The other five each win a narrower case, and the table above is the fastest way to find yours.

- Time your current path from site data to approved permit set, including any CAD redraw, so you know what the native workflow is genuinely saving you.
- Ask Enact in writing for the support and development commitment on each PVComplete product before renewing on a multi-year term.
