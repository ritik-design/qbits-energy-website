SolarEdge Designer is the free design tool most installers meet the same way: someone on the team was already fitting SolarEdge inverters, the tool was available at no cost, and it turned out to be much better than free software has any right to be. That is a genuinely good outcome for a lot of businesses. It is also a tool with a clear purpose that a buyer should understand before making it the centre of their workflow. This review covers what Designer is, why it costs nothing, what it does well enough to compete with paid platforms, where its scope ends, and which alternatives make sense depending on how you buy hardware.

**Short version.** SolarEdge Designer is a capable, genuinely free solar design tool built by a manufacturer to design its own systems. For installers standing behind SolarEdge hardware, it is one of the best value propositions in the market. For installers who choose inverters project by project, it is a good second tool rather than a platform, because vendor-neutral comparison is not what it was built to do.

Free software from a hardware vendor invites suspicion, and it should not. SolarEdge is not hiding the arrangement. The tool is on their site, under their brand, described as free with no licence fee, and it designs their systems. That is an honest trade the buyer can evaluate. The question is not whether the incentive exists. It obviously does. The question is whether your business is shaped in a way that makes the trade a good one.

## What SolarEdge Designer Actually Is

Designer is a browser-based platform for planning, building, and validating residential and commercial PV systems using SolarEdge inverters and power optimisers. You enter a site, model the roof, place modules, let the tool work out the electrical design, run an energy simulation, and produce customer-facing output.

The workflow follows the shape of a real installation job. Site imagery comes in first. SolarEdge provides HD satellite imagery with AI-assisted 3D modelling and roof detection, so the roof surface, ridges, and obstructions come together faster than manual tracing. Modelling includes multi-selection, copy and paste, and edge alignment, which sound minor until you have traced forty dormers by hand.

Then the electrical layer. This is where a manufacturer's tool has an inherent advantage. Designer calculates the electrical design automatically, including stringing and DC cabling, and for commercial systems it offers an auto-string layout that applies SolarEdge's own design rules at one click. A third-party tool has to encode those rules from a datasheet. SolarEdge wrote them.

Finally the output layer: energy simulation with shading analysis and additional system losses, financial analysis and ROI forecasting for the customer, and integration with AutoCAD, PVsyst, and the SolarEdge Monitoring Platform.

## Pricing, and What Actually Pays for It

Designer is free. SolarEdge states there are no licence or subscription fees and that automatic upgrades are included. There is no seat pricing, no add-on module, and no annual negotiation.

What funds it is hardware. SolarEdge sells inverters and power optimisers, and Designer is built and maintained inside that hardware business rather than sold as a standalone software product. That is a completely ordinary arrangement, and versions of it exist across the industry, from manufacturer configurators to free sizing tools.

The reason to say this plainly rather than treat it as an exposé is that the model is worth understanding as a buyer. A subscription platform earns its revenue from renewals across a mixed customer base; a manufacturer-funded tool sits inside a hardware business. Neither is dishonest, and both are ordinary. They are simply different structures, and in our view it is sensible to read a product roadmap in the light of the structure it sits in.

## What SolarEdge Designer Genuinely Does Well

**It costs nothing, and that is a real feature.** A two-person installer paying nothing for design software is not making a compromise for the sake of budget. They are getting roof modelling, simulation, and proposal output at a price that lets them compete with a much larger firm. Dismissing free as inferior is a habit of people who sell paid tools. On residential SolarEdge work, the gap between Designer and a subscription platform is narrower than the price difference suggests.

**Stringing and optimiser design are its strongest ground.** Power optimisers change the string sizing rules, and getting that wrong is one of the more common design errors on SolarEdge jobs. Designer applies SolarEdge's own rules automatically, validates the design instantly, and maps optimiser barcodes in bulk. When a manufacturer builds the tool for its own topology, this is the part that benefits most.

**Shading and layout are solid for the scope.** The energy simulation includes shading analysis and system losses, and because optimiser behaviour under partial shade is modelled by the company that makes the optimisers, the results on cut-up roofs are credible. AI roof detection and tree shading support have made the modelling stage faster than it used to be.

**Customer output is good enough to sell with.** Financial analysis and ROI forecasting produce something a homeowner can read. Layouts, system specs, and production estimates export into structured reports without heavy reformatting. For a residential quote, that is the job done.

**It does not trap the design.** Export to AutoCAD via DXF, export to PVsyst, and a hand-off into the SolarEdge Monitoring Platform mean the work you do in Designer can travel. A tool that let you in and never let the geometry out would be a much harder recommendation.

## Where the Scope Ends

None of what follows is a defect. It is the boundary of what the tool was built to do, and a buyer needs to see the boundary clearly.

**Hardware neutrality.** Designer is built and maintained by a hardware manufacturer, so its component libraries and electrical logic are naturally centred on SolarEdge inverters and power optimisers. If your next project is going out with a different inverter brand, this is not where that engineering work is meant to happen. The practical effect is that Designer is unlikely to serve as your single system of record unless close to every project is SolarEdge. Confirm the current scope of supported hardware with SolarEdge before you plan around it.

**Multi-vendor comparison.** A common installer workflow is to model the same roof with two or three inverter options and show the customer, or the finance team, what each costs and yields. Side-by-side comparison across manufacturers is not what a single-manufacturer tool is positioned to deliver. If comparison is part of how you sell, you need a platform where every manufacturer is a first-class option.

**Bankable reporting.** For a report an investor or lender will underwrite, most teams still produce the study in whatever tool the financing party names, and PVsyst is frequently that tool. The PVsyst export path in Designer exists partly because of this. Treat Designer's simulation as design-grade and quoting-grade, and confirm separately what your financing partner will accept. Our [PVsyst review](/blog/pvsyst-review/) covers where that requirement actually binds.

**Breadth of tariff and compliance libraries.** Financial analysis depends on local electricity tariffs, and compliance depends on local codes. Platforms whose entire business is software invest heavily in keeping wide multi-market libraries current, because that is what customers renew for. Verify the depth of coverage for your specific market and utility before you rely on the ROI figure in front of a customer. If you sell across borders, the [country-by-country design software guides](/blog/solar-design-software/) are a useful cross-check.

> **What most buyers get wrong.** They treat "free" as the whole analysis and stop there. The real question is what share of your pipeline is committed to SolarEdge hardware. At 90 percent, Designer is close to unbeatable. At 40 percent, you are running two tools and modelling every project twice, and the free one is costing you time.

## Who Should Use It

Use SolarEdge Designer as your main design tool if most of these describe you.

- You have standardised on SolarEdge inverters and power optimisers, and you intend to keep that standard.
- Your work is mainly residential and small commercial rooftops.
- Your crews are already trained on SolarEdge commissioning and monitoring, so the hand-off into the Monitoring Platform saves real steps.
- Your customer deliverable is a quote and a production estimate rather than a stamped engineering package.
- Software budget matters, and the money is better spent on stock, vans, or a salesperson.

For that installer, arguing that they should pay four figures a year for a vendor-neutral platform would be advice against their own interest. The tool is aligned with how they already buy.

## Who Should Not

- **Installers who specify across manufacturers.** If the inverter brand is a project-level decision, your design tool has to be brand-agnostic or you will do the work twice.
- **Anyone selling comparison as part of the pitch.** Showing a customer two hardware options side by side is not what a single-manufacturer tool is positioned to deliver.
- **Teams whose output is permit-grade electrical documentation.** Confirm exactly which documents Designer produces for your jurisdiction before you plan a workflow around it. The [permit design software guide](/blog/solar-permit-design-software/) covers what those packages usually require.
- **Large C&I and utility-scale engineering teams.** Designer does handle commercial work, including auto-string layout, but heavy ground-mount and utility layouts are better served by tools built for that scale. See the [commercial design software comparison](/blog/commercial-solar-design-software/).
- **Teams that need lender-accepted yield reports as standard output.** That is a separate deliverable in almost every stack, free or paid.

## Verdict

SolarEdge Designer is a good tool, and the price makes it a very easy recommendation for the installer it was designed for. AI-assisted roof modelling, automatic stringing that follows the manufacturer's own rules, shading inside the simulation, customer-ready financial output, and clean export paths would be a respectable paid product. Delivered free, it is a strong offer.

The thing to be clear-eyed about is not a flaw, it is a fact of the product: Designer exists to make specifying SolarEdge hardware easy. That alignment is precisely why the stringing logic is so good, and it is also why the tool is not built for comparing manufacturers or for holding a mixed-brand portfolio. Both statements are true at once, and a buyer needs both.

So the decision comes down to one number: what percentage of the systems you will sell in the next two years will carry SolarEdge inverters. If the answer is most of them, use Designer, keep the money, and spend it somewhere with better returns. If the answer is uncertain, or if hardware choice is something you sell on, a vendor-neutral platform like SurgePV is the sounder foundation, and Designer becomes a useful second tool for the SolarEdge jobs rather than the place all your work lives.

Three things to do next:

- Count what share of your last fifty installs used SolarEdge inverters. That ratio decides this more than any feature list.
- Take one recent job and check whether Designer's financial output matches your local tariff and your actual quoted savings.
- If you already run two tools, add up the hours spent modelling the same roof twice, then compare that to the cost of one platform that holds everything.
