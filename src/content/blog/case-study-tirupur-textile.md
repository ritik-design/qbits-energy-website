Monthly consumption: approximately **37,000–40,000 kWh**, consistent with the TANGEDCO bills showing 38,500 kWh on average over the preceding quarter. At a blended TANGEDCO commercial tariff (HT LT-3 category) of ₹8.0 per unit - inclusive of energy charges, demand charges prorated per unit, and fuel adjustment charges - the mill was spending **₹3.08 Lakh per month**, or approximately **₹37 Lakh per year**, on electricity alone.

## Step 2 - System Sizing: Why 100 kW and Not 80 kW or 150 kW

Sizing a C&I solar system is a constrained optimisation problem. The theoretical ideal is to match peak solar output to the midday load peak. The practical constraints are rooftop area, sanctioned connected load (TANGEDCO limits solar capacity to the connected load for net-metering), and capital budget.

The mill's rooftop survey identified **5,800 square feet (approximately 540 square metres)** of usable, south-facing, shadow-free area on the main production shed - adequate for 250 panels at standard row spacing. Using 400 Wp monocrystalline panels (2.1 m × 1.05 m per panel), this area comfortably accommodates the 100 kWp array with a 0.5-metre maintenance walkway on every third row.

The sizing rationale:

- **Peak load headroom**: The peak AC demand of 85–90 kW during the 10:00–13:00 window means a 100 kW inverter output keeps the plant self-sufficient at noon without any significant export. This maximises direct self-consumption - the highest-value use of every solar unit.
- **Connected load ceiling**: TANGEDCO's sanctioned connected load for this unit was 110 kVA, capping the solar system at 100 kW AC output under TNERC net-metering regulations.
- **Rooftop constraint**: A 150 kW array would have required 375 panels and approximately 810 square metres - 50% more area than was available without costly structural reinforcement of older shed sections.

The 100 kW figure emerged not from a round-number preference but from three independent constraints converging at the same point. This convergence is actually ideal: it means the system will self-consume the vast majority of its output, making the investment less dependent on the TANGEDCO export credit rate.

### Why Two 50 kW Inverters Rather Than One 100 kW Unit

A single 100 kW inverter offers marginally simpler wiring but introduces a single point of failure. If the inverter trips - whether from an internal fault, grid anomaly, or a prolonged voltage excursion - the entire 100 kW generation is lost until the unit is serviced. With two 50 kW units, a fault on one inverter still leaves 50 kW of generation capacity intact, meaning the plant loses roughly half its solar offset rather than all of it.

The Qbits TLD-50 is a three-phase, transformerless [string inverter](/glossary/string-inverter/) with dual MPPT inputs per unit. Each inverter operates its two MPPT channels independently, so a partial shading event on the eastern wing of the roof does not degrade output from the western section. The unit's [THD](/glossary/thd/) at full load is below 2.5% - well within the IEC 61727 limit of 5% and comfortably below the 3% threshold that protects VFD-controlled textile machinery.

### Cabling and Earthing

DC cabling used 4 mm² and 6 mm² UV-resistant twin-core cables, routed in perforated cable trays mounted under the panel frames to minimise thermal exposure. AC cables from each inverter to the main switchboard were sized at 35 mm² aluminium - calculated for a 1% maximum voltage drop at full load current. The earthing system followed CEA (Measures Relating to Safety and Electric Supply) Regulations 2010, with a dedicated earth electrode for each inverter frame and the array structure bonded to the building's main earth bar.

## Step 7 - THD Compliance: Protecting Sensitive Dyeing Equipment

This section addresses the most frequently skipped technical requirement in C&I solar procurement: power-quality compatibility with existing factory equipment.

Textile dyeing involves precision temperature control, pH dosing pumps, and variable-speed drives on rinse agitators. All of these are sensitive to [harmonics](/glossary/harmonics/) on the AC supply. When a solar inverter injects harmonic currents into the plant's internal distribution system, the effects cascade:

- **VFD overheating**: Variable-frequency drives on compressors and pumps are rated for a specific level of background harmonic distortion. Excess harmonics cause additional heating in the drive's input rectifier and DC bus capacitors, shortening service life.
- **Motor insulation stress**: The transformer-winding insulation in dyeing machine motors degrades faster under elevated harmonic exposure, increasing the risk of winding shorts.
- **Nuisance tripping**: Overcurrent relays calibrated for sinusoidal current may trip spuriously when harmonic currents add to the fundamental, causing unexpected shutdowns during production.

The IEC 61727 standard for grid-connected photovoltaic systems limits current THD to 5% at rated output. However, best practice for textile and pharmaceutical manufacturing - both classified as process-sensitive loads - is to specify inverters below 3% THD. The Qbits TLD-50 achieves below 2.5% THD across its full load range, confirmed by third-party test reports.

The [THD in solar inverters guide](/blog/thd-solar-inverter/) provides a complete technical treatment for procurement managers who need to specify THD limits in their EPC tender documents.

| THD level | Risk to textile equipment | Inverter grade |
| --- | --- | --- |
| > 5% | High - nuisance tripping, motor overheating | Sub-standard / unspecified |
| 3–5% | Moderate - marginal for VFD loads | Minimum acceptable (IEC 61727) |
| < 3% | Low - safe for all process-sensitive loads | Industrial grade (recommended) |

The mill's EPC contract included a THD verification clause: the inverter supplier was required to provide test data at 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% load. This clause adds no cost - any credible supplier provides this data - but it prevents substitution of a lower-quality unit during procurement.

## Step 8 - ALMM Compliance and TANGEDCO Net Metering Approval

Net metering is the mechanism that makes the financial model work. Without a bidirectional meter and a TANGEDCO interconnection agreement, the mill would be unable to export surplus solar units during the midday peak - those units would either be wasted (inverter clipping) or the system would need to be curtailed. With [net metering](/glossary/net-metering/), every exported unit earns a credit that offsets future consumption at night.

The Tamil Nadu net-metering process for C&I systems above 10 kW involves:

### Stage 1 - Application to TANGEDCO

The applicant submits a net-metering application with: single-line diagram, inverter and module specifications, ALMM certificate numbers, structural load calculation, and the EPC contractor's CEA licence. The [ALMM list](/glossary/almm-list/) maintained by MNRE is the gating document - if the inverter model is not listed, TANGEDCO rejects the application outright.

Qbits inverters are ALMM Phase III listed. The certificate number appears on the product label and in the MNRE ALMM database, allowing the TANGEDCO officer to verify compliance without additional testing.

### Stage 2 - Technical Inspection

A TANGEDCO engineer visits the site to verify that the installation matches the single-line diagram, that the anti-islanding protection is operational, and that the bidirectional meter can be installed at the existing metering point. This visit typically occurs within 15–20 working days of application submission.

### Stage 3 - Meter Change and Agreement Signing

TANGEDCO replaces the existing meter with a bidirectional net meter and issues the interconnection agreement. From this date, the solar generation data is captured on both the DISCOM billing system and the plant's own monitoring platform.

For the Tirupur mill, the entire process from application to meter energisation took **38 working days** - within the 45-day timeline mandated by TNERC's 2024 net-metering regulations. ## Common Mistakes C&I Buyers Make in Factory Solar Projects

Several avoidable errors repeatedly appear in C&I solar installations at Indian factories. The Tirupur mill's procurement process was designed specifically to avoid them.

- **Skipping the energy audit** - Sizing the system based on sanctioned load rather than actual measured consumption leads to either an oversized system (capital waste) or an undersized one (missed savings). The mill's audit revealed actual consumption was 78% of sanctioned load, which directly shaped the 100 kW decision.
- **Ignoring THD specifications** - Many procurement departments request "a 100 kW solar inverter" without specifying maximum THD. The lowest-cost bidder may supply a unit with 4.5% THD that is technically compliant with IEC 61727 but causes operational problems with dyeing machinery within the first monsoon season.
- **Applying for net metering after installation** - The net-metering application must be submitted before or during construction, not after. A mill that installs without a pending application can wait 6–8 weeks post-commissioning before exporting - losing significant generation credit during that period.
- **Underspecifying the warranty** - A 5-year parts warranty on a 100 kW inverter means the mill bears full replacement cost (₹2.5–4 Lakh per unit) in Years 6–25. The 12-year full replacement warranty standard held by Qbits eliminates this exposure for the critical first decade when most inverter component failures occur.
- **Not mapping panel strings to monitoring channels** - String-level monitoring requires each string to be individually connected to a monitoring channel. If the installation maps multiple strings per channel to save on combiner box cost, fault localisation becomes impossible - the monitoring can see inverter-level underperformance but not isolate the specific string.
