You can put solar on a house with two electricity connections, but one system normally serves one meter. Under ordinary net metering, the units your panels send to the grid are set off only against the connection the system is attached to. They do not reduce the bill of the other meter, even if it is in the same house and the same name.

So there are three ways to do it. You can put solar on one connection only. You can put a separate system on each connection. Or you can ask your DISCOM for group net metering, so one system serves both. Which one works depends on three things: your state's rules, whose name each meter is in, and whether both connections are residential. This post shows what the rules we read say, where they are silent, and what to ask before you pay.

> **What this is built on**
>
> - **National rule:** the 2021 amendment to the Electricity (Rights of Consumers) Rules, notified as G.S.R. 448(E) on 28 June 2021. We read the gazette text on 30 October 2026.
> - **State texts, read on 30 October 2026:** Delhi (DERC group and virtual net metering guidelines, Fifth Amendment dated 15 March 2024), Maharashtra (MERC Regulations 2019 and the Commission's statements of reasons for the 2023 and 2024 amendments), Karnataka (KERC Distributed Solar PV Regulations 2026, dated 19 August 2026), Telangana (TGERC Rooftop Solar PV Regulation 1 of 2025, an OCR copy of the gazette), Gujarat (GERC amendments of 2022, 2024 and 2025) and Rajasthan (RERC Regulations 2021).
> - **Subsidy:** the MNRE guidelines PDF for the PM Surya Ghar residential subsidy, opened on 30 October 2026. A later revision may differ.
> - **Limits:** we read the Maharashtra group metering text in the Commission's statement of reasons, not in the notified regulation. We did not read the Delhi principal guidelines of 2019, or any state's later amendment that we did not name. The Telangana copy is noisy, so we quote only what was clear. Your DISCOM's current order decides your case. Use this post to know what to ask.

## What should you check before choosing an option?

Start with your two bills. Houses end up with two connections in a few common ways. A ground floor and a first floor were wired separately for two brothers. A shop or clinic sits below the family home. A tenant has their own meter. Or a second connection was taken years ago for a [pump or lift motor load](/blog/solar-inverter-for-lift-water-pump-motor-loads/) or a floor extension.

Take your latest bill for each meter and write down five things:

1. **Consumer number** (also called consumer account number or service connection number).
2. **Name on the bill.** Is it the same person on both?
3. **Tariff category.** Domestic (residential) or commercial, or something else.
4. **Sanctioned load** in kW. Each connection has its own.
5. **Where the meter and the main switchboard are.** An inverter has to feed the switchboard of the connection it belongs to.

These five answers decide almost everything below.

## What do the national rules say about two meters?

The national rule ties net metering to a single meter at a single point of supply. The 2021 amendment to the Rights of Consumers Rules defines **net-metering** as exported solar units being deducted from imported units "by using a single bidirectional energy meter for net-metering at the point of supply". It leaves the arrangement itself to each State Commission's regulations.

That wording is why one system maps to one meter.

We read the whole of that amendment. It does not define group net metering, virtual net metering or community solar. Those arrangements come from state regulations and from the PM Surya Ghar guidelines, not from the 2021 text. If you read elsewhere that the 2021 rules created them, the gazette text we read does not support that.

| Connections are in different names or different categories (home and shop) | A or B, not C | Can a tenant or a second owner apply on their own consumer number? ## Option A: can you put solar on one connection only?

Yes. This is the simplest case and the one most installers will quote by default. One system, one bidirectional meter, one application. The other connection keeps its old meter and its old bill.

Three rules from the texts we read shape this option.

**Exports settle on that connection.** The Maharashtra and Rajasthan regulations define "premises" as rooftops or areas "in respect of which a separate meter" has been provided by the DISCOM. Read plainly, each meter is its own premises. Gujarat's 2022 amendment says the consumer must consume the electricity generated at the same premises. Its model agreement says the electricity must be consumed "using same point of supply". So export units from the first-floor system cannot be handed to the ground-floor meter under plain net metering.

**The size is capped by that connection's sanctioned load in most of these states.** Maharashtra (reg. 6.2) and Rajasthan (reg. 7.2) tie the system to the consumer's sanctioned load. Karnataka's 2026 regulation says that for sanctioned loads up to 10 kW, the inverter's AC capacity must not exceed the sanctioned load. Telangana allows up to 100% of sanctioned load for residential consumers. Gujarat's 2022 text for homes says projects are allowed irrespective of sanctioned load, subject to the capacity limits in the regulation. The 2025 amendment we read does not touch that clause, but ask what your DISCOM applies today.

**The inverter must connect at that connection's point of supply.** Rajasthan's regulation says the system must interconnect at the same point as the consumer's premises. In a house, this means the inverter's output goes into the distribution board fed by that meter.

Option A fits when one meter carries the daytime load. An example is a first floor with a work-from-home office and air conditioning, while the ground floor is used only in the evening. If you pick the meter with the lower daytime use, the system exports most of what it makes. Your state decides how those exports are paid. Our guide to [reading a net meter bill](/blog/read-net-meter-bill-after-going-solar/) shows how four states settle surplus units.

## Option B: can each connection have its own system?

Yes, if each has enough sanctioned load. Each connection becomes its own prosumer, meaning a consumer who also generates power. That means its own application, its own bidirectional meter, its own agreement, and its own size limit set by its own sanctioned load.

Practical points to settle before you order:

- **Roof space.** Two systems share one roof. Our [upgrade guide](/blog/upgrade-three-kw-to-five-kw-solar/) cites the MNRE rooftop FAQ at about 10 square metres of shadow-free area per kW, and the real figure varies. A 2 kW and a 3 kW system together need roughly the space of one 5 kW system, before shade.
- **Two inverters.** Each feeds a different distribution board. Do not wire both into one board.
- **Two sets of paperwork.** The [approval timeline guide](/blog/net-meter-approval-timeline/) lists the stages. You do them twice, and the two applications can move at different speeds.
- **Surplus stays separate.** Whatever one system exports can only offset that meter's imports.

Maharashtra's regulation (reg. 6.5) lets an eligible consumer install or enlarge systems at different locations in the same premises, within the capacity limit. That text counts several systems on one meter. It does not say two meters can pool them.

## Option C: can one system serve both meters through group net metering?

Sometimes. Group net metering lets one system serve more than one connection of the same consumer. It is a state-level rule, so whether you can use it depends on where you live.

The texts we read, on 30 October 2026:

| State | What the text says | Who qualifies | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Delhi | Surplus from a system at one or more locations is exported through a net meter and adjusted in "more than one electricity service connection(s) of the same consumer" in the same DISCOM's area. Rooftop systems under group or virtual net metering have no capacity cap | The same consumer. The definition does not mention category | DERC Fifth Amendment Guidelines, 15 March 2024, in force from 1 April 2024 |
| Maharashtra | Surplus is adjusted across more than one connection of the same consumer, at the same or a different premises. Credits follow a priority list and sharing ratio the consumer gives. Surplus not used is carried forward as credit. Wheeling charges and losses are exempt until rooftop capacity in the state reaches 5,000 MW | The same consumer. The Commission's reasons say the participating connections belong to "same entity/proprietor/owner" | MERC First Amendment 2023, statement of reasons dated 16 November 2023 |
| Karnataka | One plant offsets more than one connection of the same name and same category. Minimum 5 kW. Maximum the combined sanctioned load of all connections. Priority list can be revised once a year with two months' notice. The connection where the plant sits must use at least 20% of the generation, and the rest of that share lapses. Surplus beyond all connections is bought at 75% of the generic tariff. Smart meters at the consumer's cost | Same name and same category | KERC DSPV Regulations 2026, regs. 4.4 and 5.4, dated 19 August 2026 |
| Telangana | Group net metering exists for a "parent consumer" and its participating connections of the same category, on mutually agreed terms. Under 100 kWp. Net-metered prosumers cannot also take virtual net metering | Domestic (LT-I) and townships and residential colonies (HT-VI) only | TGERC Regulation 1 of 2025, gazette 15 November 2025 (OCR copy) |
| Gujarat, Rajasthan | We found no group or virtual net metering text in the GERC amendments (2022, 2024, 2025) or the RERC 2021 regulation we read | Not known from our reading | A later amendment may exist. Ask the DISCOM |

Four things stand out for a house with two meters.

**Same name is a real condition.** Karnataka says same name and same category. Maharashtra's reasons say the same owner. Say the ground floor meter is in your father's name and the first floor meter is in yours. The texts we read do not treat that as one consumer. Changing the name on a meter is a DISCOM process with its own paperwork. Ask before you assume.

**Home plus shop usually fails the category test.** Karnataka and Telangana both require the same category. A domestic meter and a commercial meter are different categories.

**A shared plant is not always the lower-risk option.** The Karnataka rules add a 20% minimum for the connection where the plant sits, and a 5 kW floor. A house with a 2 kW and a 3 kW connection reaches exactly 5 kW combined. The 5 kW floor and the combined-load ceiling meet at one number. A 1 kW plus 2 kW house cannot use that route at all.

**Group net metering adds meters and an agreement.** Karnataka requires smart meters at the generation point and at each participating connection, at the consumer's cost. It also requires a group agreement with the DISCOM. Maharashtra's reasons say consumers could terminate an existing connection agreement early to enter a new group agreement.

Virtual net metering is different. It is for several different consumers sharing a plant. Maharashtra's 2024 reasons limit it to residential consumers in a multi-storey building within a housing society. Karnataka's 2026 text lists apartments, housing societies and some institutions. Our [glossary page on virtual net metering](/glossary/virtual-net-metering/) and the guide on [solar for apartment complexes](/blog/solar-for-apartment-complex-rwa-india/) cover that route. A two-meter house owned by one family is a group net metering case, not a virtual net metering one.

## How do names, categories and the roof affect your choice?

**Owner on one meter, tenant on the other.** The tenant is a different consumer. In the texts we read, they cannot be grouped with the owner. The tenant can still apply on their own consumer number for a system on their own connection. The PM Surya Ghar guideline says an eligible consumer is one with a valid consumer account number, so the person named on the bill is the applicant. For the roof, Gujarat's 2022 text asks that the consumer be in legal possession of the premises, or hold it on rent. In practice, a tenant needs the owner's agreement. Our [guide for tenants](/blog/solar-for-rented-homes-tenants-india/) covers that conversation.

**Home and shop.** The PM Surya Ghar guideline says no central subsidy is given to non-residential segments, including commercial. A shop connection is not eligible. The home connection may be. Net metering rules for a commercial meter are the state's own, and we did not read each state's commercial section here.

**Sanctioned load per connection.** Each connection has its own number. In the states that tie size to it, your system on that connection cannot exceed it. If you want more solar than the load allows, ask the DISCOM about a load increase. For applications up to 10 kW, the Maharashtra and Gujarat texts we read say the DISCOM carries out a matching load increase. Check the current rule.

**Dues.** Maharashtra (reg. 6.6) and Telangana (reg. 4.10) say consumers with pending arrears are not eligible for net metering. Telangana also applies this to participating connections. Clear any old dues on both connections before you apply.

**The roof.** Neither the national rule nor the PM Surya Ghar guideline we read mentions a no-objection certificate (NOC) from a landlord or society. A society or a co-owner may still ask for written consent. Get it before the installer orders anything.

## What does PM Surya Ghar say about a house with two meters?

The MNRE guideline PDF for residential subsidy, which we read on 30 October 2026, says the following that matters for a two-meter house:

- **Subsidy is tied to a connection.** The guideline describes the residential plant as a grid-connected system "tagged to a particular residential power connection of the local DISCOM".
- **Group and virtual net metering are covered.** Installations under those arrangements are eligible. They must be on a roof, terrace, balcony or elevated structure, and the DISCOM must approve the metering arrangement.
- **Commercial connections get no subsidy.**
- **The applicant is the consumer.** Anyone with a valid consumer account number applies through the portal.
- **Subsidy is paid once per installation.** The text says a rooftop installation is eligible only once after it is installed.
- **The residential table stops at 3 kWp.** It lists no additional subsidy beyond 3 kWp. As printed, the rate is Rs 30,000 per kWp for the first 2 kWp and Rs 18,000 for the third kWp. Special-category states have different rates.

**What it is silent on.** We found nothing about a house where two connections sit under one roof. It does not say whether each connection can claim on its own. It does not say whether the 3 kWp ceiling applies per connection or per house. And it does not say whether two applications from one address are accepted. We do not state an answer. Ask the portal helpline and the DISCOM in writing before you plan on two subsidies. Do not assume a second subsidy because there is a second meter.

## Worked example: ground floor 2 kW, first floor 3 kW

This example is **illustrative**. The loads and yield are assumed, no tariff is used, and your state's rules decide the result.

**Assumptions**

- One family owns the house. Both meters are in the owner's name and both are domestic.
- Ground floor meter: sanctioned load 2 kW, uses 8 units a day on average.
- First floor meter: sanctioned load 3 kW, uses 12 units a day on average.
- One kW of panels makes 4 units a day on average over the year. This is an assumption, in line with the 3.5 to 4.0 units per kWp per day we assumed in our [5 kW panel-count guide](/blog/how-many-solar-panels-for-five-kw-inverter/). It is not a Qbits or MNRE number.
- The state settles by net units over the billing cycle, and each system is sized at its connection's sanctioned load.

| Option | Solar size | Units made a day | Units a day still bought from the grid | Share of the house's 20 units offset |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A1. First floor only | 3 kW on the first floor meter | 12 | 8 (ground floor) | 60% |
| A2. Ground floor only | 2 kW on the ground floor meter | 8 | 12 (first floor) | 40% |
| B. Separate systems | 2 kW plus 3 kW | 20 | 0 on paper | 100% on paper |
| C. Group net metering, if allowed | One 5 kW plant | 20 | 0 on paper | 100% on paper |

Three readings of the table.

1. **Option A is capped by one meter, not by your roof.** Even with space for 5 kW, a plant on the first floor connection is limited to that connection's 3 kW load. This holds in the states that tie size to load. The ground floor keeps paying for 8 units a day.
2. **B and C look the same on average and differ on bad days.** "100%" is an average. In a monsoon month a 3 kW system makes less than 12 units a day, and the first floor imports the shortfall. In a summer month it makes more, and the extra is surplus. Under B, surplus from one floor cannot help the other floor. Under C, the pooled surplus can, in the order of your priority list.
3. **A locked-up floor shows the difference.** Say the ground floor is empty for a month and uses 2 units a day. Under B, its 2 kW system still makes about 8 units a day. About 6 units a day of surplus pile up on a meter with no use for them, and the state's surplus rule decides their value. Under C, those units go to the first floor first. That is the case group net metering is built for.

**What this example does not tell you.** It does not say C is cheaper than B. Group net metering in the Karnataka text brings smart meters, an agreement, a 20% minimum for the source connection and a 5 kW floor. Where group net metering is not available, B is the realistic way to cover both meters.

## What should you ask before you sign?

Ask the **installer**, in writing:

1. Does your quote cover one connection or both? Which consumer number is each system tied to?
2. For option B, does the price include two applications, two meters' paperwork and two inverters?
3. Is each system sized within its connection's sanctioned load? If not, who applies for a load increase?
4. Which distribution board will each inverter feed?
5. Will you check the roof layout so that two arrays do not shade each other?
6. Who is the DISCOM contact for each application?

Ask the **DISCOM**, and keep the reply:

1. Does my state allow group net metering for a house with two connections? Under which order or regulation?
2. Do both connections need the same name and the same category? What does a name change need?
3. What is the minimum and maximum size, who signs the agreement, and what are the meter costs?
4. How is surplus handled: carried forward, bought at a rate, or lapsed? Does that differ for group net metering?
5. Must the connection where the plant sits use a minimum share of the generation?
6. Can I change the priority list or sharing ratio later? How often?
7. Do pending dues on either connection block approval?
8. Can the second connection get its own system on the same roof?

Ask the **PM Surya Ghar portal or DISCOM**:

1. Is the subsidy paid per consumer number, per roof or per house when two connections share one roof?
2. If I apply for group net metering, which connection do I enter as the applicant?
3. Does the 3 kWp ceiling apply per connection or to the whole house?
4. Does a commercial meter on the same roof change the eligibility of my home connection?

## Which option fits your house? A short decision guide

- **One meter carries most of the load:** start with option A on that meter. Add the second later if the numbers justify it.
- **Both meters carry daytime load, both in your name, both domestic:** ask for group net metering first. If your state has none, use option B.
- **Different names:** plan option A or B, each on its own consumer number. Check roof consent first.
- **Home and shop:** treat them as two separate projects. Only the home connection can look at PM Surya Ghar.
- **Unsure of your state's rules:** do not pay an advance until the DISCOM has told you in writing which arrangement it will approve.
