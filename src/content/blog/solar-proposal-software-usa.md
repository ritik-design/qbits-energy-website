## What US Solar Proposals Have To Get Right In 2026

A US proposal carries more regulatory and financial weight than a proposal in almost any other market. Four things have to be correct or the document is worse than useless.

**Federal ITC treatment.** The Investment Tax Credit sits at a 30% base through 2032, according to the IRS (2026). Residential claims run under Section 25D and are straightforward. Commercial claims run under Section 48E, where the credit can reach 50% by stacking a 10% domestic content bonus and a 10% energy community bonus. Systems above 1 MW AC must satisfy prevailing wage and apprenticeship requirements to earn the full rate rather than the 6% base. SurgePV's [financial modelling engine](https://surgepv.com/generation-financial-tool) applies these as separate toggles, and the full mechanics are covered in SurgePV's [IRA solar tax credit guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/usa/guides/ira-solar-tax-credits).

**Depreciation on commercial deals.** MACRS five-year depreciation, with bonus depreciation where it still applies, changes after-tax IRR substantially on C&I projects. A proposal that shows a commercial buyer only the ITC is understating the return, which sounds harmless until a competitor shows the full picture and wins the deal on arithmetic you left out.

**State incentive layers.** SREC markets operate in New Jersey (SREC-II), Massachusetts (SMART), Illinois (Illinois Shines), Maryland, Pennsylvania, Ohio, and DC. Program caps, block pricing, and adders move several times a year. A maintained library beats a hand-built template the moment you sell across a state line.

**Export compensation reality.** This is where most tools fall short, and it deserves its own section.

## The NEM 3.0 Problem Most Proposal Software Still Ignores

Under California's NEM 3.0, exported energy earns roughly $0.05 to $0.08 per kWh while imported energy costs close to $0.40 per kWh at peak. That is a spread of five to eight times. New York's VDER runs on a formula rather than retail rate. Arizona credits excess energy near $0.09 per kWh. New Jersey still pays close to full retail under NEM Plus, and so the old model survives in pockets.

> **What most US installers get wrong.** Offset percentage is not savings. Under NEM 3.0 a system can hit 100% annual offset and still deliver a fraction of the bill reduction the customer expects, because the exported kWh were sold at a sixth of what the imported kWh cost.

The metric that matters now is self-consumption, which means the share of generated energy the household uses at the moment it is produced. Raising it requires an hourly model, and raising it deliberately requires battery dispatch logic. A proposal tool that runs on annual kWh totals cannot compute it at all.

This creates a specific test you can run on any vendor demo. Ask them to show you the hourly load and generation overlay for a California address with a battery, then ask for the self-consumption percentage. Tools built on an 8,760-hour engine answer in seconds. Tools built on annual offset change the subject. The engineering side of that capability is covered in our [solar plus storage design software](/blog/solar-plus-storage-design-software/) comparison, and SurgePV's [California NEM 3.0 guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/usa/guides/california-nem-3) sets out the rate mechanics.

The commercial consequence is real. Customers who signed on an offset headline and then saw a bill that did not match are the source of a meaningful share of US solar complaints. A proposal that models self-consumption honestly closes at a slightly lower rate and cancels far less.

## Worked Example: 8.4 kW Plus Battery In San Diego

This is an illustrative calculation, not a specific customer project. The inputs are stated so you can check the arithmetic.

Inputs: 8.4 kW DC array, 11.5 kWh battery, south and west roof planes, 1,750 kWh per kW annual specific yield, retail import averaging $0.38 per kWh, NEM 3.0 export credit at $0.06 per kWh, installed cost $31,000 before incentives.

| Line | Offset-led proposal | Self-consumption-led proposal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Annual generation | 14,700 kWh | 14,700 kWh |
| Household consumption | 13,900 kWh | 13,900 kWh |
| Headline claim | 106% offset | 71% self-consumption |
| Energy used on site | Not modelled | 10,437 kWh |
| Energy exported | Not modelled | 4,263 kWh |
| Value of self-consumed energy | Implied at $0.38 | $3,966 |
| Value of exported energy | Implied at $0.38 | $256 |
| Year 1 bill saving presented | $5,586 | $4,222 |
| Federal ITC at 30% | $9,300 | $9,300 |

The offset-led proposal overstates year one savings by about $1,364, roughly 32%. That gap does not show up at signing. It shows up on the first summer bill, and it is the reason the customer calls. Note also that the battery is what lifts self-consumption to 71%; without it the same array would land nearer 45% on these load assumptions.
