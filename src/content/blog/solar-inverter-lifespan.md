> **Quick answers**
>
> - Warranty term, design life and actual service life are three different numbers.
> - Electrolytic capacitors and cooling fans are the recognised wear items.
> - Heat is the main accelerant, which makes placement a lifespan decision.
> - Replace on evidence, not on reaching an assumed age.
> - Budget from a current quote, not a percentage of the original system price.
> - Do not reset a faulting unit repeatedly through the warranty period.

**Short version.** An inverter is the component of a rooftop system most likely to need attention before the panels do. Plan for that rather than assuming a single number, keep it cool and well placed, and make the repair or replace decision on a diagnosis instead of an age threshold.

## Three numbers that get confused

| Term | What it actually is |
| --- | --- |
| Warranty duration | A contractual period with defined remedies and exclusions |
| Design or expected life | An engineering estimate under stated operating conditions, where published |
| Actual service life | How long your specific installed unit stays useful and economic to maintain |
| Module performance warranty | A separate contract covering the panels, not the inverter |

The last row is worth isolating, because it is the most common planning error. Panel performance warranties run considerably longer than inverter product warranties. Applying a module's term to inverter planning produces a budget that assumes no inverter event across the life of the system, which is optimistic.

The [warranty guide](/blog/solar-inverter-warranty/) covers the contract fields in detail. Do not infer guaranteed operation from a headline duration.

## What actually ages an inverter

Understanding the mechanisms explains why placement matters more than most owners expect.

**Electrolytic capacitors** are the component most often named in discussions of inverter ageing. Their degradation is temperature dependent, which is why sustained high internal temperature is treated as the primary accelerant rather than simple elapsed time.

**Cooling fans**, where fitted, are mechanical wear items. They have a finite service life, they draw in dust, and a failed fan removes the cooling the design assumed. A fan that never runs during high output is worth investigating rather than enjoying the quiet.

**Thermal cycling** stresses solder joints and semiconductor packaging. Every day the unit heats under load and cools overnight, and materials with different expansion rates work against each other across thousands of those cycles.

**Grid conditions** matter too. Sustained over voltage or under voltage outside the rated window, and surge events on the distribution network, both impose stress the unit was not designed to absorb indefinitely.

The practical implication is that two identical inverters commissioned the same week can reach very different ages, because one sits shaded and well ventilated while the other bakes on a west wall behind stored material.

## Placement is a lifespan decision

Because heat drives most of the ageing mechanisms, the decisions made at installation have a longer effect than anything done afterwards.

Specified clearances on all sides, a location out of direct afternoon sun, distance from other heat sources, an airflow path that stays clear, and accessible service space are the levers. They cost little at installation and are expensive to correct once the system is commissioned and the wall is finished.

The [overheating guide](/blog/inverter-overheating/) covers how derating works and which heat symptoms warrant a service call. An enclosure rating such as IP66 describes dust and water protection and says nothing about thermal headroom, so a sealed unit in direct sun is not protected against the thing that ages it.

## Signs worth acting on

Distinguish normal behaviour from evidence of decline.

- **Repeated faults that cluster over days** rather than tracking hot weather.
- **Measured performance decline** confirmed against your own monitoring records, not an impression.
- **Temperature events in mild conditions**, particularly in winter.
- **Audible fan failure** or a fan that does not run under load.
- **Parts no longer available**, or the model out of manufacturer support.
- **Any burning smell, discolouration or visible damage**, which is an immediate service call rather than a monitoring exercise.

The [low output guide](/blog/solar-inverter-low-output-causes-india/) helps separate inverter faults from array and grid causes, which matters because a shaded or soiled array is frequently blamed on the inverter.

## Repair or replace

Get a qualified diagnosis first. Replacing an inverter that was tripping because of a grid or string problem leaves you with a new inverter and the same fault.

Once diagnosed, compare on five points.

1. **Written repair scope and the warranty on that repair.** A repair with no warranty on the work is worth less than it appears.
2. **Parts availability and lead time.** Downtime is lost generation, and on an older model parts can be the binding constraint.
3. **Whether the model remains supported**, including firmware and monitoring.
4. **A current replacement quote** for a suitable model, not a remembered price.
5. **Consequential changes.** A replacement with different MPPT limits, voltage windows or communication may force string reconfiguration, new monitoring, mounting changes or a fresh approval.

That last point catches people. An inverter swap is rarely just an inverter swap on a system that has been running for years.

Two errors are worth naming. Do not replace a healthy inverter because it reached an assumed age. And do not keep resetting a faulting unit to get through the day, because it erases the fault history that would have supported a warranty claim and often carries you past the warranty period entirely.

## Keep the records that protect the decision

Most of the information needed to make a good repair or replace decision is generated at commissioning and then lost.

Keep the invoice and commissioning date, the model designation and serial number read from the label, the written warranty that applied at sale, the commissioning test readings, and the single line diagram. Add to that a running record of generation, so that a claim of declining output rests on your own figures rather than a recollection.

When a fault occurs, record the exact event code and timestamps rather than a paraphrase, and keep every support ticket reference. This costs nothing at the time and is the difference between a warranty claim that can be assessed and one that cannot.

## Budgeting realistically

Use a current model specific quote plus the expected installation work. A percentage of your original system price is a planning placeholder, not a market price, and it ages badly.

Include in the estimate the replacement unit, removal and reinstallation labour, any string or protection changes, recommissioning, and any notification or approval the utility requires for an equipment change. On a battery backed system, add the compatibility work, since a different inverter may not support the installed pack in closed loop.

Revisit the figure every few years rather than fixing it once. Specifications and availability move.

It is also worth deciding in advance how you would handle a failure outside warranty during a high generation month. The pressure to accept whatever is available quickly is considerable when the system is down and the weather is good. Knowing roughly what a suitable replacement costs, and which models are compatible with your existing strings and monitoring, converts that into a decision rather than a scramble.
