## Why The Zero Bill Promise Fails

> **What most Spanish quotes get wrong.** They subtract annual surplus credit from annual consumption cost, arrive at approximately zero, and present that as the outcome. The arithmetic is fine. The rules it ignores are not.

Two separate constraints stand between a Spanish household and a zero bill.

The first is the floor. Compensación simplificada credit is capped so that the energy component of the bill cannot go below zero in any month. Surplus beyond that point does not carry forward and is not paid.

The second is what the credit can reach at all. A Spanish bill contains charges beyond the energy component, and the surplus credit does not apply to them. Even a household whose energy component is fully offset every single month still receives an invoice.

The practical consequence is that "we will eliminate your bill" is unachievable, while "we will substantially reduce it, and here is the month-by-month picture" is both achievable and more persuasive to anyone who has read their own invoice.

There is a commercial argument here as well as an ethical one. Once a customer understands the floor, a competitor's zero-bill promise stops looking like a better deal and starts looking like a warning sign. Explaining the rule is a way of disqualifying the quotes you are competing against.

## Twelve Rows, Not One

The single highest-value change most Spanish installers can make to their proposal template is replacing the annual summary with a monthly table.

| What an annual summary shows | What the monthly table reveals |
| --- | --- |
| Generation roughly matches consumption | Winter months still import substantially |
| Surplus fully compensated | Summer months hit the cap and lose surplus |
| One payback figure | Where the system is oversized for this load |
| A tidy conclusion | Which months load shifting or storage would help |

The monthly view is not merely more accurate. It is more useful to the customer and more useful to you, because it converts an abstract sizing argument into a visible pattern. A homeowner looking at three summer rows where surplus exceeds the cap can see for themselves why a smaller array, a shifted load or a battery makes sense. That is a far easier conversation than asserting it.

It also sets expectations for winter. A customer who has seen January in the table before signing does not phone in January.

## Price The Export At Hourly Spot

Spanish surplus is credited at the hourly spot price. Because solar generation is correlated across the country, midday spot prices are depressed at exactly the hours a residential system exports most.

A proposal applying an average annual price to exported units therefore overstates export value, and the error is systematic rather than random. Using an hourly price series gives a lower, defensible figure.

That lower figure is not bad news for the sale. It strengthens two recommendations that genuinely help the customer. Load shifting into the midday window converts low-value export into avoided import at full retail. Storage does the same thing across a longer time gap. Both arguments depend on showing the spread between what surplus earns and what imported energy costs, and average pricing hides that spread almost completely.

## Regional And Municipal Support, Named Not Assumed

Spanish incentives are not national in the way customers often assume.

**IDAE-funded programmes** are administered by the autonomous communities, so availability, terms and application windows differ by region. A proposal should name the specific programme and state whether the customer is confirmed eligible or whether an application is required and pending.

**IBI relief** is municipal. Both the percentage reduction and its duration are set locally, and plenty of municipalities offer nothing at all. Presenting an IBI reduction without confirming the municipality offers one is the most common way a Spanish quote becomes undeliverable after signature.

The reliable rule is to distinguish clearly between confirmed and applied-for support, and to show the customer's position with and without anything that is not yet secured.
