To learn **how to choose a solar inverter for home**, start with the household and site, not a brand ranking. Collect the bill, simultaneous loads, outage needs, grid phase, roof and array information, battery plan, installation conditions, approvals, and service expectations. Then compare exact models against those requirements and keep the evidence.

A model can be well made and still be wrong for a particular design. This checklist turns a vague "best inverter" question into eight checks an installer can answer and a homeowner can audit. It deliberately does not recommend a kW size or a brand. If you already know your home type and want typical size ranges and example models, read [the best solar inverter for home guide, organised by home size](/blog/best-solar-inverter-for-home-india/). Come back here to test the quote you receive.

> [!KEYTAKEAWAYS]
> - Work through eight checks: load and bill, outages, phase, strings and MPPT, battery, site and enclosure, warranty and service scope, and documents and handover.
> - Use at least twelve months of bills. A bill shows energy in units, not the power drawn at one moment, so it cannot size an inverter alone.
> - An ordinary on-grid inverter stops feeding when the grid fails (anti-islanding). Decide on backup before buying, because adding it later usually means a second inverter or a replacement.
> - Do not choose phase by a rule such as "above 5 kW must be three-phase". Check the bill, meter, sanctioned load and the DISCOM's own limits.
> - "ALMM listed inverter" is not a meaningful claim: ALMM lists modules and cells. Ask for the exact model's BIS registration instead.
> - A usable quote names the exact module, inverter and battery models, includes a string calculation, and names who issues the warranty and who services your PIN code.

> **The eight checks**
>
> 1. Load and bill: what the home actually uses
> 2. Outages: grid-only or backup
> 3. Phase and grid connection
> 4. Panels, strings and MPPT
> 5. Battery, if any
> 6. Site and enclosure
> 7. Warranty and service scope
> 8. Documents, commissioning and handover

## Check 1: Load and bill, what does the home actually use?

**Collect recent electricity bills, a load list, simultaneous-use and starting-load information, and plans for future load.** Record what is measured, what comes from a document, and what remains an assumption.

Use one household requirement sheet:

Table: Household requirement sheet to complete before requesting quotes

| Input | Record | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Electricity use | Billed units, billing period and tariff components | Supports energy and financial modelling |
| Simultaneous loads | Appliance rating, measured input where available and likely combinations | Supports inverter output review |
| Starting loads | Exact appliance or motor data and the intended starting combination | Prevents a nameplate-only power check |
| Daytime use | Which loads run between 9 am and 4 pm | Decides how much solar is consumed directly |
| Future change | EV, extra air conditioner, battery, extension or phase change | Makes expansion explicit rather than assumed |

Use at least twelve months of bills if you can. Summer and winter consumption in most Indian homes differ by a wide margin, and a single peak-month bill will oversize the system. A bill shows energy in units, not the power drawn at one moment. A home using 400 units a month may never run more than 4 kW at once, and another using the same 400 units may spike far higher when a pump, a geyser and two air conditioners start together.

Do not size an inverter from labels such as "2 BHK" or "one AC". An air conditioner's running watts, starting surge and inverter-compressor behaviour all differ by model. The [solar inverter for AC guide](/blog/solar-inverter-for-ac/) explains which electrical inputs are needed. Motors in pumps and washing machines need the same treatment.

> [!TIP] Write two short load lists
> A simple discipline helps: write down the three or four loads that must run together on a hot afternoon, and the loads that must keep running during a power cut. Those two lists drive Checks 2 and 5.

## Check 2: Outages, does anything have to run when the grid fails?

**Decide the architecture from the required operating modes.** Ask what should happen with the utility present, during grid loss, after restoration, when solar exceeds load, when a battery reaches a limit, and when communication fails. A category label does not answer those scenarios.

Table: Outage and operating requirements mapped to the architecture question each raises

| Requirement | Architecture question |
| --- | --- |
| Grid-connected savings without backup | Does the approved on-grid design meet array, grid and monitoring requirements? |
| Selected loads during outages | Which hybrid or backup architecture supports the defined circuits and transfer behaviour? |
| Battery added later | Is the exact future battery pairing, electrical range, communication and firmware supported? |
| Weak or fluctuating grid with the grid still present | Does the model's published AC operating range suit the measured site voltage? |
| Export restriction | Does the approved model and control arrangement support the required measurement and commissioning process? |

An ordinary on-grid inverter stops feeding power when the utility supply fails. This anti-islanding behaviour is a safety function that protects people working on the lines, so it is not a defect. The [anti-islanding guide](/blog/anti-islanding-protection-solar-inverters/) explains why. It also means a home with daily power cuts and a plain on-grid inverter will have no solar power exactly when the grid is out.

The [on-grid, hybrid and off-grid decision guide](/blog/on-grid-vs-hybrid-vs-off-grid-decision-guide/) maps the operating choices, and on-grid versus hybrid covers the basic difference. If supply fluctuates while the grid is present, the [weak-grid inverter comparison](/blog/best-solar-inverter-weak-grid/) separates published AC operating ranges from protection settings and actual site measurements.

> [!IMPORTANT] Decide backup before you buy
> Decide now, because the choice is hard to reverse. A grid-tied string inverter has no battery connection. Adding backup later usually means a second inverter or a replacement, with more cabling and approvals. Read the [hybrid inverter range](/hybrid-inverter/) and the [on-grid inverter range](/on-grid-inverter/) to see how model families differ in what they publish.

## Check 3: Phase and grid connection

**The inverter phase must match the property's supply and the DISCOM's process.** Check the electricity bill, meter and approved connection records for single-phase or three-phase supply, sanctioned load and any rule on rooftop capacity.

Do not rely on a rule such as "anything above 5 kW must be three-phase". Different distribution companies set different limits for single-phase connections and for net-metered capacity, and the rules change by order and by state. The quotation should state:

- existing phase and sanctioned load;
- proposed inverter phase and rated AC output;
- expected export arrangement (net metering, gross metering or zero export);
- protection and isolation arrangement; and
- approvals or connection changes that remain pending, and who applies for each.

If a connection upgrade is needed, ask whether its cost and timeline are in the quote or outside it. The single-phase versus three-phase guide, the net metering guide and the [DISCOM approval guide](/blog/discom-approval-solar-inverter-net-metering/) are useful once you have these site facts.

A common error is picking a three-phase inverter for a single-phase home because it looks more capable, or the reverse. The inverter must connect to the supply you actually have.

## Check 4: Panels, strings and MPPT

**Compatibility needs exact model numbers and a string design, not matching brand names or headline kW.** An MPPT (Maximum Power Point Tracker) sets the operating point of a connected string of panels. For each planned string the designer should check:

1. open-circuit voltage at the lowest design temperature, against the inverter's maximum DC voltage (cold mornings push panel voltage up);
2. operating voltage across the expected temperature range, against the MPPT window (hot afternoons pull it down);
3. string current against the per-input limit, including modern high-current modules;
4. the number of strings allowed on each input; and
5. whether strings sharing one MPPT have compatible orientation, tilt and shading.

Ask the installer for a string calculation using the selected module's datasheet. The Qbits [string sizing calculator](/string-sizing-calculator/) lets you cross-check a proposed string against an inverter's published limits. Treat it as a cross-check, not a replacement for the designer's record.

The MPPT count follows the roof, not the budget. A single roof plane with uniform shade suits one input. An east-west split often suits two independent inputs. Dual MPPT does not fix shade from a water tank falling across part of a string, because the shaded modules still pull down their own string. The MPPT explainer, the dual versus single MPPT guide and the DC oversizing guide go deeper.

Model families differ more than their headline kW suggest. In the Qbits range, for example, the QB 1.5 to 4.0 kW TLS family is single-MPPT and the 4/5/6 kW TLD family is dual-MPPT, each with its own voltage window, which is why exact-model documents matter. Use the [datasheet library](/download-datasheets/) and the [datasheet reading guide](/blog/how-to-read-solar-inverter-datasheets/) for whichever brands you compare.

## Check 5: Battery, if any

**Battery energy and inverter power are separate checks.** Inverter output must support the loads that run together, including starting surges. Battery energy depends on the backup loads, how many hours you want, the usable operating window, conversion losses, temperature and reserve.

Use these as starting points, not finished designs:

- required backup power begins with the loads that must run at the same moment during a cut;
- backup energy begins with average backup load multiplied by required hours, then allows for the battery's usable window and system losses; and
- PV sizing is separate: it begins with the energy objective and site yield, then must pass the string checks in Check 4.

For a hybrid system, the battery must be on the inverter maker's supported list, with a matching voltage range, charge and discharge current and communication method. "Compatible" in a quote is not enough. A matching CAN or RS485 label does not prove that two products share the same protocol or approved firmware. The [battery sizing guide](/blog/battery-sizing-hybrid-solar/) and the BMS explainer cover the numbers.

Hybrid does not automatically mean whole-home backup. Ask which circuits are on the backup path, how many watts they can draw together, and what happens when a pump or compressor starts. If the home has no outage problem, a battery is an extra cost and an extra maintenance item. Skip it unless the loads justify it.

## Check 6: Site and enclosure

**Check the exact mounting location against the model's published environmental limits.** Indian homes put inverters on balcony walls, terraces, stairwells and in meter rooms, and each location creates different stress.

Table: Site conditions and what to check against the model's published limits

| Site condition | What to check |
| --- | --- |
| Heat and direct sun | Published operating temperature range, derating behaviour above the stated ambient, shading from direct sun, clearances for airflow |
| Monsoon and driving rain | Ingress rating (IP code), cable gland quality, drip loops, mounting orientation, drainage |
| Coastal air | Enclosure and heatsink finish, salt exposure, whether the unit is sheltered. See the coastal inverter guide |
| Dust | Ingress rating, accessible cooling surfaces, cleaning access |
| Lightning and surges | Earthing quality, surge protective devices on DC and AC sides, cable routing |
| Access | Space to read the display, open the unit and service it safely |

An IP code describes the sealed enclosure as tested, not the finished installation. The IP65 versus IP66 guide explains what each digit tests. A strong enclosure rating cannot rescue a unit mounted in a hot, unventilated cupboard or under a dripping AC outlet. Follow the manual's clearances; overheating faults are common in cramped installations.

Peak efficiency belongs in this stage only as a tie-breaker. It is one laboratory operating point, so read the peak versus weighted efficiency note before using a decimal difference to choose.

## Check 7: Warranty and service scope

**Separate the manufacturer's product warranty from the installer's workmanship, design, application, commissioning and maintenance obligations.** For each promise, identify the responsible party, the covered event, exclusions, evidence the owner must keep, the response process, costs, remedy, escalation and duration.

Ask every seller the same questions:

1. Which legal entity issues the warranty for this sale?
2. When does cover start, and is registration required by a deadline?
3. Is the remedy repair, replacement or an equivalent unit?
4. Who pays for diagnosis, travel, labour, freight, removal and reinstallation?
5. Which installation, earthing, surge or environmental conditions can exclude a claim?
6. Who receives the first ticket for my PIN code, and where does it escalate?

A headline year count answers none of these. Read the [solar inverter warranty guide](/blog/solar-inverter-warranty/) and the installation versus product warranty note before comparing. Check that a locator or directory lists a partner for your district, then ask whether that partner supports the exact model. For Qbits models, the public datasheets describe an expandable warranty but do not state the base term, remedy, registration deadline or exclusions, so request the current written terms for the quoted model. The [authorised service partner directory](/authorized-service-partners/) shows the published partners by state and district.

The installer scope should also state application work, metering, protection, cable and structure specifications, monitoring access, handover documents, owner training and the acceptance test. A purchase of an inverter without a complete installation scope is not a complete home solar decision.

## Check 8: Documents, commissioning and handover

**The quotation should be specific enough that a different model cannot appear on installation day without your approval.** Require these in writing:

1. exact module, inverter and battery model numbers and quantities;
2. array layout and string-to-MPPT allocation;
3. string voltage and current calculations;
4. single-line diagram, protection, isolation and earthing scope;
5. normal-grid and grid-loss operating description;
6. backup circuits and duration assumptions, if relevant;
7. monitoring setup and who owns the account;
8. approval, metering and application responsibilities;
9. warranty documents and service contacts; and
10. substitutions, exclusions, payment stages and change control.

At commissioning, photograph the nameplate and serial number and confirm the installed model matches the contract. If backup was bought, witness a real grid-loss and restoration test. Confirm that the monitoring account belongs to you, live data appears, faults are visible and the connection works where the unit is mounted. Keep the drawings, settings, test records, warranty and invoice together. The [commissioning guide](/blog/solar-inverter-commissioning-in-india/) lists the tests, and the [solar quotation checklist](/blog/solar-quotation-checklist/) helps compare quotes line by line.

## Red flags in an inverter quote

Stop and ask for clarification if you see any of these.

> [!WARNING] Red flags in an inverter quote
> - Only a brand and a kW figure appear, with no exact model code.
> - "Hybrid ready" or "battery compatible" with no named battery.
> - No string calculation, or a string plan that was not prepared with your module.
> - A family brochure offered instead of the datasheet of the quoted variant.
> - Warranty described only as a number of years, with no issuing entity or remedy.
> - A claim that the inverter is "ALMM listed". ALMM covers modules and cells, not inverters (see the approvals section below), so ask for the model's BIS registration instead.
> - No mention of who applies for net metering or what happens if approval is delayed.
> - A demand for payment in full before model and settings are confirmed.

## Common mistakes, in order of cost

**Choosing outage backup after the system is installed.** The architecture is hard to change. Decide it in Check 2.

**Sizing from a bill alone.** The bill gives units, not peak power or roof limits.

**Treating MPPT count as a quality score.** It is a layout tool. Count the roof planes first.

**Reading efficiency to two decimals.** The gap between two models' peak figures is smaller than the effect of shade, heat and a bad string design.

**Ignoring who services the product.** A published directory shows that a route exists. It does not show restoration time.

**Mixing up approvals and product.** Net metering, the subsidy portal and DISCOM acceptance are separate from the inverter's own certificates. Keep a list of which approval is the installer's job.

## Datasheet fields explained for a home buyer

A datasheet is only useful if you know what each line decides. These are the fields that most often matter at home scale.

Table: Home-scale datasheet fields, what each means and how to use it

| Field | What it means | What to do with it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rated AC output (kW) | Power the inverter can feed to the home and grid continuously | Match to the array and the DISCOM's capacity rules, not to your appliance total |
| Max AC apparent power (VA) | Short-term or maximum output | Do not read it as the rating |
| Max DC input voltage | The highest string voltage the inverter tolerates | Compare with cold-morning open-circuit string voltage |
| MPPT voltage window | The range in which the inverter tracks the strings | The strings must stay inside it across heat and cold |
| Start-up voltage | Voltage needed to begin operating | Matters for short strings and weak light |
| Max input current per MPPT | Current limit for each input | Compare with the module's operating and short-circuit current |
| Number of MPPTs and strings per MPPT | How many independent inputs and how many strings share one | Match to roof planes |
| Max efficiency / weighted efficiency | Peak or weighted conversion efficiency | A late tie-breaker; weighted figures say more about a year |
| Ingress rating (IP code) | Dust and water protection of the sealed enclosure | Match to the mounting site; check cable glands |
| Operating temperature range and derating | The ambient range, and where output starts to fall | Compare with your summer and the mounting position |
| Grid voltage and frequency range | The AC window in which the inverter stays connected | Compare with measured site voltage |
| Communication | Wi-Fi, RS485, GPRS, Bluetooth or other routes | Confirm which is standard and which is an extra |
| Standards listed | Safety and grid-interface tests, such as IEC 62109 and IEC 62116 | Ask for the model's certificates; a list is not a certificate |

The [datasheet reading guide](/blog/how-to-read-solar-inverter-datasheets/) shows these fields on a real datasheet. Some fields are printed per model inside a family and some for the whole family, so read the column for the exact model on your quote.

## After installation: what the owner should do

A good installation still needs a few owner habits.

> [!CHECKLIST] Owner habits after installation
> - **Keep the paperwork together.** Invoice, nameplate photograph, serial number, commissioning record, settings, warranty and service contacts in one folder.
> - **Check the monitoring.** Look at the app or portal every few weeks. A sudden fall in output on a sunny day is the earliest sign of a fault, a shaded string or a dirty array.
> - **Keep the unit clear.** Do not stack boxes in front of the heatsink or let plants and debris build up around it.
> - **Know the first call.** The installer for workmanship, the service party for product faults. Write both numbers on the folder.
> - **Schedule a basic inspection.** The inverter maintenance guide lists routine checks, and fault codes can help you describe a problem accurately when you call.

## How subsidy and approvals touch the inverter choice

Many home buyers choose an inverter with the subsidy in mind, so it helps to know what the rules do and do not say about the inverter itself.

- **MNRE's ALMM lists cover modules and cells.** The [Approved List of Models and Manufacturers page](https://mnre.gov.in/en/approved-list-of-models-and-manufacturers-almm/) publishes List-I for solar PV modules and List-II for solar PV cells. An inverter does not appear on those lists, so "ALMM listed inverter" is not a meaningful claim.
- **Inverters have a separate BIS route.** BIS's notified-products list names IS 16221 (Part 2) for PV inverter safety and IS 16169 for islanding prevention in utility-interconnected inverters. Ask for the registration for the exact model; the [BIS notified-products annexure](https://www.bis.gov.in/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/temp_2131689472.pdf) shows the standards. The Indian solar inverter brands guide explains how to look a registration up.
- **The DISCOM decides the connection.** Net metering approval, capacity limits and any inspection are DISCOM steps, whatever inverter you buy. See the [DISCOM approval guide](/blog/discom-approval-solar-inverter-net-metering/) and the net metering guide.
- **Subsidy rules change.** Check the official PM Surya Ghar portal, your state agency and the installer's registration status before paying. Subsidy approval is an official decision, not something an installer can promise. See the [subsidy amount guide](/blog/pm-surya-ghar-subsidy-amount/).
- **GST.** Solar devices attract GST at 5 percent from 22 September 2025 under the revised schedule. For mixed or composite supplies, the treatment can differ, so follow the sourced explanation in the [GST on solar inverters guide](/blog/gst-on-solar-inverters-2026/) and ask for the tax line on the quote.

## Worked example: reading two quotes side by side

Imagine a three-bedroom home with a single-phase supply, occasional short cuts and a two-plane roof. Two installers send quotes. This table shows how the eight checks turn them into a clear comparison. Everything in it is illustrative.

Table: Illustrative side-by-side reading of two inverter quotes against the eight checks

| Check | Quote A | Quote B | Reading |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Model | "5 kW inverter, branded" | Exact model code and datasheet attached | A fails until it names a model |
| Outages | "Hybrid ready" | Plain on-grid, cuts are short | B is consistent with the stated need; A needs a named battery |
| Phase | Not stated | Single-phase, sanctioned load noted | A is open |
| Strings | No calculation | String table using the chosen module, two MPPTs used | A is open |
| Site | Not discussed | Shaded wall, clearances noted | B passes |
| Warranty | "10 years" | Warranty document attached, issuer named | A cannot be compared |
| Service | "Pan-India network" | Named party for the PIN code | A is open |
| Handover | Not described | Commissioning test and monitoring handover listed | A is open |

Quote B is not necessarily cheaper or better. It simply answers the questions. Quote A can be fixed by asking for the missing items in writing. If it cannot be fixed, that tells you something about the seller.

## If you cannot decide about backup

Some homes sit in between: cuts are not daily, but a few have been long. A few options help, none of them a default.

- **Decide on the loads, not the label.** Write down what you would actually want on during a long cut: perhaps a refrigerator, a few fans and lights. A short list makes a small hybrid and a small battery plausible. A long list that includes air conditioners makes backup large and expensive.
- **Ask what it takes to add backup later.** A grid-tied string inverter normally has no battery port, so the honest answer may be "replace or add an inverter". Get that answer in writing.
- **Consider a separate small backup for essentials.** Some homes keep a stand-alone backup device for a refrigerator and lights and use a standard on-grid system for savings. This is a design choice for the installer and the household, not a recommendation.
- **Check the grid history.** Neighbours, the DISCOM's reliability reports and your own log of cuts over a few months are better than a guess.

When in doubt, resolve the backup question first and everything else follows. The [on-grid, hybrid and off-grid decision guide](/blog/on-grid-vs-hybrid-vs-off-grid-decision-guide/) walks through it.
