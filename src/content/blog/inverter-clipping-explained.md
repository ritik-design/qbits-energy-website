For solar EPCs and installation companies across India, understanding inverter clipping and DC oversizing is essential when designing systems with modern high-wattage panels. This FAQ guide addresses 12 critical questions to help you make informed design decisions and confidently explain clipping to clients.

## 1. What Is Inverter Clipping?

Inverter clipping occurs when DC power generated by the array **exceeds the inverter's maximum AC output capacity**. During peak production around solar noon on clear days, the inverter reaches its limit and cannot convert all available DC power. Excess is "clipped" or curtailed, creating a flat-topped power curve rather than the typical bell shape.

**Example:** A 50 kW inverter connected to a 60 kW array can only output 50 kW during optimal conditions, the remaining 10 kW is clipped. In Indian installations, clipping typically occurs only a few hours per year during peak irradiance.

## 2. Why Does Clipping Occur?

Clipping occurs when DC array capacity exceeds the inverter AC rating. Triggers include:

- **Peak irradiance above 1000 W/m²**: common in Rajasthan, Gujarat, Karnataka
- **Cool, clear winter mornings** when panels operate at near-peak efficiency
- **High-wattage panels (650–750W)** packing more power into existing roof footprint

Qbits inverters support up to **100% DC oversizing** (2:1 ratio), providing flexibility for modern panels.

## 3. Is Clipping Harmful?

**No**: when within manufacturer specifications.

Modern inverters have:

- Robust protection mechanisms specifically designed for clipping
- Sophisticated control algorithms that smoothly limit conversion without electrical stress
- Thermal management designed for sustained max-load operation
- Full warranty coverage within published DC oversizing limits

Clipping reduces stress compared to rapid power fluctuations from passing clouds.

## 4. What Is DC Oversizing?

Connecting more DC array capacity than the inverter's AC output:

```
DC:AC Ratio = DC Array Capacity ÷ Inverter AC Rating
```

**Example:** 55 kW DC array on a 50 kW AC inverter = **1.1:1 (110% oversizing)**.

### Why It Makes Sense

Real-world conditions rarely allow arrays to operate at STC ratings:

- Temperature derating: **10–15%** in India's hot climate
- Soiling: **5–10%** between cleanings
- Panel degradation, wiring, mismatch losses

DC oversizing compensates. Inverters peak at **30–50% of rated capacity** and maintain high efficiency from 20–100% load, oversizing keeps them in this sweet spot longer.

## 5. When Should You Design for Clipping?

Intentional clipping through strategic oversizing typically delivers **superior economics**:

- Solar panel prices continue declining; inverter costs are stable
- Adding 10–20% more panel capacity usually costs less than upgrading inverter size
- High-irradiance sites (Rajasthan, Gujarat, Karnataka) suit aggressive oversizing
- C&I rooftops with limited area benefit most from oversizing

## 6. What Is the Optimal Clipping Threshold?

Industry best practice: **1–3% annual clipping losses**.

| Scenario | Trade-off |
| --- | --- |
| 2% clipping loss | 12–15% additional energy from oversizing |
| Net effect | 8–12% LCOE reduction over 25 years |

A system producing **105,000 kWh annually with 2% clipping** delivers far better value than one producing 95,000 kWh with zero clipping.

## 7. How Do You Calculate Clipping Losses?

Use simulation software like **PVsyst** with:

- Detailed irradiance profiles (TMY3 data)
- Panel specs (temperature coefficients, efficiency curves, degradation)
- Inverter specs (AC rating, efficiency curves, max DC input)

The software models hour-by-hour production and identifies clipping periods.

**Worked example:** A 55 kW DC array on a 50 kW inverter in Ahmedabad. Using TMY data: ~**180 hours annually with clipping**, losing **~1,200 kWh**: **about 1.8% of total production**.

## 9. How Does Clipping Show Up in Monitoring?

Clipping appears as a **flat-topped power curve** during peak hours instead of the typical bell shape.

### Distinguishing Clipping from Faults

- **Flat at inverter max during peak irradiance** → normal clipping
- **Flat well below max** or during suboptimal conditions → fault (grid curtailment, thermal derating, shading)

### Alert Configuration

Configure monitoring to recognise expected clipping patterns based on system design and season. Alert thresholds should trigger only when performance deviates from expectations.

## 10. On-Grid vs Hybrid Clipping

### On-Grid

Excess DC is simply curtailed during peaks, lost as a design trade-off.

### Hybrid

Excess can potentially be diverted to **battery charging** rather than clipping entirely.

But hybrid clipping prevention depends on:

- **Battery state of charge**: if full, clipping still occurs
- **Battery charging rate**: if 15 kW excess but battery accepts 10 kW, 5 kW still clips

Economic analysis for hybrid systems is more complex, stored energy value during peak-rate or backup periods can justify more aggressive DC oversizing than pure on-grid.

## 11. How to Explain Clipping to Clients

**The Highway Analogy:**

> The inverter is like a four-lane highway. Sometimes you have six lanes of cars (60 kW of solar) trying to use a four-lane road (50 kW inverter), so some wait. But during the other 23 hours of the day, all four lanes are full instead of mostly empty.

**Visual aid:** show side-by-side power curves, bell vs flat-topped. The flat-topped curve's area (energy) is **larger overall** despite the flat top.

**Economic framing:**

> By investing in 20% more panels instead of a larger inverter, we increase annual energy production by 14% while losing only 2% to clipping. This reduces your cost per kWh by 10% and improves ROI by approximately 15%.
