## Why Teams Look for an Energy Toolbase Alternative

Four reasons come up repeatedly, and only one of them is about the software being weak.

### The annual number lands badly

At $299 per user per month billed annually, a single Individual seat is about $3,588 a year. Three designers on Business at $333 each is close to $12,000 a year before anyone buys a design tool. That is defensible when every proposal is a six-figure commercial storage deal. It is harder to defend when half your pipeline never touches a demand charge.

### You are paying for one layer, then paying again

Energy Toolbase is deliberately economics and proposals. Array layout on the Business tier is third-party PV layout tool integration, not a native 3D engine. So the real cost of the stack is ETB plus a design platform, plus the time keeping the two in agreement on system size, orientation, and production. Every handoff is a place where a number quietly stops matching.

### The pipeline changed shape

Plenty of teams bought Energy Toolbase when their work was mostly commercial storage, then grew a residential arm or expanded outside North America. Its depth is concentrated where its customer base is. Outside the United States you are more often building a custom tariff than selecting one, which is work you were paying a premium to avoid.

### Seat pricing does not match how teams use it

Economics tools have a few genuine power users and a long tail of people who only read the output. Per-seat pricing does not distinguish between them. Count how many people actually build models before you shortlist anything.

## What to Evaluate in an Energy Toolbase Alternative

Shortlists go wrong when people compare feature grids instead of testing the thing their proposals actually turn on. Six checks, in order of how often they decide the outcome.

**1. Does your tariff exist, or do you build it?** Load the exact utility rate your pipeline uses. A maintained rate is a real saving. If you build it by hand, price the hours and the risk of getting a ratchet wrong.

**2. Is battery dispatch simulated or assumed?** There is a large difference between a tool that dispatches a battery against an interval load profile hour by hour and one that applies an annual savings percentage. Only the first survives a finance review. The [solar plus storage design software guide](/blog/solar-plus-storage-design-software/) covers which tools do this properly.

**3. Does it also do design?** This decides whether you buy one licence or two. Be honest about whether your designers would keep their existing tool anyway.

**4. Total annual cost at your real seat count.** Convert every monthly figure to a yearly one and multiply by the seats you need. Monthly pricing flatters expensive products.

**5. Market coverage.** NEC, IEC, AS/NZS, and IS code, plus net metering, feed-in tariffs, and subsidy schemes, matter the moment you sell in more than one country.

**6. Does the output survive interrogation?** A commercial buyer's finance team will ask how the savings number was derived. A single figure does not answer that. The tariff, the load profile, the dispatch, and the bill delta do.

> **What most teams get wrong.** They run the trial on a clean, simple project because it is faster to set up. That tests nothing. Run the trial on your ugliest job: the one with the awkward tariff, the ratchet, the odd roof, and the customer who asks questions. The tool that handles that is the tool to buy.

## Six Energy Toolbase Alternatives Compared

### 1. SurgePV, the one-licence pick

**Best for:** Installers, EPCs, and developers who want design, simulation, tariff modelling, and proposals in a single seat, in one or several markets.

SurgePV is the option we recommend first for teams whose answer to "which half do you need" is "both". It runs $1,899 per user per year on the Individual plan, $1,499 per user per year on the 3-User Team plan, and $1,299 per user per year on the 5-User Team plan. Against $3,588 to $3,996 per user per year for ETB Developer, the annual difference is material, and that is before you account for the separate design licence that ETB's third-party layout integration implies.

What sits in the licence: AI 3D roof modelling from satellite imagery, [8,760-hour module-level shading](https://surgepv.com/shadow-analysis), [financial and tariff modelling](https://surgepv.com/generation-financial-tool), bankable P50, P75, and P90 output, DXF and DWG export for AutoCAD handoff, and [white-label proposals](https://surgepv.com/solar-proposals) with e-signature. Code and tariff coverage spans NEC, IEC, AS/NZS, and IS, alongside net metering, time-of-use, feed-in tariff, and schemes such as PM Surya Ghar.

**The honest trade-off:** on the most complex United States commercial tariffs, Energy Toolbase's dedicated rates team and dispatch simulation are a deeper specialism than any general platform. SurgePV wins on breadth, annual cost, and one tool instead of two, not on out-modelling a rates team on their own ground.

### 2. Aurora Solar, for United States residential

**Best for:** Residential sales teams in the United States who need remote roof assessment and a strong customer-facing close.

Aurora's pricing is publicly reported at roughly $159 to $259 per user per month at the time of writing; confirm current terms with the vendor. That is about $1,908 to $3,108 per user per year. It solves the other half of the problem: roof capture, layout, and the sales conversation, rather than tariff depth. If your pipeline moved from commercial storage to homeowners, ETB is over-specified for the work. See the [Aurora Solar alternative comparison](/blog/aurora-solar-alternative/) if Aurora is on your shortlist too.

**Weakness against ETB:** it is not positioned around commercial demand charge modelling, and its code and tariff library is centred on the United States. Confirm current scope with the vendor.

### 3. HelioScope, for commercial and industrial engineering

**Best for:** Engineering teams whose hard problem is laying out large commercial roofs and producing a yield report that survives review.

HelioScope is the design pairing most C&I teams already run next to Energy Toolbase, so for many buyers these two are complements rather than competitors. Published pricing ranges from roughly $99 to over $300 per user per month. If you are leaving because the engineering side matters more than the tariff side, HelioScope plus a spreadsheet is a coherent stack. The [HelioScope pricing breakdown](/blog/helioscope-pricing/) covers the tiers, and the [HelioScope alternative guide](/blog/helioscope-alternative/) covers what else fits that slot.

**Weakness against ETB:** it is an engineering tool rather than a tariff and storage economics tool, so utility rates, demand charges, and battery dispatch are not what it is built around. Do not switch to HelioScope expecting the economics to come with it.

### 4. PVsyst, for bankable simulation

**Best for:** Consultants and independent engineers who need simulation output lenders and investors accept.

PVsyst is the reference simulation tool for bankability, licensed as desktop software rather than per-seat cloud subscription, which changes the cost shape entirely for a small consultancy. If your Energy Toolbase spend is really about producing credible numbers for a financier, PVsyst answers that for the production side of the model. The [PVsyst alternative guide](/blog/pvsyst-alternative/) and [PVsyst pricing overview](/blog/pvsyst-price/) cover the details.

**Weakness against ETB:** it is scoped to modelling energy rather than money at tariff level, and it is not positioned as a utility rate database or a proposal builder. Plan on something else for the financial layer, and confirm current scope with the vendor.

### 5. OpenSolar, the free option

**Best for:** Small residential installers who need fixed software cost close to zero.

OpenSolar's entry tier is free, funded through hardware and finance partners rather than seat fees. For a team whose pipeline turned residential and whose budget turned tight, this is the honest floor of the market. Design is competent for straightforward roofs and the proposal output is usable. The [OpenSolar alternative guide](/blog/opensolar-alternative/) covers where it stops.

**Weakness against ETB:** the economics layer is basic by comparison, add-ons stack up as you grow, and commercial work gets thin fast. Free is a real advantage right up until your pipeline includes a demand charge.

### 6. A spreadsheet plus a design tool, the honest baseline

**Best for:** Teams in simple net metering markets with flat or basic time-of-use tariffs.

This is what a large share of working solar businesses genuinely use, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. A design tool for the layout, and a maintained internal spreadsheet for the financial model, costs nothing beyond the design licence. Where the tariff is one rate and the incentive is one number, it is adequate.

**Weakness against ETB:** it collapses on commercial demand charges, particularly ratchets, where a single bad peak can set the charge for the following eleven months. A model that ignores ratchets overstates savings, and plenty of spreadsheets do exactly that. It also carries version risk the moment three people are editing it, and no auditable derivation for a customer's finance team.

Keeping it and pairing it with HelioScope or PVsyst is a perfectly good answer.
