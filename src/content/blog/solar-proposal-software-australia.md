## What An Australian Solar Proposal Has To Get Right

**STC deeming, computed not assumed.** The Small-scale Renewable Energy Scheme creates certificates for systems under 100 kW based on capacity, solar zone, and remaining scheme years to December 2030. Most retailers pass the value through as an upfront discount in exchange for assignment. The deeming period shortens each year, so a rebate figure hardcoded in a template decays annually without anyone noticing. SurgePV's [STC scheme guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/australia/guides/stc-scheme) covers the mechanics.

**CEC accreditation and approved products.** STC eligibility requires a CEC-accredited installer holding a relevant electrical licence and maintaining accreditation annually, plus CEC-approved components. Both halves matter. See SurgePV's [CEC accreditation guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/australia/guides/cec-accreditation).

**AS/NZS compliance at design time.** AS/NZS 5033:2021 sets DC cable sizing, string protection fusing, array and frame earthing, arc fault detection, and commissioning testing. AS 4777.2:2020 sets grid connection behaviour including volt-watt and volt-VAR response, ride-through, and anti-islanding. AS/NZS 5139:2019 governs battery installation. The [AS/NZS 5033 guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/australia/guides/as-nzs-5033) and [AS 4777.2 guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/australia/guides/as-4777-2) cover the detail.

**DNSP export limits.** Ausgrid in NSW, Energy Queensland, SA Power Networks, and Western Power all set their own limits and connection conditions, and constrained areas increasingly get dynamic or zero export. See the [export limits guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/australia/guides/export-limits).

## The Two Errors That Cost Australian Installers Money

> **What most Australian quotes get wrong.** They treat the STC rebate as a fixed dollars-per-kW figure and the feed-in tariff as unconstrained. Both were roughly true five years ago. Neither is true now, and the two errors compound in the same direction, making the system look cheaper and more productive than it will be.

**The deeming error.** Because the scheme ends in December 2030, the deeming period shortens every year on 1 January. A template carrying last year's per-kW rebate overstates the discount, and the gap widens each year until the scheme closes. Solar zone compounds it: the same system deems differently in Zone 1 and Zone 4, so a national flat rate is wrong nearly everywhere.

**The export limit error.** This one is larger in dollar terms. Consider a 10 kW system on a network segment with a 5 kW export limit. Generation above the limit is curtailed, not sold. On a household with low daytime load, that can remove a meaningful share of the expected feed-in income, and the customer sees it as underperformance rather than as a network constraint that was never modelled.

The consequence is the same in both cases. The installer who models honestly looks more expensive than the one who does not, and loses deals on a comparison that was never valid. The defence is to show the constraint explicitly in the proposal, because a customer who understands the export limit will accept a smaller system or a battery rather than assume you are padding.

## Worked Example: 10 kW In Adelaide, With And Without An Export Limit

An illustrative calculation showing the size of the export limit effect. Inputs: 10 kW array, 1,550 kWh per kW specific yield, 15,500 kWh generation, 6,200 kWh self-consumed, import at 42c per kWh, feed-in at 5c per kWh.

| Line | Unconstrained export | 5 kW export limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Annual generation | 15,500 kWh | 15,500 kWh |
| Self-consumed | 6,200 kWh | 6,200 kWh |
| Exported | 9,300 kWh | 6,900 kWh |
| Curtailed | 0 kWh | 2,400 kWh |
| Value of avoided import at 42c | $2,604 | $2,604 |
| Feed-in income at 5c | $465 | $345 |
| Total year one benefit | $3,069 | $2,949 |

The headline gap is $120, which looks small. That is the point worth making to the customer: at a 5c feed-in tariff, curtailment barely matters, and the 2,400 kWh being thrown away is worth $120 as export but $1,008 if shifted into household use. On these numbers the export limit is not the problem. The 5c tariff is, and it is the strongest battery argument in the Australian market. A proposal that only reports total savings hides that entirely.
