Pylon is one of the few solar platforms that will tell you what it costs before you talk to a salesperson. That single fact shapes how you should evaluate it. Where most of this category hides behind demo bookings and negotiated annual contracts, Pylon publishes a per-project rate, lets you start with free credits, and asks for no minimum spend. This review looks at what Pylon actually is in 2026, what the published pricing means once your real volume hits it, what the product does genuinely well, where the fit breaks down, and which alternatives deserve a look before you standardise on it. The criticisms here are scoped to things that can be checked against Pylon's own published material.

It is built for speed, publishes its pricing openly, and is strongest in Australia. It suits selective, high-close-rate residential teams. It suits high-volume quoting and lender-driven bankability work much less well.

## What Pylon Is

Pylon is a cloud platform for designing residential and commercial solar systems, generating customer-facing proposals, and managing the sales pipeline that surrounds them. You work from high resolution aerial imagery, place panels, run a 3D shading simulation, apply a financial projection, and produce a proposal the customer can open in a browser or receive as a PDF.

Around that core sit the operational outputs an installer needs after the sale: single-line diagrams, installer job sheets, and system owner's manuals. Those decide whether a tool leaves the sales team or stays stuck in it.

The CRM is the third piece. It handles pipeline, email sync, SMS follow-up, web forms, and an automated lead processor. It is sold separately, so you can run Pylon purely as a design and proposal engine alongside whatever CRM you already have.

Pylon is built in Sydney. Australian installers and customer stories are front and centre on its own site, and it was voted the number one solar design tool for 2025 by Australian installers.

Three details in the fine print matter more than the headline rates. Revisiting or editing an existing project costs nothing extra, so iteration is free once the project is bought. Each project allows up to five different designs, which covers the normal habit of presenting a good, better, best set. And team users are unlimited on the project side, so you are not paying per designer to open the tool.

That last point is the real argument for this model. A five-person team that quotes 40 Standard projects a month pays $160 for the project side. There is no per-seat design licence in that number at all. Compare that to any annual seat contract and the difference is not close.

**Worked example, illustrative only.** Take a team quoting 60 projects a month, split 45 Standard and 15 Pro, with three CRM seats. That is $180 plus $150 plus $147, so roughly $477 a month or about $5,724 a year. Now take a team quoting 250 projects a month, split 150 Standard and 100 Pro, with five CRM seats: $600 plus $1,000 plus $245, so roughly $1,845 a month or about $22,140 a year. Same product, wildly different bill. Confirm current rates with Pylon before budgeting against these numbers.

## What Pylon Genuinely Does Well

**Pricing transparency.** This is a real strength and it deserves to be named first. Pylon publishes rates, offers free credits for five Standard projects, and requires no credit card to start. In a category where most vendors will not give you a number until you have sat through a call, that is a meaningful difference in how you can evaluate the product. You can find out whether it works on your roofs before anyone knows your name.

**Speed to proposal.** The under-two-minutes claim is the product's organising principle, not a marketing afterthought. Imagery, layout, shading, financials, and the customer-facing document sit in one continuous flow. For a residential team whose constraint is quote turnaround rather than engineering rigour, that flow is worth more than any individual feature.

**Zero cost on unused seats.** The project side has unlimited team users. A coordinator who opens a project twice a month costs nothing, and so does a seasonal salesperson. Anyone who has run a per-seat contract through a slow quarter knows what that is worth.

**Free iteration.** Editing an existing project incurs no extra charge, and one project supports up to five designs. That removes the worst failure mode of per-unit pricing, which is a designer avoiding a second look because it costs money.

**The post-sale outputs.** Single-line diagrams, installer job sheets, and owner's manuals are included in the operational feature set. Plenty of sales-led tools stop at the signed proposal and hand the installation team a PDF and a shrug.

**Commercial and battery in scope.** The Pro tier explicitly covers battery storage design and commercial-sized projects, along with custom load profiles. Battery modelling for off-peak charging and time-of-use scenarios is called out directly, which reflects the Australian market Pylon grew up in, where time-of-use tariffs and batteries are ordinary rather than exotic.

## Where Pylon Falls Short

Every criticism below is scoped to what can be checked. Where we are unsure, we say so rather than asserting absence.

**The cost model inverts as you scale.** This is the structural limitation. Per-project pricing is a gift at low volume and a liability at high volume, because your bill tracks quotes rather than revenue. A team with a 20% close rate pays for five proposals to win one. If your growth strategy involves quoting more aggressively, Pylon's pricing works directly against the behaviour you are trying to encourage. Per-seat pricing has the opposite failure mode: it punishes idle headcount and rewards heavy use. Neither is universally better, but you should know which one matches how your business actually runs.

**Costs are variable and therefore harder to budget.** Forecasting a per-project line item means forecasting your marketing performance, which nobody does accurately. Add the 2.50% payment gateway fee and the $0.80 eSignature charge and the monthly number moves in three separate ways.

**Imagery coverage is the geographic constraint.** Pylon describes high resolution imagery as available in select countries. That is honest of them and it is also the single most important fit question for anyone outside the core markets. The two-minute proposal depends on good input imagery. Where the imagery is thin, the workflow that makes Pylon distinctive is the workflow that degrades. Check your specific service area before committing, not the country.

**Not positioned as a bankability tool.** Pylon does not present itself as the platform you hand a lender. Where a financing agreement names a specific simulation package, or where an investment committee expects P50, P75, and P90 yield bands from a recognised engine, Pylon is not what that requirement is asking for. This is not a defect. It is a product scoped for sales velocity being asked to do a different job. Teams in that position generally run a second tool, which changes the cost comparison completely.

**The CRM is a second decision, not an included one.** Calling the platform design, proposals, and CRM in one is fair, but the CRM is a separate monthly per-user subscription while the rest is per project. The $49 per user per month is a genuine per-seat cost sitting underneath an otherwise seat-free product.

> **What most buyers get wrong.** They compare Pylon's $4 against a competitor's annual seat price and stop there. The honest comparison needs your monthly project volume, your Standard-to-Pro mix, your CRM seat count, and your close rate. Run those four numbers first. The answer flips somewhere in the middle and you need to know which side of it you are on.

## Who Should Buy Pylon

Buy Pylon if most of these describe you.

- You sell residential solar in Australia, or in a market where Pylon's high resolution imagery is confirmed for your service area.
- Your monthly project volume is modest, roughly under 30 projects per designer, or your close rate is high enough that few quotes are wasted.
- Your team has more occasional users than full-time designers, so unlimited project seats save you real money.
- Time-of-use tariffs and battery attachment are central to your pitch, which is where the Pro tier earns its $10.
- You want to test a platform properly before you buy, without a sales call and without a contract.

At that profile Pylon is hard to argue against. A five-person team quoting selectively can run the design and proposal side for the price of a phone plan. Pretending that is not compelling would be dishonest.

## Who Should Not Buy Pylon

- **High-volume quoting operations.** If you quote everything and close a fifth of it, per-project pricing charges you for the four you lost. Model your annual spend at real volume before you sign.
- **Teams that need lender-accepted yield reports.** Where a financier names a simulation package, you will carry a second licence and a second workflow. That changes the total cost picture entirely.
- **Teams selling into markets with thin imagery coverage.** The speed advantage is built on input data quality. Verify your actual service area rather than trusting a country-level answer.
- **Finance teams that need a fixed software line.** Per-project, per-signature, and per-transaction charges are three moving parts in one invoice.
- **Anyone whose bottleneck is engineering depth, not proposal turnaround.** Pylon is optimised for the second problem.

## Verdict

Pylon is a well-scoped product that is honest about what it costs, which is rarer in this category than it should be. It is fast, it publishes its pricing, it lets you try it without a sales call, and it does not charge you for people who barely use it. For a selective residential team in Australia with a healthy close rate, that combination is genuinely hard to beat, and buying it is an easy decision.

The case gets harder as you scale. Per-project pricing is a promise that quietly reverses on you: the model that saved you money at 40 quotes a month bills you differently at 250, and it bills you for the losses as well as the wins. Add the separate CRM subscription, the transaction fee, and the signature charge, and the simple $4 headline turns into a variable line nobody can forecast. None of that is a criticism of the product's quality. It is a description of who the pricing model fits.

That is why, for teams past the early stage, our recommendation is SurgePV. Not because Pylon is a bad tool, but because once your quote volume is high, a flat $1,299 to $1,899 per user per year with design, shading, financial modelling, and proposals in one licence is both cheaper and predictable, and predictable is worth something on its own. If your volume is low and your market is Australia, buy Pylon and do not overthink it. If you are quoting hard, growing fast, or crossing borders, run the arithmetic before you commit to a model that charges you for every attempt.

Three things to do next:

- Count your real projects per month for the last quarter, including the ones that never closed, and multiply by the Standard and Pro rates.
- Confirm high resolution imagery coverage for your specific service area, not just your country.
- Rebuild three recent jobs in a competing tool and compare the output, then compare the two annual totals side by side.
