## The Farm's Energy Problem: Diesel Bills and PSPCL Agricultural Feeders

Agricultural feeders in Punjab operate on a scheduled supply system managed by PSPCL (Punjab State Power Corporation Limited). In Ludhiana district, the typical agricultural feeder provides approximately 14–16 hours of grid supply daily, with power cuts concentrated in morning and late-night hours, often the times when a dairy farm's chilling demand is highest after a morning milking session.

The representative farm in this case study ran a 30 kVA diesel generator to bridge the gap. At ₹89–92 per litre for high-speed diesel (HSD) as of June 2026 and a fuel consumption of 6–7 litres per hour at 60–70% load, the monthly fuel bill alone reached ₹55,000–₹65,000. Add engine oil top-ups, annual servicing at ₹18,000–₹22,000, and the opportunity cost of one farm worker dedicated to DG operation and monitoring, the true annual diesel cost approached ₹8.5–9 Lakh.

The farm's daytime electrical loads are summarised below:

| Load | Rated Power | Daily Runtime (Avg) | Daily kWh |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bulk milk chiller (2 units) | 7.5 kW each | 10 hours | 75 kWh |
| Pasteurisation unit | 5 kW | 4 hours | 20 kWh |

| Lighting and fans (shed + office) | 3 kW | 12 hours | 36 kWh |
| **Total daytime demand** | **~30 kW avg** | - | **~175 kWh** |

The farm's PSPCL tariff under the AP (agricultural pump) category was approximately ₹3.5 per kWh for metered consumption, low by commercial standards, but the actual unit cost blended with diesel bridging exceeded ₹14 per kWh over a full month. Solar, generating power at under ₹3 per kWh on a levelised basis, represented a clear opportunity.

## System Design: 30 kW on Cattle Shed Rooftops

The shed rooftops of a standard Punjab dairy operation (corrugated iron or asbestos cement sheets on a mild steel frame) present an unconventional but workable solar mounting surface. The representative farm used two cattle sheds, each approximately 25 metres long by 12 metres wide, with a south-facing pitched roof at roughly 12° inclination.

The EPC team mounted 75 panels of 400 Wp monocrystalline modules (total 30 kWp DC) in three strings of 25 panels each. The south-facing roof sections of both sheds were used, avoiding the north-facing sections and the narrow walkway between them. A [site survey](/glossary/site-survey/) and shading analysis confirmed no significant obstructions between 9 AM and 4 PM across the year.

The key system specifications:

- **Inverter**: Qbits TLD-30K three-phase on-grid string inverter (30 kW AC, IP66, dual MPPT)
- **Panels**: 75 × 400 Wp monocrystalline PERC modules, ALMM-listed
- **Mounting**: Galvanised angle-iron channel rail on existing shed purlins (no roof penetration drilling)
- **Monitoring**: Qbits AI WhatsApp monitoring via SIM-card data logger (Airtel 4G)
- **Metering**: PSPCL bidirectional net meter installed at existing agricultural connection
- **DC protection**: 1,000 V DC surge protection device (SPD) at combiner box
- **AC protection**: MCB, RCCB, and SPD at AC distribution board

The dual-[MPPT](/glossary/mppt/) configuration allowed the EPC team to wire shed 1 strings to MPPT-1 and shed 2 strings to MPPT-2, independently tracking the slight orientation difference between the two rooftops and preventing cross-string mismatch losses.

Total installed cost before subsidy: ₹19.5 Lakh (₹65,000 per kW, inclusive of civil, electrical, and monitoring, excluding GST at 12%).

## The KUSUM Framework: How Component-C Applies to a Dairy Farm

KUSUM (Kisan Urja Suraksha evam Utthaan Mahabhiyan) has three distinct components. The one most relevant to an existing grid-connected dairy farm is Component-C (the solarisation of existing grid-connected agricultural pump connections) administered in Punjab through [PEDA](https://peda.gov.in) (Punjab Energy Development Agency).

### The 3-Gate KUSUM Component-C Eligibility Check

1. **Active agricultural electricity connection**: The farm must hold a valid PSPCL agricultural tariff connection (AP-1 or equivalent). Dairy farms with PSPCL agricultural connections qualify; commercial tariff connections do not.
2. **System size proportionate to sanctioned load**: The solar system capacity typically cannot exceed the sanctioned pump load. A farm with a 30 kW sanctioned pump load can install up to 30 kW of rooftop solar under Component-C guidelines.
3. **ALMM-listed panels and BIS-certified inverter**: All equipment must appear on the MNRE Approved Models and Manufacturers List ([ALMM List](https://mnre.gov.in/almm-list/)) and hold BIS IS 16221 certification. The Qbits TLD-30K is ALMM-listed and BIS-certified.

Under Component-C, the central government provides a 30% capital subsidy and the state contributes a further 30%, reducing the farmer's net capital expenditure to 40% of the total system cost. On a ₹19.5 Lakh system:

| Funding component | Percentage | Amount |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Central Government subsidy | 30% | ₹5.85 Lakh |
| Punjab State (PEDA) subsidy | 30% | ₹5.85 Lakh |
| **Farmer's net capital outlay** | **40%** | **₹7.80 Lakh** |

The representative farm also applied 40% [accelerated depreciation](/glossary/accelerated-depreciation/) in Year 1 under Section 32 of the Income Tax Act, reducing the effective after-tax capital outlay further to approximately ₹5.5–6 Lakh in present-value terms, a benefit available to any agricultural business paying income tax on profits.

The KUSUM scheme also connects to the broader [PM KUSUM](/glossary/kusum/) framework under MNRE's rural electrification agenda, which has a national target of solarising 20 lakh grid-connected agricultural pumps by 2026. Farmers evaluating whether their state's KUSUM / DREBP implementation route applies to their connection type should confirm the exact subsidy stacking rules with their EPC before submitting a PEDA application, since disbursement timelines and documentation vary by state nodal agency.

(*Note: the two savings streams overlap in time; the total is the blended saving of ₹7–8 Lakh, not the arithmetic sum.*)

3. **Net metering export credit**: Midday surplus solar (when loads are low) exports to PSPCL at the agricultural feed-in tariff. At approximately 15–20 kWh of daily surplus on good-sun days, annual export credit adds ₹18,000–₹25,000 to the ledger. Modest, but non-zero.

4. **DG maintenance saving**: Reduced DG runtime (from 8–10 hours daily to 1–2 hours for non-solar periods) extends oil change intervals and engine service cycles. Annual DG maintenance cost drops from ₹22,000 to under ₹8,000. Saving: ~₹14,000 per year.

## IP66 and Farm Environment Protection: What the Specifications Mean on the Ground

Most solar specification discussions about [IP66](/blog/ip65-vs-ip66-solar-inverters-weather-protection-guide/) focus on monsoon rain. For a dairy farm, the relevant threats are more chemically aggressive.

### Ammonia and Hydrogen Sulphide

Cattle urine and decomposing manure produce ammonia (NH₃) and hydrogen sulphide (H₂S), both corrosive to copper conductors, tin-lead solder joints, and aluminium heatsinks inside an inverter. Farms that install IP54 or IP65 inverters without additional conformal coating inside animal sheds frequently report premature PCB corrosion within two to three monsoon cycles. IP66 enclosures with fully sealed cable glands and no ventilation openings prevent gas ingress.

### Wash-Down Water Pressure

Cattle sheds are cleaned with high-pressure hoses, water pressure ranging from 50 to 80 bar at 1.5–3 metres distance. IP65 protection is defined against water jets from any direction; IP66 extends this to high-pressure, heavy-volume jets. Inverters installed under shed eaves within the splash zone of wash-down operations require IP66 as a minimum.

### Dust from Feed Storage

Dry fodder, grain, and silage generate fine particulate matter in the 1–5 micron range, small enough to pass through unsealed vents on IP54 enclosures. IP66's dust-tight rating (the "6" first digit) ensures complete dust exclusion, preventing the abrasive particle accumulation on power capacitors and IGBT modules that accelerates derating and thermal failure.

| Threat | IP54 | IP65 | IP66 | Risk without IP66 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Corrosive gas (NH₃, H₂S) | ✗ | ✗ | ✓ | PCB corrosion within 2 years |
| High-pressure wash jets | ✗ | Partial | ✓ | Water ingress, board failure |
| Fine feed dust | Partial | ✓ | ✓ | Capacitor wear, derating |
| Monsoon rain splash | ✗ | ✓ | ✓ | Short circuits |

The representative farm's Qbits TLD-30K was installed inside the feed storage annex adjacent to shed 1, approximately 4 metres from the nearest cattle stall. The mounting position was chosen to minimise vibration from the pasteurisation unit, maximise airflow on the inverter's rear heatsink, and remain accessible to the EPC technician without entering the milking area.

## Installation Checklist and Common Mistakes on Agricultural Solar Projects

Agricultural solar installations share some challenges with industrial rooftop systems but add farm-specific complications that standard EPC teams occasionally overlook.

- **Roof load verification before panel mounting**: Corrugated iron or asbestos cement shed roofs vary significantly in structural capacity. The representative farm's EPC team engaged a structural engineer, following the same structural and civil engineering assessment process used on larger rooftop projects, to confirm the shed purlins could carry 10–12 kg/m² additional dead load from panels and rails before proceeding. Skipping this step has caused partial roof collapses on farms that installed on ageing shed structures.

- **DC cable routing away from cattle**: DC cables at 600–1,000 V pose an electrocution risk if chewed by rodents or contacted by cattle tails during maintenance. The representative farm ran all DC wiring inside galvanised steel conduits sealed at both ends, with no exposed cable runs below 2.5 metres height.

- **Earthing to PSPCL standards**: Agricultural installations in Punjab must meet PSPCL earthing requirements for grid-connected systems. The inverter chassis, panel frames, and mounting rails were earthed to a single point earth electrode with a resistance below 5 ohms, verified by the PSPCL inspection engineer at commissioning.

- **Inverter location away from high-vibration equipment**: Pasteurisation units and milk pumps generate significant vibration. Installing the inverter on the same wall or platform as these machines accelerates solder-joint fatigue on the PCB. The recommended clearance is at least 3 metres from heavy rotating machinery.

- **PSPCL net metering application before commissioning**: Several farms commission systems before the bidirectional meter is installed, then run in zero-export mode for months waiting for PSPCL approval. The correct sequence is: site survey → EPC contract → PSPCL net meter application → PSPCL approval → installation → PSPCL inspection → commissioning. The net meter application should precede installation by at least 4–6 weeks.

PSPCL's agricultural feeder scheduling means that most of these connections operate diesel generators for 6–10 hours daily. The [KUSUM scheme](/blog/kusum-yojana-agricultural-solar-subsidy/) under MNRE targets 20 lakh grid-connected pump solarisations nationally under Component-C, with Punjab one of the priority states given its large AP connection base and PEDA's established implementation capacity. Applications open periodically through the [PEDA portal](https://peda.gov.in) and are processed in tranches by district.

Dairy farm owners considering solar frequently ask whether [net metering](/blog/net-metering-india-complete-guide/) applies to their PSPCL agricultural connection, the short answer is yes, under the PSPCL net metering policy for agricultural consumers, but the feed-in tariff is set at a rate below the standard retail tariff and above the agricultural concessional tariff. The financial value of net metering exports is secondary to diesel displacement for farms with long power cuts; the primary case for solar on a Punjab dairy farm is fuel cost elimination, not grid export income.

The [solar versus diesel generator](/blog/solar-vs-diesel-generator-india/) analysis for C&I buyers in India shows that farms running generators for more than four hours daily achieve payback periods under three years even without subsidies, and with KUSUM Component-C funding available, the case becomes even more compelling.

For farms that want to understand the full investment framework before approaching an EPC, the [on-grid versus hybrid decision guide](/blog/on-grid-vs-hybrid-vs-off-grid-decision-guide/) provides a structured comparison across system types, cost profiles, and backup capability requirements, a useful reference before the first site assessment meeting.

Punjab dairy operators who move first on solar (before subsidy scheme windows close and before PSPCL agricultural tariffs normalise upward) will capture the strongest 25-year returns. The 3.5-year payback window documented in this case study is available now, on real farm rooftops, with real KUSUM funding. The question is not whether the numbers work. The numbers are clear. The question is how quickly a farm can navigate the application process and get a system commissioned before the next monsoon cuts into generation.
