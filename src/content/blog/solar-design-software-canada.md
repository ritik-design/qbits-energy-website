Canadian solar design is not American solar design with different spelling. Solar PV falls under Section 64 of the Canadian Electrical Code, CSA C22.1:24, and the conductor sizing rules differ from NEC 2023 on derate factors and conduit fill. Every component must carry a Standards Council of [Canada](/blog/solar-software-canada/) accredited certification mark, so a module with a US-only UL listing is not legal to install. And net metering is not a national program at all: there are ten provincial frameworks, with capacity caps from 100 kW to 5 MW and export credits from 4.39 cents to full retail rate. Most solar [design software](https://heavendesigns.in/blog/solar-design-software-for-installers/) treats Canada as a US region. This guide compares the seven platforms Canadian installers should shortlist, and explains where the US-built tools quietly stop working.

## What Canadian Installers Actually Need From Solar Design Software

**CSA C22.1 Section 64 compliance.** The current national standard is CSA C22.1:24, the 26th edition, adopted by British Columbia, Ontario, and Alberta among others. Section 64 covers renewable energy systems and storage. Installation must be performed by or under the supervision of a licensed electrical contractor. The practical software requirement is that conductor sizing, [string sizing](/blog/solar-string-sizing-software/), and overcurrent protection run against CSA derates rather than NEC ones. SurgePV's [CSA C22.1 guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/canada/guides/csa-c22-1) covers the specific clauses.

**SCC-accredited certification filtering.** Components need CSA, cUL, cETL, or cULus marks from a Standards Council of Canada accredited body. This trips up installers sourcing internationally, because a module with an IEC or US UL listing but no Canadian mark cannot legally be commissioned. A design tool with a Canadian-filtered database catches this before the BOQ goes to procurement.

**Provincial net metering, modelled per province.** This is the biggest single gap in US-built tools. The table below is the real picture.

| Province | Authority | Max system size | Export credit | Reset period |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| British Columbia | BC Hydro | 100 kW | 10 cents per kWh from July 2026 | Annual payout |
| Ontario | Hydro One and local LDCs | 500 kW | Retail, roughly $0.14 to $0.15 per kWh | 12 months |
| Quebec | Hydro-Quebec | 1 MW, expanded 2026 | kWh credit at average supply cost | 24 month reset |
| Alberta | ENMAX, ATCO and others | 5 MW | Retail below 150 kW, wholesale above | Monthly |
| Saskatchewan | SaskPower | 100 kW | 7.5 cents per kWh until March 2029 | Non-expiring |
| Manitoba | Manitoba Hydro | 100 kW | $0.04390 per kWh net billing | Monetary credit |

The design consequence is direct. In Ontario at retail credit with a 12 month window, oversizing against annual consumption is rational. In Manitoba at 4.39 cents, every exported kWh is worth roughly a third of an imported one, so the correct design maximises self-consumption and probably includes a battery. Same array, opposite sizing logic. A tool that models one national export rate gets one of these wrong. SurgePV's [Canada compliance library](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/canada) carries the provincial detail.

**Winter shading accuracy.** Canadian latitudes produce very low December and January sun angles, so obstruction and inter-row shading losses in winter are far more severe than in the US sunbelt. An 8,760-hour module-level simulation captures it. Monthly averaging does not. Our [solar shading analysis software](/blog/solar-shading-analysis-software/) guide covers why hourly module-level resolution is the minimum credible standard.

## Canadian Incentives That Belong In The Proposal

The federal Clean Technology Investment Tax Credit gives businesses a 30% refundable credit on capital cost through 31 December 2033. That is the anchor for all commercial and agricultural work. Residential relies on provincial programs, which vary widely.

| Program | Level | Value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clean Technology ITC | Federal, business only | 30% refundable to 31 Dec 2033 |
| BC Hydro | Provincial | Up to $10,000, solar plus battery |
| Quebec grant | Provincial | $1,000 per kW installed, launched March 2026 |
| Ontario HRSP | Provincial | Up to $5,000, solar only |
| PEI residential | Provincial | $1,000 per kW, max $10,000 |
| Manitoba | Provincial | $0.50 per DC watt installed, to 10 kW |

> **What most Canadian proposals still get wrong.** They reference the Canada Greener Homes Grant. It closed on 31 March 2024, and the associated loan closed on 1 October 2025. Any template still carrying either line is quoting money the customer cannot get.

Quebec's $1,000 per kW grant, launched in March 2026, is the most generous per-kW residential program in the country and materially changes residential economics there. If your proposal software cannot model it, you are understating the return in the province where it is currently strongest.

## Worked Example: 12 kW Rooftop, Ontario Versus Manitoba

This is an illustrative calculation to show why provincial modelling changes the design, not a specific customer project. Inputs are identical in both provinces: 12 kW DC array, 1,150 kWh per kW annual specific yield, 11,000 kWh household consumption, 13,800 kWh generation.

| Line | Ontario, retail credit | Manitoba, net billing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Annual generation | 13,800 kWh | 13,800 kWh |
| Self-consumed | 5,900 kWh | 5,900 kWh |
| Exported | 7,900 kWh | 7,900 kWh |
| Export credit rate | $0.145 per kWh | $0.04390 per kWh |
| Value of exports | $1,146 | $347 |
| Value of self-consumption at $0.145 | $856 | $856 |
| Total year 1 benefit | $2,002 | $1,203 |

Same hardware, a 40% difference in year one benefit. In Ontario the oversized array pays. In Manitoba the correct response is a smaller array, or the same array with a battery to convert low-value exports into avoided imports. An installer using a tool that assumes retail-rate net metering will recommend the Ontario design in Winnipeg and be wrong by roughly $800 a year.
