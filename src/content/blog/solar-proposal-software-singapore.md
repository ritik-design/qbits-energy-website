## Present ECIS As A Range, Not A Number

> **What most Singapore proposals get wrong.** They quote ECIS at an average USEP figure and present it with the same confidence as the fixed SCT rate. Those are not comparable claims. One is a contracted price and the other is a forecast of a wholesale market, and dressing the second up as the first is the kind of precision that erodes trust when the settlements arrive.

The honest structure shows SCT as a single number, because it is one, and ECIS as a range with the assumptions visible. The range should come from running the hourly generation profile against a USEP price profile rather than multiplying annual kWh by an average, because the two series are correlated: solar exports most at midday, and midday pricing is not representative of the daily mean.

A useful framing for the customer is that SCT trades upside for certainty. At roughly S$0.20 per kWh it sits above the low end of the typical USEP range, so it is not obviously the weaker commercial choice. ECIS makes sense where the customer has the administrative capacity for EMC Market Participant registration and a view on wholesale prices.

There is a simplifying fact worth leading with in most commercial proposals: self-consumption dominates. With commercial import prices well above both the SCT rate and the typical USEP range, avoided import usually accounts for the large majority of total value. The scheme choice therefore affects a minority of the return, and telling the customer that plainly saves everyone a long conversation about a secondary variable. SurgePV's [SCT guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/singapore/guides/sct) and [ECIS guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/singapore/guides/ecis) cover the registration mechanics.

## The 1 MWac Threshold Is A Scheduling Line

Below 1 MWac, no EMA Generation Licence. At or above it, a full licence through the ELISE portal at elise.ema.gov.sg, estimated at three to six months. On a large industrial rooftop that boundary is often within design discretion, and a proposal that reports only DC capacity hides the decision entirely.

The practical recommendation is to state the AC rating prominently and, where the design sits close to the line, present both options: the sub-threshold system that can proceed quickly, and the larger system with the licensing timeline attached. Customers with a fixed commissioning target will usually take the first. Customers optimising lifetime value may take the second. Either way it should be their choice rather than an accident of how the tool reported capacity. See SurgePV's [EMA licensing guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/singapore/guides/ema-licensing).

A Licensed Electrical Worker must submit the Electrical Installation application at every size, so that cost belongs in every quote regardless of whether licensing applies.

## Reporting Value Belongs In Commercial Proposals

Much of Singapore's solar demand comes from listed companies, multinationals and institutions with group-level sustainability reporting obligations. For those buyers, quantified avoided emissions sit alongside payback rather than beneath it, and a proposal that reports only a financial return is answering half the question.

JTC also imposes mandatory deployment obligations on certain industrial premises, covered in SurgePV's [JTC solar guide](https://www.surgepv.com/solar-compliance/singapore/guides/jtc-solar). Where those apply, the buying rationale changes completely: the customer is not evaluating whether to install, but how to install efficiently. A payback-led proposal misreads that situation, and a proposal that leads with compliance and cost-efficiency reads it correctly.

## Worked Example: 1.15 MWp Industrial Roof, Either Side Of The Threshold

An illustrative calculation, not a specific customer project. Inputs: usable roof supporting 1.15 MWp DC, 1,250 kWh per kWp specific yield, 70% self-consumption, import at S$0.28 per kWh, SCT export at S$0.20 per kWh.

| Line | Designed to 990 kWac | Designed to 1,035 kWac |
| --- | --- | --- |
| DC capacity | 1,100 kWp | 1,150 kWp |
| AC rating | 990 kWac | 1,035 kWac |
| EMA Generation Licence | Not required | Required, est. 3 to 6 months |
| Annual generation | 1,375,000 kWh | 1,437,500 kWh |
| Avoided import at S$0.28 | S$269,500 | S$281,750 |
| SCT export at S$0.20 | S$82,500 | S$86,250 |
| Year 1 benefit | S$352,000 | S$368,000 |
| Time to first generation | Standard | Plus 3 to 6 months |

The larger system earns S$16,000 more per year and arrives up to six months later. On those numbers the delay costs roughly S$150,000 of foregone benefit at the outset, which takes nearly a decade of the S$16,000 annual advantage to recover. For most buyers the sub-threshold design is the better project, and that conclusion is only visible if the proposal reports AC rating rather than DC capacity alone.
