## Who Needs a 3-Phase Solar Inverter in India

The short answer: any facility whose DISCOM sanctioned load is 10 kW or higher. In practice, this covers a wide cross-section of Indian commercial and industrial users.

The Central Electricity Authority mandates three-phase connections for loads above a threshold that varies by state but is almost universally set between 5 kW and 10 kW. Once the connection is three-phase, the solar inverter must also be three-phase to synchronise with the utility supply. Connecting a single-phase inverter to a three-phase metering point is a protection relay fault waiting to happen.

Facility types that typically require three-phase solar inverters:

- **Textile mills and garment factories** - motor-driven looms, compressors, and VFD-controlled spindles draw balanced three-phase loads ranging from 50 kW to several MW. Even a mid-sized weaving unit with 200 looms typically runs a 200–500 kW sanctioned demand.

- **Educational institutions** - large campuses with central air-conditioning, workshops, and hostels typically carry 50–250 kW sanctioned loads.
- **IT parks and data centres** - UPS feeds, precision air-conditioning, and server room infrastructure are three-phase by design.

## How a 3-Phase Solar Inverter Works - Technical Foundations

A three-phase solar inverter houses three independent power-conversion bridges inside a single enclosure, each producing a 230 V AC output that is 120° phase-shifted relative to the other two. The combined output at the AC terminals is 415 V line-to-line - the standard industrial voltage in India.

The DC input side is served by one or more [MPPT](/glossary/mppt/) trackers. In a typical 50 kW three-phase on-grid inverter, two MPPT inputs each handle a separate string array. Each MPPT independently tracks the maximum power point for its array, which means partial shading on one section of the roof does not drag down the output of the other section.

Key electrical characteristics to understand at the specification stage:

- **DC input voltage range** - most three-phase inverters for Indian C&I applications accept 200–1,000 V DC. The Qbits TLD series is designed for string voltages up to 1,000 V, allowing longer strings with higher-voltage 72-cell or 144-half-cell modules.
- **Maximum input current per MPPT** - typically 25–30 A per MPPT channel in a 50 kW unit. With 560 Wp bifacial modules that produce 14.8 A Isc, each MPPT can handle two parallel strings without exceeding the current limit.
- **AC output current** - at 415 V three-phase, a 50 kW inverter outputs approximately 70 A line current (50,000 W ÷ (415 V × √3 × power factor)). The interconnecting AC cable must be rated accordingly.
- **Power factor** - three-phase on-grid inverters typically operate at unity power factor (0.99–1.0) under IEC 61727. However, facilities with highly inductive loads may benefit from inverters that offer reactive power compensation capability.
- **THD** - [total harmonic distortion](/glossary/thd/) at the grid connection point must remain below 5% per CEA regulations. Premium inverters achieve THD below 3% at full load, which matters for facilities running CNC machines or sensitive medical equipment.

The efficiency curve of a three-phase inverter is broadly similar to its single-phase counterpart at equivalent power ratings. European Weighted Efficiency (EWE) values of 97.5–98.2% are typical for quality three-phase units in the 25–100 kW range. The Qbits TLD series achieves 98% peak efficiency, which translates to roughly 1.5–2% more annual energy yield compared to older-generation units rated at 96–96.5%.

## The C&I 3-Phase Solar Sizing Checklist - 6 Steps Before You Specify

Every C&I solar project that sizes incorrectly does so at one of six decision points. Working through these steps in order prevents the most common and costly mistakes.

### Step 1: Confirm the Sanctioned Load and Connection Type

Pull the latest DISCOM sanction letter or the meter certificate. Confirm:
- Sanctioned load in kW (not kVA - they differ by power factor)
- Connection voltage (LT 415 V three-phase, or HT 11 kV / 33 kV)
- Existing contracted demand for ToD (time-of-day) tariff accounts

HT connections above 11 kV require a step-down transformer on the solar AC output side and a separate metering arrangement - the inverter alone is not the complete system boundary.

### Step 2: Audit the Monthly Consumption Profile

Obtain 12 months of bills. Calculate:
- Average monthly consumption in kWh
- Peak demand in kW (from the demand charge line on the bill)
- Load factor = average kW / peak kW

A load factor above 0.65 indicates a relatively flat load profile - ideal for maximising self-consumption. A load factor below 0.40 (common in seasonal manufacturing) means the solar system must be sized to the daytime average, not the peak, or the facility will over-export during low-production months.

### Step 3: Calculate the Target Solar Capacity

Target solar capacity (kW) = (Monthly kWh consumption × self-consumption fraction) ÷ (monthly peak sun hours × performance ratio)

For a textile factory in Tamil Nadu consuming 60,000 kWh per month with 5.5 peak sun hours per day and a 0.80 performance ratio:

Target capacity = (60,000 × 0.80) ÷ (5.5 × 30 × 0.80) = 48,000 ÷ 132 ≈ 364 kWh/day ÷ 5.5 hours ≈ **50 kW AC inverter**

This aligns with a 60–65 kWp DC array at a DC:AC ratio of 1.25.

### Step 4: Design the MPPT String Array

With 560 Wp modules (Voc 49.5 V, Vmp 41.8 V, Isc 14.8 A, Imp 13.4 A):

- Minimum string voltage at 45 °C: Vmp × temperature correction × string count ≥ inverter Vmp(min) of 200 V → requires at least 5 modules per string
- Maximum string voltage at 5 °C cold-start: Voc × temperature correction (1.08 at 5 °C) × string count ≤ 1,000 V → maximum 19 modules per string
- Safe design: 16 modules per string → Vmp string = 16 × 41.8 V × 0.96 (at 45 °C) = 641 V - well within range

For the 50 kW TLD-50K with dual MPPT at 30 A each: 2 parallel strings per MPPT (2 × 13.4 A = 26.8 A - within limit). Total: 4 strings × 16 modules = 64 modules × 560 Wp = 35.8 kWp. A second inverter unit brings the array to 71.6 kWp for a 65 kWp target - the slight excess accounts for cable losses and soiling.

Use the [string sizing calculator](/string-sizing-calculator/) to validate these numbers before finalising the procurement list.

### Step 5: Check DC:AC Ratio Against Site Irradiation

| State / Region | Recommended DC:AC Ratio | Rationale |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rajasthan, Gujarat | 1.35–1.45 | High irradiation, limited clipping risk |
| Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh | 1.25–1.35 | Good irradiation, moderate humidity |
| Maharashtra, Karnataka | 1.20–1.30 | Monsoon cloud cover reduces clipping risk |
| Uttar Pradesh, Bihar | 1.15–1.25 | Lower GHI; tighter ratio improves performance ratio |
| West Bengal, Assam | 1.10–1.20 | High humidity, haze; avoid aggressive oversizing |

Higher DC:AC ratios are validated through detailed energy yield simulations. A 1.4 ratio on a Tamil Nadu site typically adds 3–5% more annual energy versus a 1.25 ratio, at the cost of 1–2% clipping at peak noon hours.

### Step 6: Verify THD Compliance and Utility Interface

Confirm with the EPC:
- Inverter THD < 5% at full load (mandatory per CEA Safety Regulations)
- Anti-islanding protection compliant with IEC 62116 (mandatory for grid-connected systems)
- DC injection limit < 0.5% (required per [CEA regulations](https://cea.nic.in/))
- Neutral connection scheme - LN230 V systems require a solidly grounded neutral at the inverter AC output panel

For facilities with sensitive CNC equipment or medical devices, specify THD < 3% and confirm the inverter includes an active filter stage or has been tested at low load fractions where THD tends to rise.

## 3-Phase Net Metering in India - State-by-State Rules

Net metering for three-phase connections works on the same principle as single-phase: surplus solar units exported to the grid are credited at a state-determined feed-in rate against future consumption. The procedural and technical requirements, however, are more complex.

### Sanctioned Load Thresholds for Three-Phase Net Metering

Most state SERC orders cap net metering eligibility at a system size equal to the sanctioned load. A facility with 100 kW sanctioned load can install up to 100 kW of solar and claim net metering. Above this threshold, states typically mandate gross metering - where all exported units are billed separately at a (usually lower) feed-in tariff, and all consumed units are billed at the full import tariff.

The [net metering India complete guide](/blog/net-metering-india-complete-guide/) tracks state-by-state thresholds, which are updated as state SERCs revise their regulations.

### Three-Phase Net Metering Application Process

1. **Apply to the DISCOM** with the technical proposal (single-line diagram, inverter datasheet, proposed panel layout).
2. **DISCOM technical inspection** - the distribution engineer verifies the proposed connection point and metering arrangement.
3. **Bidirectional meter installation** - for three-phase connections, the meter must be a three-phase, four-wire bidirectional energy meter capable of recording import and export on each phase separately.
4. **Agreement signing** - interconnection agreement and net metering agreement with the DISCOM.
5. **Commissioning** - the DISCOM inspector witnesses the commissioning test, including anti-islanding verification.

Processing timelines vary: Karnataka, Telangana, and Gujarat have streamlined processes with 30–45 day turnarounds. UP and Bihar DISCOM approvals can take 90–120 days for larger three-phase systems.

### Export Limits and Zero-Export Configurations

Some DISCOMs in states with high daytime solar penetration (Tamil Nadu, Rajasthan) impose zero-export or export-cap requirements for rooftop systems above a certain threshold. This requires the inverter to operate in export-limiting mode - detecting real-time net power flow at the utility meter and throttling MPPT output accordingly.

The Qbits TLD series supports dynamic export limiting via RS485 Modbus communication with an external smart meter, meeting the zero-export requirements of DISCOMs that mandate this configuration.

## Comparing On-Grid vs Hybrid Three-Phase Inverters for C&I

The choice between an on-grid three-phase inverter and a hybrid three-phase inverter comes down to two variables: the facility's power continuity requirement and the local grid reliability.

### On-Grid Three-Phase Inverters (TLD Series)

On-grid inverters are grid-dependent by design. When the utility supply fails, the inverter shuts down within 200 ms (anti-islanding protection per IEC 62116). This is a safety requirement - without it, the inverter could energise a line that utility workers believe is de-energised.

**Best suited for:** factories and commercial buildings in cities with reliable grid supply (DISCOM reliability above 99.5% uptime) where the primary objective is bill reduction and tax optimisation, not power backup.

**Pros:**
- ✓ Lower capital cost - no battery, no hybrid-topology complexity
- ✓ Faster payback - ₹35–45 lakh for a 50 kW on-grid system vs ₹55–70 lakh for hybrid
- ✓ Simpler DISCOM approval - net metering process is well-defined
- ✓ Full AD benefit applies to all components

**Cons:**
- ✗ Zero generation during power outages - critical for factories with continuous processes
- ✗ Cannot island - no self-powered operation without utility synchronisation
- ✗ Export curtailment if DISCOM imposes zero-export rules reduces returns

### Hybrid Three-Phase Inverters (HT Series)

Hybrid inverters integrate battery management and can operate in island mode during grid outages. The Qbits HT series operates in three modes: grid-tied (normal operation, full export/import), backup (grid outage, battery sustains critical loads), and self-consumption (minimise export, maximise battery charge).

**Best suited for:** hospitals, data centres, food processing facilities, and educational campuses where grid outages directly cause financial loss or safety risk.

**Pros:**
- ✓ Continuous operation through grid outages - switchover in <20 ms for critical load panels
- ✓ Time-of-day arbitrage - charge battery at off-peak rates, discharge at peak tariff hours
- ✓ DG displacement - replaces diesel genset backup with silent battery backup
- ✓ Future-proof - battery can be added later on HT topology without rewiring

**Cons:**
- ✗ Higher upfront cost - battery bank adds ₹15–25 lakh for a 100 kWh LFP system
- ✗ Longer payback - typically 5.5–7 years vs 4–5.5 years for on-grid
- ✗ Battery replacement cycle at year 10–12 requires capital provision

## Common Mistakes in 3-Phase Solar Procurement

Even experienced procurement teams make avoidable errors when specifying three-phase solar systems. The following mistakes account for the majority of post-commissioning performance shortfalls.

**1. Specifying inverter kW based on panel Wp, not on actual load** - a 65 kWp panel array does not require a 65 kW inverter. The inverter AC rating should reflect the facility's daytime load and net metering approval limit, not the DC array size. Oversizing the inverter relative to the load leads to poor partial-load efficiency and lower self-consumption ratios.

**2. Ignoring temperature derating** - inverters derate output when ambient temperature exceeds 40 °C. An inverter rated 50 kW at 25 °C may deliver only 44–46 kW at the 48–50 °C temperatures typical of Indian industrial rooftops in May. Always check the derating curve in the datasheet, not just the nameplate rating.

**3. Skipping the load-flow study for HT connections** - facilities connected at 11 kV or 33 kV must submit a load-flow study to the DISCOM or SLDC before the solar system can be commissioned. Omitting this step causes a commissioning delay of 3–6 months.

**4. Mismatching inverter THD with facility load sensitivity** - a 4.9% THD inverter meets the CEA limit but may still introduce interference in facilities with CNC machines or medical imaging equipment. The specification must match the facility's sensitivity, not just the regulatory floor.

**5. Choosing a 3-year-warranty inverter to save ₹1.5 lakh** - a 50 kW inverter replacement mid-life costs ₹4–6 lakh in equipment plus 2–3 days of lost production. The ₹1.5 lakh saved on a 3-year-warranty unit is a false economy. The [solar inverter lifespan guide](https://www.iea.org/reports/solar-pv-global-supply-chains) and field data consistently show warranty depth as the single strongest predictor of long-term total cost of ownership.

**6. Not verifying ALMM compliance** - for projects claiming accelerated depreciation or applying for state-level incentives, the inverter must appear on the MNRE ALMM (Approved List of Models and Manufacturers) list. Verify the exact model number against the [ALMM list](https://mnre.gov.in/) before issuing the purchase order.
