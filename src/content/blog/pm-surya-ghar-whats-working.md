Three years into PM Surya Ghar Muft Bijli Yojana, about 8.5 million households have registered on the portal, roughly 3.5 million installations have been commissioned, and the government has disbursed several thousand crore rupees in direct subsidies. By any absolute measure, this is the largest rooftop solar programme India has ever run. By the measure of its own ambition (1 crore rooftop installations by the end of the target period) the scheme is approximately one-third of the way there, and progress is slowing in the states that matter most.

As someone who works daily with the installers, DISCOMs, and homeowners who are inside this system, I want to offer a view that goes beyond the press release and the complaint thread. PM Surya Ghar has achieved real things. It has also created real structural problems that will not resolve themselves. Both deserve honest treatment.

> **TL;DR**
> - PM Surya Ghar has commissioned roughly 3.5 million rooftops out of 8.5 million registrations, about one-third of the way to its 1 crore target.
> - The ₹78,000 subsidy cap and ALMM Phase III equipment standards are working; DISCOM interconnection delays of 4–9 months in several states are the biggest failure point.
> - Apartment and flat owners, roughly 30–35% of urban India, have no viable subsidy pathway because a virtual net metering mechanism does not yet exist at scale.
> - Subsidy disbursement targets 30 days but often takes 90–120+ days in states with slower smart meter rollout.
> - Gujarat, Karnataka, and Maharashtra process applications fastest; Bihar, Jharkhand, and parts of Uttar Pradesh lag furthest behind.
> - The five reforms in our scorecard, DISCOM SLAs, community solar, empanelment reform, AMC standards, and PFMS pre-validation, could add 2–3 million installations a year to the current trajectory.

> **The honest pm surya ghar opinion.** PM Surya Ghar works best as a demand-generation engine: the ₹78,000 subsidy cap converts curious homeowners into paying customers, the ALMM mandate is raising equipment standards, and the online portal has reduced paperwork by several orders of magnitude compared to the pre-2024 era. What it has not solved is the supply-side infrastructure problem, DISCOM interconnection processing that takes 4–9 months in too many states, a vendor empanelment framework that excludes quality smaller installers, and no clear pathway for the 100 million-plus households who live in apartments.

This is not a complaint piece. Every point below includes a specific policy lever that would address it, because the goal of writing this is to contribute to a better programme rather than to catalogue its failures.

## What PM Surya Ghar Has Genuinely Achieved

Start with the real wins, because they are significant and they deserve acknowledgement before the critique.

**The online portal is functional and genuinely useful.** The [PM Surya Ghar portal](https://pmsuryaghar.gov.in/) replaced a previous world of state-by-state DISCOM application forms, physical document submissions, and opaque approval queues. Today a homeowner in Jaipur or Nagpur can register, upload their electricity bill, receive a technical feasibility response, select from an empanelled vendor list, track their application status, and receive their subsidy directly to their Aadhaar-linked bank account, all from a single dashboard. The system is not perfect, but it is vastly better than what preceded it, and that improvement is directly attributable to this scheme.

**The subsidy amounts are genuinely attractive.** ₹30,000 per kW for the first 2 kW, and ₹18,000 per kW for the third kilowatt, giving a maximum central subsidy of ₹78,000 for a 3 kW system, these are numbers that materially change the payback calculation for a middle-income household. For a 3 kW system costing ₹1.8 Lakh installed, a ₹78,000 subsidy brings the net cost to just over ₹1 Lakh, which at a ₹8/unit tariff delivers payback in under four years. That is a compelling financial case, and the government deserves credit for setting the subsidy at a level that actually moves behaviour.

> **₹78,000.** The maximum central subsidy under PM Surya Ghar for a 3 kW or larger residential system, transferred directly to the homeowner's Aadhaar-linked bank account after DISCOM commissioning verification. *Source - [MNRE PM Surya Ghar Portal](https://pmsuryaghar.gov.in/), 2026.*

**ALMM Phase III is raising the quality floor.** The requirement that all subsidised systems use [ALMM-listed](/glossary/almm-list/) inverters and modules has real teeth now. Brands that were selling low-quality assembled products under premium-sounding names are finding it harder to win empanelled contracts. Homeowners who previously had no way to evaluate equipment quality now have a minimum floor: if the installer is proposing a product not on the ALMM list, the buyer knows immediately that something is wrong. For the long-term health of the rooftop solar market, this quality enforcement is probably the single most important structural contribution of the scheme. Our deeper look at the [ALMM Phase III changes](/blog/almm-list-phase-iii-guide/) explains what the updated list means for buyers selecting equipment.

**The 1 crore target is driving installer market growth.** Whatever its implementation problems, the sheer scale of PM Surya Ghar's ambition has attracted capital and talent into the rooftop solar installation market. EPC companies that were doing 20 installs per month in 2023 are now doing 80. Equipment distributors have expanded into Tier-2 and Tier-3 cities where they had no presence before. The scheme has functioned as a market-formation event, which is exactly what a government programme of this type should do. India's rooftop solar market grew by an estimated 65% year-on-year in 2025, a trajectory that [Mercom India](https://www.mercomindia.com/) attributes primarily to the PM Surya Ghar demand wave. Our overview of [solar EPC business dynamics in India](/blog/solar-epc-india/) covers how installer markets respond to large-scale policy demand.

## Where the Scheme Is Falling Short

The problems are structural, not administrative. They will not be solved by portal upgrades or communication campaigns. They require genuine policy intervention.

### DISCOM Interconnection Backlogs

This is the single biggest failure point in the programme, and it affects every homeowner who applies. The [DISCOM](/glossary/discom/) is responsible for three steps that sit between a completed installation and a working net metering connection: a technical inspection, a smart meter installation, and a synchronisation approval. In the best-performing states (Gujarat, Karnataka) this process takes 30–45 days. In states including parts of Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Rajasthan, and Madhya Pradesh, the same process takes 4–9 months.

The root cause is that DISCOMs were not resourced for the volume this scheme has created. The same engineers who handle industrial HT connection applications are processing residential solar net metering requests. Smart meters (mandatory for net metering under the scheme) are procured centrally and distributed unevenly. Some DISCOM substations have backlogs of several hundred pending meter installations with no clear schedule.

The consequence for the homeowner is brutal: the system is installed and sitting on the roof, generating power, but because net metering has not been approved, they cannot export surplus power to the grid. They either waste that generation or over-draw it by running unnecessary loads. The subsidy is also not disbursed until commissioning is formally completed, so the homeowner has paid the installation cost and is waiting (sometimes for the better part of a year) to receive money they were promised.

Our complete guide to [net metering in India](/blog/net-metering-india-complete-guide/) explains why the process works smoothly in some states and gets stuck in others.

### Vendor Empanelment Excluding Quality Smaller Installers

Empanelment criteria vary by DISCOM and, in several states, include minimum annual turnover requirements that automatically exclude smaller regional installers, even those with excellent installation track records and strong after-sales service capacity. The intent behind the turnover requirement is understandable: the government wants financially stable vendors. The practical effect is that homeowners in many districts are choosing from an empanelled list of three or four companies, none of which may be the most technically competent installer in that area.

The problem compounds when empanelled vendors win more work than they can handle. Their installation quality drops. Their after-sales response times lengthen. The homeowner has no alternative empanelled option, so they are stuck. This is a real pattern in the Tier-2 city market, and it undermines the quality-floor ambition of the scheme.

Our post on [empanelled vendor selection for PM Surya Ghar](/blog/empanelled-vendor-pm-surya-ghar/) details what homeowners should verify before signing with any empanelled installer.

### Subsidy Disbursement Delays Post-Installation

The MNRE portal targets 30-day disbursement from commissioning report submission to bank transfer. This target is met in Gujarat and Maharashtra. It is not consistently met elsewhere. The delay drivers are:

- **Smart meter shortage**: commissioning cannot be formally completed without a working bidirectional smart meter, and meter inventory is unevenly distributed.
- **PFMS gateway mismatches**: the Public Financial Management System that handles direct benefit transfer requires exact Aadhaar-bank account linkage; any discrepancy triggers a manual review that can take 30–60 additional days.
- **DISCOM inspection queuing**: the physical inspection that must precede commissioning report submission is scheduled by the DISCOM, not the homeowner; in backlogged states this queue itself takes weeks.

For a homeowner who has invested ₹1 Lakh or more in a system, a 4–6 month wait for a ₹78,000 subsidy is financially significant. Several homeowners we have spoken to have taken personal loans to bridge the gap, which adds interest cost that partially offsets the subsidy benefit.

### Apartment and Flat Owner Exclusion

This is perhaps the most important structural gap, given that approximately 30–35% of urban India lives in multi-storey apartment buildings. The current scheme has two routes for apartment residents: (1) individual installation on your own terrace portion with RWA NOC, which is only practically feasible for penthouse or top-floor residents; (2) RWA-level installation for common areas at ₹18,000 per kW up to 500 kW.

What does not exist is a virtual [net metering](/glossary/net-metering/) mechanism that would allow a single rooftop plant (installed by the RWA or a developer) to distribute energy credits across all flat owners' electricity bills. This is the mechanism that makes community solar viable in markets like the United States and several European countries. Without it, the 30–40 million households in urban apartment blocks are effectively excluded from PM Surya Ghar's core benefit, regardless of their willingness to pay.

The government is aware of this gap. Several state electricity regulatory commissions have published draft virtual net metering regulations. But the implementation has not kept pace with the demand. Our analysis of [gross metering versus net metering](/blog/gross-metering-vs-net-metering/) explains how metering policy directly affects whether apartment installations pencil out financially.

### Limited Rural DISCOM Bandwidth

For households in rural areas, the scheme works where modern DISCOM infrastructure exists, roughly 60% of rural India by 2026 estimates. In the remaining 40%, the absence of smart meter infrastructure, the limited technical capacity of rural DISCOM offices, and the unreliable grid voltage profiles that make grid-tie inverters trip frequently all combine to make the scheme functionally inaccessible. Farmers and agricultural communities are better served by the PM-KUSUM scheme, which has a different structure. According to [IRENA's India Renewable Energy Country Profile](https://www.irena.org/Countries/India), rural grid reliability and distribution infrastructure remain the primary bottleneck to residential solar adoption in semi-urban and rural zones. Rural residential households who genuinely want rooftop solar and are prepared to invest face barriers that the scheme does not address.

## The 5-Point PM Surya Ghar Reform Scorecard

This is our proprietary framework for evaluating what structural changes would have the highest impact on programme outcomes. We score each reform on a 0–10 scale across two dimensions: implementation feasibility and adoption impact. High-scoring reforms are the ones that should be prioritised.

### The 5-Point PM Surya Ghar Reform Scorecard

1. **Mandatory DISCOM SLA (60-day processing guarantee)**: MNRE should mandate a 60-day maximum from application receipt to net metering commissioning, with automatic subsidy enhancement of ₹5,000 for every additional month of DISCOM delay. This creates a financial incentive for DISCOMs to invest in processing capacity. Feasibility: 8/10. Adoption impact: 9/10.

2. **Community solar / virtual net metering provision**: Create a national framework for apartment-block installations where one rooftop plant credits multiple electricity accounts through a [virtual net metering](/glossary/virtual-net-metering/) mechanism administered by the DISCOM. Feasibility: 6/10 (requires SERC alignment in each state). Adoption impact: 10/10.

3. **Empanelment reform, capability-based criteria**: Replace minimum turnover thresholds with capability-based criteria: minimum installation count, after-sales SLA commitment, and ALMM-only equipment policy. Allow post-empanelment capacity audits rather than pre-entry financial screens. Feasibility: 8/10. Adoption impact: 7/10.

4. **AMC minimum standards for empanelled vendors**: Mandate a post-installation Annual Maintenance Contract commitment with a guaranteed 48-hour on-site response SLA. Today there is no post-installation service obligation. Systems that fail and sit unserviced for months damage the government's programme reputation. Feasibility: 9/10. Adoption impact: 6/10.

5. **PFMS pre-validation for Aadhaar-bank linkage**: Allow applicants to validate their Aadhaar-bank linkage at the registration stage, before installation, rather than discovering a mismatch during disbursement. This single UX change would eliminate approximately 20% of disbursement delays. Feasibility: 10/10. Adoption impact: 5/10.

Applied together, these five reforms would likely add 2–3 million installations per year to the current trajectory. The [IEA's Solar PV Policy and Deployment Report](https://www.iea.org/reports/solar-pv) notes that subsidy programme performance globally correlates most strongly with administrative processing speed, not subsidy quantum, which is precisely why DISCOM SLA reform would outperform any increase in the subsidy amount itself.

## How the Leading States Compare on Programme Delivery

Not all state-level implementations are equal. The contrast between the best and worst performers illuminates what is possible when state government is genuinely committed.

### Gujarat: The Benchmark

Gujarat's implementation is the clearest evidence that the structural problems are solvable. GUVNL has a dedicated renewable energy cell that handles only rooftop solar applications. Smart meters are procured and installed within 30 days of commissioning. Subsidy disbursement averages 45 days. Gujarat also has the highest per-capita PM Surya Ghar registrations of any large state. The enabling factors are: dedicated headcount, pre-provisioned meter inventory, and a single-window processing philosophy that the state adopted before the national scheme launched.

### Karnataka: The Digital-First Model

BESCOM's rooftop solar team is perhaps the most digitally capable DISCOM team in India. Applications are processed through a dedicated module that integrates with the national portal, inspector scheduling is done via a mobile app, and commissioning reports are digitally signed. Average processing time: 35–50 days. Karnataka's model is transferable but requires upfront investment in systems and training.

### Maharashtra: Scale with Inconsistency

MSEDCL handles a far larger application volume than either GUVNL or BESCOM. Its digitised inspection workflow works well in Pune and Mumbai. In the Vidarbha and Marathwada regions, where DISCOM infrastructure is older and installer density is lower, processing times stretch to 90–120 days. Maharashtra demonstrates that a large state with heterogeneous infrastructure cannot simply replicate the Gujarat model, it requires a differentiated approach for different regional contexts.

### States Needing Structural Support

Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, and Jharkhand consistently appear at the bottom of per-capita registration and commissioning completion rates. The common factors are: limited smart meter rollout, understaffed DISCOM solar cells, and a thinner empanelled vendor base. These states need targeted central support, dedicated technical assistance teams, centrally provisioned smart meter inventory, and perhaps a performance-linked funding mechanism that rewards state DISCOMs for processing speed.

## What the Scheme Means for Equipment Buyers Right Now

If you are a homeowner navigating PM Surya Ghar today, the following decisions will determine whether your experience is smooth or frustrating.

The most important check is not the subsidy amount, it is the DISCOM's average processing time in your circle. Before you sign any contract, call the nearest DISCOM office and ask how many pending net metering applications they have and what the current average completion time is. If the answer is more than 90 days, factor that into your financial plan and ask your installer what bridge arrangement they can offer during the wait period.

The second most important check is the ALMM status of every component your installer proposes. The [ALMM list is published by MNRE](https://mnre.gov.in/) and updated quarterly. A non-ALMM component is an automatic subsidy disqualifier regardless of the installer's claims. Our guide to [what solar inverter buyers need to know about PM Surya Ghar subsidy eligibility](/blog/solar-inverter-with-subsidy-pm-surya-ghar/) walks through the verification steps.

The third check is the vendor's post-installation commitment. Ask specifically: what is the response time if the inverter goes offline? Is there a written service agreement? What is the warranty escalation process? The [pm surya ghar eligibility requirements](/blog/pm-surya-ghar-eligibility/) cover the installation side, but post-installation service is entirely a function of the vendor relationship.

The honest summary: PM Surya Ghar is a good programme running at less than half its potential because of solvable infrastructure problems. The demand is there. The intent is right. The subsidy amounts are appropriate. What is missing is the last-mile infrastructure (DISCOM processing capacity, smart meter supply chains, and a regulatory framework that allows urban apartment buildings to participate) that would convert registered applications into commissioned and satisfied rooftop solar owners.

The government has signalled awareness of these gaps in recent MNRE consultations. The question is pace.

## Common Mistakes Homeowners Make When Applying

Knowing the programme's structural problems helps, but most homeowners who have a bad experience trace it to one of a small set of avoidable mistakes.

- **Choosing an empanelled vendor purely on price**: the empanelment list guarantees eligibility, not quality. The cheapest empanelled vendor in your area may have the longest after-sales queue. Call three or four references before deciding, or cross-check candidates against a local solar installer directory that lists more options than the DISCOM empanelment shortlist alone.
- **Not verifying ALMM status independently**: do not take the installer's word. Look up the inverter model on the live MNRE list yourself. [ALMM Phase III](/blog/almm-list-phase-iii-guide/) changed the eligible model list significantly; an older quotation may include a since-de-listed product.
- **Underestimating the net metering wait**: plan your finances assuming a 90-day wait from commissioning to subsidy disbursement, even in fast-processing states. If it comes sooner, the positive surprise is welcome. If it takes longer, you will not be caught short.
- **Ignoring the warranty length**: the [subsidy payback period calculation](/blog/pm-surya-ghar-subsidy-amount/) assumes the system generates reliably for at least 4–5 years. An inverter with a 5-year warranty that fails in year 4 can turn a positive ROI negative. The [solar inverter warranty](/blog/solar-inverter-warranty/) you accept at contract stage is the single biggest risk lever in the entire transaction.
- **Not filing a formal complaint when DISCOM delays occur**: most homeowners wait passively when their net metering application stalls. Filing a formal complaint with the state SERC or the MNRE grievance portal actually does move applications forward; DISCOMs are more responsive to formal complaints than to informal follow-up calls.
